\section{Archival OCR and VLM Evaluation}
\label{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}

Ingesting historical local archives into an automated legal decision-support system requires converting scanned physical documents into structured digital text. In the Davao City collection, enactments from earlier legislative terms mostly exist as scanned PDF files rather than searchable digital documents. These historical records contain common scanning noise, such as skewed pages, uneven ink, and staple marks that overlap official seals. If degraded scans are transcribed with erroneous characters or broken layouts, subsequent search indices and inference models inherit corrupted text. This section introduces an evaluation framework for legal document transcription, presents a stratified sample of archival records, compares four document extraction methods, and explains why a locally hosted vision-language model was selected. After verifying the extraction pipeline, the discussion examines the post-digitization linguistic characteristics of the transcribed local archive and evaluates structural token length compliance across the unified dual statutory search space.


\subsection{Candidate Document Extraction Systems}
To determine the best document extraction method for historical local archives, the proponents evaluated four distinct approaches. Traditional optical character recognition relies on binary image inputs and polygonal line tracking for character shape classification~\cite{smith2007overview}, with modern updates in Tesseract v5.x incorporating line-level LSTM recurrent networks. As seen in classical OCR workflows, traditional engines treat individual characters as isolated shapes without considering sentence meaning. While fast on central processing units, traditional engines struggle when encountering slanted lines, faded ink, or paper bleed-through.

Document-layout OCR frameworks separate page layout analysis from text line recognition~\cite{paruchuri2024surya}. Modern tools like Surya v0.6+ use object detection models to locate text columns and tabular headers before transcribing lines. A separate recognition model then reads the text, and reading order is reconstructed using sorting rules. This modular design preserves layout well on multi-column pages, but requires multiple neural networks that demand significant GPU memory during long batch runs.

Commercial cloud vision platforms run large multimodal transformer models on enterprise server clusters~\cite{google2024documentai}. Cloud services like Google Document AI achieve high accuracy across various scan qualities and complex tables. However, they require continuous internet access, charge ongoing per-page API fees, and require sending local legislative drafts to third-party servers. In a local government setting, recurring subscription costs and external data transfers conflict with operational independence and data privacy mandates.

Local vision-language models combine image encoding and text generation inside a single transformer architecture~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. Using two-dimensional rotary position embeddings (2D-RoPE) and flexible image patches, visual information is passed directly to a language decoder. With 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) compression~\cite{dettmers2024qlora}, models like Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct run completely offline on standard workstation hardware. The language model decoder helps resolve visual blur or scanner noise by using its understanding of grammar and statutory phrasing.


\subsection{Document Evaluation Framework}
Evaluating text extraction on local legislation reveals challenges that general OCR benchmarks often miss. As Lopresti showed~\cite{lopresti2008optical}, character errors in early steps cascade into downstream language processing tasks. In legal drafting, changing a single character or dropping a zero completely alters legal meaning. An effective evaluation framework for legislative text must therefore combine standard string edit distances with domain-specific legal entity accuracy and semantic embedding checks. The foundational character and word accuracy metrics are formulated via the Levenshtein edit distance $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}$~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}. To assess baseline typographical fidelity across candidate document parsers, Character Error Rate and Word Error Rate are computed as follows:

\noindent\textit{Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{CER} = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{c}_{\text{hyp}})}{|\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}}|} = \frac{S_c + D_c + I_c}{N_c}, \quad
    \text{WER} = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}(\mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{w}_{\text{hyp}})}{|\mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}}|} = \frac{S_w + D_w + I_w}{N_w}
    \label{eq:cer_wer}
    \eqcaption{Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER)}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $S$, $D$, and $I$ denote character or word substitutions, deletions, and insertions relative to the gold-standard reference sequence of length $N$.

Equation~\ref{eq:cer_wer} defines Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) using Levenshtein edit distance. These standard metrics establish a baseline of typing accuracy when transcribing historical paper ordinances. The formulas count character or word substitutions ($S$), deletions ($D$), and insertions ($I$) compared to the total length of the reference text ($N$). In legal documents, however, low overall error rates can hide serious mistakes, such as dropping a single digit in a penalty fine. While CER and WER measure general transcription noise, they treat all characters equally, which makes legal entity accuracy checks necessary.

Because local enactments vary widely in length from single-page resolutions to multi-page regulatory codes, raw edit counts alone cannot compare transcription quality across documents of different sizes. Normalizing the edit distance by sequence length places document similarity on a common scale regardless of whether a clause contains ten words or several hundred. Following the normalized edit distance formulation of Marzal and Vidal~\cite{marzal1993computation} and Levenshtein~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}, the proponents compute Normalized Edit Distance as a bounded similarity measure:

\noindent\textit{Normalized Edit Distance (NED):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{NED}(\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}}) = 1.0 - \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}(\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}})}{\max(|\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}|, |\mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}}|)}
    \label{eq:ned}
    \eqcaption{Normalized Edit Distance (NED)}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}$ and $\mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}}$ denote the reference and hypothesis string sequences, bounded between 0.0 (maximum dissimilarity) and 1.0 (exact match) to provide a length-invariant evaluation baseline.

Equation~\ref{eq:ned} defines Normalized Edit Distance (NED) as an intuitive similarity score between 0.0 and 1.0. This metric scales the raw edit distance against the maximum length of the two texts; the resulting value is subtracted from 1.0 so that an exact match yields 1.0. When evaluating multi-page ordinances, NED prevents long standard clauses from distorting overall error measurements. This score provides a clear summary of text alignment before checking specific legal entities.

Beyond general string alignment, legislative conflict detection requires exact-match fidelity across critical statutory tokens. A single altered digit in a penalty amount or a misread Republic Act number can break keyword retrieval or invert a legal rule even when the surrounding paragraph achieves high overall character accuracy. General edit-distance metrics assign uniform weight to ordinary words and legally decisive identifiers, so they must be paired with targeted slot checks. Adapting the exact-match slot filling evaluation standard from MUC-4~\cite{chinchor1992muc4}, the domain-specific component accuracy is computed as follows:

\noindent\textit{Domain-Specific Legislative Component Accuracy:}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Metric Accuracy} = \frac{1}{|K|} \sum_{k \in K} \mathbb{I}\left( \mathbf{v}_{\text{hyp}}^{(k)} \equiv \mathbf{v}_{\text{ref}}^{(k)} \right)
    \label{eq:legal_metric_acc}
    \eqcaption{Domain-Specific Legislative Component Accuracy}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $K$ represents the set of extracted legal entity instances, and $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is an exact-match indicator function comparing hypothesis values $\mathbf{v}_{\text{hyp}}^{(k)}$ against gold references $\mathbf{v}_{\text{ref}}^{(k)}$.

Equation~\ref{eq:legal_metric_acc} defines Domain-Specific Legislative Component Accuracy across key legal terms. This metric ensures that important statutory details (such as statutory citations and penalty amounts or effectivity dates) are transcribed exactly. The formula measures the share of legal terms that match the reference text word-for-word using the indicator function $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$. In local ordinances, a typo in a Republic Act number prevents BM25 keyword searches from finding the law and confuses dense vector search. Checking exact component accuracy confirms whether an extraction tool preserves the critical legal references needed for Stage~1 retrieval.

These metrics evaluate exact text matches across several key areas: Legal-Entity Accuracy (LEA) for government offices and private parties; Ordinance-Number Accuracy (ONA) for local ordinance numbers; Date Accuracy (DA) for approval and effectivity dates; Monetary-Value Accuracy (MVPA) for fines and fee limits; Section-Heading Integrity (SHI) for section titles; and Page Completeness (PC) for preserving all pages in order. Table quality is also evaluated using Tree-Edit-Distance-based Similarity (TEDS)~\cite{zhong2020image}; this metric evaluates row and column accuracy across tax and zoning schedules. Semantic Embedding Cosine Fidelity (SECF) checks semantic preservation by calculating the cosine similarity between embeddings of the reference and extracted texts. Together, these complementary metrics measure character fidelity alongside structural and semantic preservation across digitized ordinances.


\subsection{Limitations of Standard Spellchecking}
Standard spellcheckers and automatic dictionary tools cannot reliably check legal document transcription. In legal conflict detection, the impact of word errors is uneven. If an OCR engine writes ``Secton'' instead of ``Section'', the edit distance is only one character and an ordinary spellchecker easily fixes the error. On the other hand, if an engine reads a penalty of ``\textpeso 5,000.00'' as ``\textpeso 50,000.00'', or changes ``shall'' to ``may'', or alters ``Ordinance No. 0667-21'' to ``Ordinance No. 0661-21'', a standard spellchecker flags nothing because all the words are valid dictionary words. Under Philippine legal principles and Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160, mistakenly converting a \textpeso 5,000.00 penalty into \textpeso 50,000.00 transforms a valid local ordinance into an unlawful act.

Philippine local ordinances also contain specialized terms that generic spellcheckers frequently flag as errors. Ordinances regularly include legal Latin phrases (\textit{in pari materia}, \textit{ultra vires}), local agency acronyms (such as CTTMO and CENRO or CPDO), and local place names. Davao City alone contains 182 barangays, including Buhangin, Bunawan, Calinan, Marilog, Paquibato, Talomo, and Toril. Standard spellchecking tools flag these legitimate names as mistakes. If automatic autocorrection is turned on, generic software often replaces these unique local names with standard English words; this substitution corrupts the authentic statutory text.

The language model decoder provides an effective reading prior: when letters in standard legislative boilerplate are faded or smudged by scanner noise, the model's linguistic representations help reconstruct the missing characters accurately. However, because autoregressive text completion introduces a potential hallucination risk for sensitive numerical values, the system pairs VLM extraction with strict regex penalty validators and routes ambiguous monetary figures to human legal officers for manual confirmation.

Table~\ref{tab:historical_ocr_benchmark} compares word and character error rates across four authentic Davao City legislative records representing different eras and physical conditions. This comparison demonstrates how mechanical typewriter defects and physical paper aging affect automated transcription accuracy relative to clean modern scans. The empirical measurements reveal a sharp performance divergence between modern digital prints and mid-twentieth-century manual typewriter records. On modern digital scans (Ordinance No.~000667-21), the local vision-language model achieves near-perfect fidelity (0.00\% WER and 0.02\% CER). However, on the three older typewriter records (1956 to 1981), error rates rise substantially to 11.06\%--19.78\% WER and 5.91\%--12.87\% CER. This steep rise in error rates demonstrates that archival transcription difficulty is driven primarily by physical medium defects rather than model capacity limitations.

Detailed inspection of the historical transcripts reveals specific physical degradation mechanisms documented in document image defect literature~\cite{baird1993defect,lopresti2008optical}. In Ordinance No.~000044-56 (1956), mechanical strike velocity variance caused ink to fill closed-loop characters (\textit{e}, \textit{a}, \textit{o}), leading the local vision-language model to misread \textit{municipal waters} as \textit{municipal maters} and to transcribe \textit{log pond or for storage of logs} as \textit{lux powd or for straight of lots}. Furthermore, uneven carbon transfer caused the engine to misread the statutory license fee of \textit{\textpeso 200.00} as an American dollar figure (\textit{\$200.00}) and to alter \textit{Penal Provisions} into \textit{Final Provisions}. Large embossed city seal stamps and dark cursive ink signatures also obscured official attestation blocks; consequently, the model omitted the names of the acting mayor and city secretary.

Similar physical occlusions affected Ordinance No.~0166-81 (1981), where dark circular binder hole punches along the left margin severed section numbering and paragraph catchlines. The mechanical typewriter ribbon also caused heavy ink fill-in across title lines, causing the model to misread \textit{Lungsod ng Dabaw} as \textit{Luneskon ng Darw}, \textit{Series of 1981} as \textit{Series of 1961}, and \textit{liquified petroleum gas} as \textit{liquid profession (cas)}. In the approval block, overlapping cursive ink pen strokes across the city council seal caused the model to misinterpret \textit{Carried, by a majority} as \textit{Cashed: By a majority}, and to misread the Sangguniang Panlungsod secretary's name. In Ordinance No.~000332-10 (1968), horizontal typewriter underline strikes directly sliced through character descenders (\textit{p}, \textit{g}, \textit{y}, \textit{q}); this distortion fragmented word segmentation and obscured the legislative term.

These empirical findings highlight the principle of asymmetric legal error cost in local statutory auditing. Under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160 and the judicial doctrine of \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, local ordinance penalty provisions are legally invalid if they exceed statutory fine limits. If an optical character recognition engine guesses at smudged mechanical numbers and transcribes a \textpeso 200.00 fee as \textpeso 2,000.00 or drops a decimal zero, it artificially manufactures a statutory preemption defect where none exists in law. When visual glyphs fall below human legibility thresholds due to physical paper acidification, tropical moisture bleed, or hole-punch cuts, an automated system must avoid unconstrained hallucination. This necessitates a two-tier archival quarantine framework where severely degraded enactments are formally flagged for human verification; downstream semantic conflict detection then operates on legally reliable statutory text.


\subsection{Model Selection and Hardware Boundaries}
Selecting Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct as the primary document ingestion engine balances transcription accuracy with operating cost and data privacy. While traditional OCR runs quickly on standard CPUs, its difficulty in recognizing section headers and its errors on numbers hurt downstream search and conflict detection. Although commercial cloud platforms deliver strong accuracy, using commercial APIs across 1,816 multi-page ordinances (1,925 source PDFs) incurs recurring financial costs. Sending internal local government drafts and committee working texts to third-party cloud servers also raises compliance concerns under Republic Act No.~10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. A system designed for local government offices should operate independently without requiring ongoing subscription fees.

Running Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct in 4-bit NF4 precision delivers high transcription accuracy while operating completely offline. The model processes pages consistently while preserving data privacy within the 8~GB memory limit of Host~B. The main trade-off of the vision-language approach is processing speed. On the laptop GPU, each single page requires approximately 4.0 to 8.5 seconds to transcribe, compared to under one second for traditional OCR. However, because digitizing the historical archive is a one-time preparation step done in advance, this extra processing time is manageable. Limiting generation to 1,000 tokens per page and saving progress after each document ensures steady batch processing across the entire local collection.


\subsection{Document Extraction Limitations}
Testing archival transcription identified three practical limitations of the system. First, severe physical document damage sets an upper limit on how much text can be read accurately. On carbon-copy records from the 1950s and 1960s where ink has bled through from the other side of the page, even human readers cannot agree on blurred numbers; this difficulty confirms that errors stem from physical paper degradation rather than software flaws. Second, complex tables in older tax ordinances often have irregular layouts and merged cells. While the vision-language model accurately reads tax rates and numbers in Markdown tables, slight differences in column alignment reduce automated table scores (TEDS) even when the monetary numbers are completely correct. Third, dense embedding models can sometimes overlook small errors; a single wrong digit in a fine might produce only a tiny drop in similarity score, even though it creates a significant legal conflict. These edge cases show why the system is designed as an assistive tool to support legal researchers, rather than an automatic replacement for human review.

\section{Stage 1 Candidate Retrieval}
\label{sec:results_stage1_retrieval}

In a two-stage conflict detection system, the main goal of Stage~1 coarse retrieval is to quickly search the 27,248 unique enactments (177,423 unique provisions) and return a compact shortlist of top-$k$ candidate laws ($k \in \{5, 10, 20, 50\}$). In legal information retrieval, search performance is evaluated using ranked retrieval metrics rather than classification accuracy~\cite{manning2008introduction}, specifically candidate recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) and Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$). Candidate recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) measures the share of queries where the relevant national law appears within the top $k$ results, while Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$) measures how close to the top that law is placed ($1/\text{rank}$). If the correct national statute is missed in Stage~1, the Stage~2 inference model cannot detect the conflict, no matter how capable it is. At the same time, the shortlist must stay compact ($k \le 50$) so that comparing text pairs in Stage~2 runs quickly and fits within standard office computer memory.

\section{Stage 2 Conflict Detection}
\label{sec:results_stage2_training}

After Stage~1 narrows the candidate pool, Stage~2 evaluates pairs of legal provisions using Transformer Cross-Encoders. Standard language models process everyday prose well, yet struggle with formal statutory exceptions and preemption rules in local government law. As recent COLIEE evaluations report, unadapted models exhibit positive bias and miss negated conditions~\cite{alshehri2026cross}, often defaulting to neutral or entailment on conflicting statutory provisions. Fine-tuning updates cross-attention weights so the model learns to distinguish compliant local clauses from provisions that contravene or narrow a superior national statute.

The experiments in this section evaluate zero-shot screening, supervised fine-tuning, and threshold calibration on the 350-pair ground truth benchmark, comprising 198 unique instances over 22 authentic national statutes curated in Davao City drafting syntax with assistive phrasing from Gemini Flash 3.8 and formal verification by the thesis adviser. These evaluations establish baseline model behavior, determine optimal training hyperparameters, and calibrate classification thresholds prior to deployment. The calibrated pipeline provides the empirical foundation for the institutional evaluation procedure planned for the 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers. Subsequent subsections detail screening results across 28 scouted models, supervised fine-tuning outcomes, ablation comparisons, and training dynamics.

\subsection{Training Loss and Convergence}
In supervised learning, the loss function measures the difference between a model's predicted class probabilities and the actual ground truth labels. For three-class natural language inference, training optimizes standard Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Loss~\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. Evaluating this loss across the 245 training pairs guides parameter updates during back-propagation so that each architecture separates Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction relations. Well-separated probability outputs are also necessary before calibrating downstream decision thresholds for local ordinance screening.

\noindent\textit{Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Training Loss Function:}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} y_c \log \hat{P}(c)
    \label{eq:cross_entropy_loss}
    \eqcaption{Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Training Loss Function}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $y_c \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes the binary ground truth indicator for class $c \in \mathcal{C}$, and $\hat{P}(c)$ represents the calibrated softmax probability output by the model.

Equation~\ref{eq:cross_entropy_loss} defines the Multi-Class Cross-Entropy loss used for fine-tuning the Cross-Encoder models. This loss function penalizes mistakes between predicted probabilities and actual relationships (Entailment, Neutral, Contradiction). The formula calculates the negative log-likelihood of the correct class, giving larger penalties when the model makes an incorrect prediction with high confidence. During training on the 245 pairs, gradients from this loss update the model's weights so that the model learns to recognize legal conflicts. Minimizing this loss encourages the model to output clear, well-separated class probabilities needed for setting reliable confidence thresholds.

An epoch represents one complete pass through all 245 training pairs. Tracking both training loss and validation loss across epochs is the standard way to check model learning and spot the point between underfitting and overfitting. In healthy training, both training loss and validation loss drop together. When training loss keeps falling while validation loss levels off and starts rising, the model has finished learning general patterns and has begun memorizing the specific training examples.

The fine-tuning results show that the top five models (DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI, DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI, ModernBERT-large, unaligned deberta-v3-base, and PoL-BERT-Large), together with the standard bert-base-uncased baseline, reach their lowest validation loss and most stable scores at Epoch~3. These six models were selected as finalists for out-of-sample testing and deployment evaluation. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_loss_curves}, the validation loss and Macro $F_1$ trajectories trace convergence across all six finalists under their best configurations ($\eta = 2\times 10^{-5}, B = 8$). Comparing both curves side by side identifies the epoch where holdout generalization peaks before overfitting begins.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Regularization}
Overfitting is an important concern when fine-tuning large transformer models (spanning 86~million to 435~million parameters) on a small training dataset ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$). Without regularization, a neural network can memorize specific phrases instead of learning general legal conflict rules; this shortcut lowers training error but degrades performance on new local ordinances. The proponents applied AdamW weight decay ($\lambda = 0.01$)~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} and dropout ($p = 0.10$)~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} alongside early stopping~\cite{prechelt1998early} to curb overfitting. At the optimal Epoch~3 checkpoint, the gap between training accuracy (92.4\%) and validation accuracy (84.6\%) remained at 7.8\%, showing that the models learned generalizable preemption rules rather than superficial keyword shortcuts.

The candidate models were also sensitive to the initial learning rate. At a low learning rate of $\eta = 1\times 10^{-5}$, models underfitted, learning too slowly across the small dataset and leaving validation loss relatively high ($\ge 0.589$). Raising the learning rate to $2\times 10^{-5}$ or $3\times 10^{-5}$ provided the best balance, so the classification layers could adapt smoothly without disrupting the underlying language representations. In contrast, a higher rate of $\eta = 5\times 10^{-5}$ caused instability; large gradient updates over small batches led to loss fluctuations and premature overfitting, and validation Macro $F_1$ dropped by an average of 5.8\%.

\subsubsection{Premise Deduplication and Grouped Cross-Validation}
\label{sec:premise_grouped_cv}

\section{Algorithmic Scalability and Optimization}
\label{sec:results_scalability_optimization}

Extending the resource optimization analysis for Specific Objective~2, this section evaluates deployment viability under local government hardware constraints. Beyond classification accuracy, running an ex-ante legislative audit tool inside a local government office imposes strict compute and memory limits. Under Commission on Audit guidelines classifying tangible ICT items costing below \textpeso 50,000.00 as semi-expendable property~\cite{coa2022circular004} and public-sector ICT procurement bidding specifications calling for Intel Core i3 or i5 desktop processors with 8~GB of DDR4 memory~\cite{dict2020specs}, local legislative workstations typically operate on modest CPU-bound computers without discrete graphics accelerators. The following subsections examine scalability boundaries, a 30-run performance matrix, solution quality gaps, and architectural trade-offs.

\subsection{Solution Quality and Optimality Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:optimality_gap}

In algorithmic optimization, solution quality compares observed objective values against theoretical upper bounds or oracle baselines. In this cascaded architecture, solution quality spans Stage~1 candidate retrieval recall across the statutory knowledge base and Stage~2 classification performance under cost-sensitive threshold calibration. On the controlled benchmark candidate pool (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_retrieval_ablation}), standalone BM25 achieves 79.7\% Recall@5 and 99.7\% Recall@50, whereas the hybrid pipeline (Reciprocal Rank Fusion with $k=60$ combining BM25 and all-MiniLM-L6-v2) achieves 88.6\% Recall@5 and 100.0\% Recall@50. Under unconstrained open-corpus conditions across the 25,432 national statutes within the 27,248-enactment dual repository (164,620 national provisions out of 177,423 total), standalone BM25 Recall@50 is 43.14\% (Table~\ref{tab:full_corpus_retrieval_benchmark}), while unified dual-stream retrieval achieves 65.14\% Recall@50 with an MRR of 0.3462. Setting $k=50$ therefore provides an operational recall buffer while bounding inference latency on local CPU hardware.

In Stage~2 conflict inference, solution quality is governed by the decision threshold $\tau^*$. Standard softmax classification applies an uncalibrated default threshold of $\tau = 0.50$, implicitly assuming symmetric error costs. However, in local government law, failing to detect an illegal ordinance provision (a False Negative) carries an asymmetric cost, as enacting an unlawful measure exposes the local government unit to costly annulment litigation under \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}. Specifically, the optimal decision threshold $\tau^* = 0.45$ was identified on the dedicated development validation split ($N_{\text{val}} = 52$, where validation Contradiction $F_1 = 0.8947$ with zero false negatives) of the static 70/15/15 partition pursuant to Equation~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold}, and then evaluated out-of-sample on both the stratified held-out test split ($N_{\text{test}} = 53$) and across the 22 held-out statutory premise groups under zero-leakage cross-validation (\texttt{GroupKFold}). Table~\ref{tab:app_threshold_sensitivity_sweep} details the corresponding multi-threshold sensitivity sweep across the complete 350-instance ground truth benchmark in-sample operating profile ($N=350$); $\tau^* = 0.45$ establishes the primary operating point balancing precision and recall ($F_1 = 0.929$, 8 false negatives, 8 false positives; 95\% CI for premise-grouped CV accuracy: [80.9\%, 90.6\%]). Across the broader region $\tau \in [0.40, 0.45]$, the model maintains $F_1 \ge 0.917$ with at most 8 false negatives (7 false negatives at $\tau = 0.40$ and $\tau = 0.42$; 8 at $\tau = 0.45$); decision quality therefore remains stable across minor threshold shifts. Furthermore, adjusting to $\tau^* = 0.42$ prioritizes high-recall preemption screening ($F_2$ optimization); this tuning raises Conflict Recall on the held-out test set from 82.5\% to 92.4\% and closes the cost-weighted risk gap by 14.8\% relative to the uncalibrated baseline.


\subsection{Critical Analysis of Architectural and Resource Trade-Offs}
\label{sec:tradeoff_analysis}

A core design goal of this thesis is balancing solution quality against latency and hardware footprint across three architectural trade-offs. The first trade-off centers on model parameter scaling versus inference latency. Comparing DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI (435M parameters) against DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI (86M parameters) reveals diminishing marginal returns with scaling. While DeBERTa-v3-large achieves a slightly higher Conflict $F_1$-score on the validation set (0.9000 vs. 0.8947, a $+0.53\%$ difference), it requires nearly $3\times$ higher inference latency per pair on CPU hardware (348.5\,ms vs. 120.9\,ms) and consumes over $3.8\times$ more memory footprint. For an interactive local government decision-support system, conceding 0.53\% in validation score to gain a $3.3\times$ speedup and fit comfortably inside an 8~GB edge workstation is a practical engineering choice.

A second critical trade-off balances dense vector semantic accuracy against lexical exact-match retrieval. Standalone dense bi-encoders (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) capture paraphrased statutory concepts well and surpass BM25 by $+17.3\%$ on Tier~3 jurisprudential queries. However, pure dense retrieval occasionally misses exact numerical references, such as confusing Section~458 with Section~459. Combining BM25 and dense embeddings via Reciprocal Rank Fusion ($k=60$) incurs a negligible 0.07\,ms latency overhead while offsetting the blind spots of both individual retrieval methods.

Finally, the system balances offline institutional sovereignty against commercial cloud processing. As demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}, commercial cloud multimodal APIs (Google Gemini Flash 3.8) achieve 2.15\% Character Error Rate compared to 3.12\% for local 4-bit vision-language models (Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct). However, cloud APIs require ongoing per-page fees and mandate transmitting confidential legislative drafts across external internet servers. The local 4-bit VLM trades approximately 3 to 7 seconds of page processing time during the one-time archival ingestion phase to preserve 100\% offline data sovereignty, zero ongoing fees, and compliance with Republic Act No.~10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012).


\subsection{Base-Rate Prevalence Shift and Screening Dynamics}
\label{sec:prevalence_shift}

A fundamental methodological consideration when translating benchmark metrics to operational legislative review is the shift in class prevalence. On the curated ground truth benchmark, contradictory instances constitute 32.00\% of the 350 evaluation pairs ($112/350$, and 33.33\% across the 198 unique instances, $66/198$); this reflects a balanced experimental distribution designed for statistical power. In authentic legislative drafting, however, overt statutory preemption is a low-prevalence event that occurs in less than 1.0\% of submitted local ordinance provisions ($p_{\text{prevalence}} < 0.01$). Under Bayes' theorem, when screening for rare events, Positive Predictive Value (PPV) is suppressed by low base rates according to:
\begin{equation}
    \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{Sensitivity} \cdot p}{\text{Sensitivity} \cdot p + (1 - \text{Specificity}) \cdot (1 - p)}
    \label{eq:ppv_bayes}
    \eqcaption{Bayesian Positive Predictive Value Formulation}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $p$ represents true event prevalence. Even a detector with high sensitivity and specificity produces many false alarms when the underlying event is rare, because false positives accumulate across the 99\% compliant provisions. Evaluating expected PPV at $p = 0.01$ therefore clarifies how many flagged items a legislative researcher must inspect to locate one genuine preemption defect. Table~\ref{tab:bayesian_ppv_prevalence_scenarios} summarizes the projected Positive Predictive Value across three retrieval sensitivity and specificity regimes at this 1.0\% prevalence baseline.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering

Under controlled candidate retrieval (Regime~2), pairing the benchmark cross-encoder sensitivity of 92.86\% (104 of 112 contradictions recalled at $\tau^* = 0.45$, Table~\ref{tab:app_threshold_sensitivity_sweep}) with 86.67\% section specificity raises operational PPV to 6.57\% (1 real conflict per $\sim 15$ flags), and using the 100.0\% sensitivity (7 of 7 caught) observed on the Tier~2 oracle diagnostic raises projected PPV to 7.04\%. Methodologically, Regime~2 pairs benchmark cross-encoder sensitivity with pilot section specificity where boilerplate filtering assists non-conflict clearing, whereas Regime~3 derives from the full in-sample threshold sweep and is an indicative upper-bound projection rather than an out-of-sample guarantee.

In ex-ante local legal review, this precision-recall trade-off is an intentional and legally sound design choice. In local governance, the institutional cost of an unflagged statutory preemption (a False Negative) is severe, leading to local enactment of void legislation, costly taxpayer-funded annulment litigation under \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, and potential administrative sanctions under Republic Act No.~7160. Conversely, the institutional cost of an unneeded preemption alert (a False Positive) is manageable: a legislative researcher performs a rapid manual inspection of the side-by-side explainable AI card to verify compliance before plenary Second Reading, representing an estimated brief review overhead during routine committee intake (subject to empirical timing confirmation in post-study usability trials). To address this base-rate sensitivity dynamically, the web prototype incorporates a user-adjustable decision threshold slider ($\tau \in [0.20, 0.80]$). Legislative researchers conducting rapid routine First Reading intake can set $\tau^* \ge 0.60$ to suppress false alarms, while staff preparing critical regulatory measures for Second Reading floor interpellation can calibrate $\tau^* \le 0.45$ to enforce exhaustive preemption recall.

\section{System Integration and Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:results_summary_roadmap}

The empirical findings documented across this chapter validate the core computational thesis of this study. A cascaded architecture integrating dual-stream coarse retrieval with cost-sensitive cross-encoder inference provides an accurate and sovereign offline solution for ex-ante legal conflict detection. By decoupling candidate search from deep logical classification, the pipeline processes complex regulatory provisions within standard local government workstation limits. To synthesize full-system validation under Specific Objective~4 alongside operational deployment under Research Question~5 and Specific Objective~5, this section presents the decision-support prototype and the multi-tier end-to-end validation progression. The subsequent subsections evaluate the interactive prototype interface, full-document system stress testing, Statutory Knowledge Graph expansion, landmark Supreme Court preemption cases, and the institutional evaluation deployment roadmap for the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod.

In operationalizing the Side-by-Side Explainable AI (XAI) Inspector, the production interface bridges intrinsic transformer interpretability with human-centered legislative ergonomics. Rather than relying on rigid regex keyword patterns that fail when draft ordinances use novel phrasing or domain-specific synonyms, the prototype extracts token attribution directly from the cross-attention tensors of the fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder in real time ($<1.0$\,ms). For each flagged candidate pair, the system extracts the classification token's attention distribution from the final layer ($l=12$), min-max normalizes the saliency weights, and maps non-trivial weights ($\tilde{w} \ge 0.15$) onto a continuous red color gradient ($\alpha \in [0.08, 0.70]$) rendered directly over the verbatim text. Directly above the side-by-side text columns, each card features a calibrated Confidence Rate bar with a dynamic decision threshold marker ($\tau^*(u, v)$); rather than presenting a fixed cutoff line, the marker dynamically repositions along the track to $30.0\%$ for direct authority links ($d_G = 1$), $37.5\%$ for shared regulatory domain links ($d_G = 2$), or $45.0\%$ for unlinked provisions ($d_G \ge 4$), explicitly visualizing the topologically calibrated decision boundary in real time. To prevent visual fatigue and support varied review modalities, each comparison card incorporates an interactive toggle switch and a visual gradient legend bar ($0.0 \rightarrow 1.0$), allowing legislative researchers to switch instantly between color-coded contradiction heatmaps during rapid intake review and clean, unpolluted statutory text when preparing formal committee amendments.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:tier3_ablation_matrix}, input representation engineering and decision boundary calibration produce markedly different trade-offs in legal cross-encoders. Under Configuration~C, prepending serialized knowledge graph node attributes (\texttt{[JURISDICTION: NATIONAL | DOMAIN: ...]} and \texttt{[JURISDICTION: LOCAL | DOMAIN: ...]}) to input pairs elevated contradiction sensitivity from 40.0\% to 60.0\%, and the model detected the aerial pesticide preemption in \textit{Mosqueda v. PBGEA} ($P(\text{Contradiction})$ rose from 3.8\% to 74.7\%). However, because the cross-encoder was fine-tuned on authentic statutory text without prefix tags, the multi-head cross-attention mechanism attended strongly to the contrasting lexical tokens ``NATIONAL'' versus ``LOCAL'' as semantic opposition. This artificial polarity inflated contradiction probabilities across the board and triggered widespread false preemption alarms across three valid local police power enactments: \textit{Evasco v. Monta\~nez} (billboard zoning setbacks) jumped from 19.2\% to 85.4\% contradiction; \textit{City of Davao v. ARC Investors} (facially compliant business tax text mirroring the Local Government Code) jumped from 6.2\% to 56.8\%; and the \textit{Davao Anti-Smoking Code} (stricter smoke-free public spaces) jumped from 14.6\% to 96.8\%. Consequently, Configuration~C degraded police power specificity from 100.0\% to 40.0\%, which lowered precision and overall accuracy to 50.0\%. In local legislative drafting, flooding council staff with false alarms on 60\% of compliant measures impairs institutional utility, so the proponents rejected prompt-conditioned metadata injection.

In contrast, Topologically Calibrated Dynamic Thresholding (Configuration~B) preserves 100\% authentic verbatim statutory text and modulates the decision threshold via shortest-path graph distance ($\tau^*(u, v) = \tau_{\text{base}} - \Delta_{\tau} \cdot \gamma(u, v)$ pursuant to Equations~\ref{eq:topological_proximity} and~\ref{eq:topological_dynamic_threshold}). Configuration~B tightens the screening boundary to $\tau^* = 0.300$ for enactments sharing direct statutory citations, $\tau^* = 0.375$ for enactments sharing common macro-domains, and $\tau^* = 0.400$ for three-hop links. Both Configuration~A and Configuration~B achieve 70.0\% accuracy and a 0.5714 $F_1$-score while maintaining 100.0\% specificity and 100.0\% precision with zero false alarms, reliably clearing all five valid local enactments and detecting direct facial preemption conflicts (\textit{First Meridian} and \textit{Magtajas} at rounded 100.0\% contradiction probability). Configuration~D, by comparison, suffers the same specificity drop to 40.0\% and $F_1$ decline to 0.5455 as Configuration~C because metadata prompt conditioning already distorted cross-attention before dynamic thresholding was applied.

Analysis of the three undetected preemption controversies (\textit{Mosqueda v. PBGEA} at 3.8\% contradiction on Presidential Decree No.~1144, the \textit{Davao Mining Ban} at 0.1\% contradiction on Republic Act No.~7942, and \textit{City of Manila v. Laguio} at 11.8\% contradiction on constitutional due process) clarifies the structural boundary of sentence-level natural language inference. In \textit{Mosqueda}, Presidential Decree No.~1144 does not contain a literal antonym commanding that aerial spraying must be conducted; rather, Section~6 vests exclusive regulatory jurisdiction in the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority (FPA). The Supreme Court voided Ordinance No.~0309-07 under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine because an LGU cannot prohibit an activity whose regulation is vested exclusively in a national agency. Similarly, in the \textit{Davao Mining Ban}, Republic Act No.~7942 establishes national mineral ownership and concession agreements; the conflict is institutional rather than a surface syntactic clash. In \textit{Manila v. Laguio}, Manila Ordinance No.~7783 collided with the 1987 Constitution's Substantive Due Process Clause (Article~III, Section~1), requiring constitutional police power balancing (means-ends reasonableness) that exceeds sentence-level NLI cross-attention. These three cases indicate that jurisdictional delegation and constitutional police power balancing represent the natural frontier for subsequent neuro-symbolic research and motivate the technical recommendations in Chapter~5.

Across the seven correctly classified enactments in Table~\ref{tab:tier3_supreme_court_benchmark}, the fine-tuned cross-encoder exhibits distinct strengths when evaluating positive law. The system demonstrates high semantic sensitivity on direct facial preemption controversies where local provisions impose procedural conditions or prohibitions that contradict affirmative statutory standards. In litigation arising under the Davao City Revenue Code (\textit{City of Davao v. First Meridian Development, Inc.}~\cite{scp2022firstmeridian} and \textit{Rock Steel Resources, Inc. v. City of Davao}~\cite{ctarocksteel139}), Section~423 of Davao City Ordinance No.~158-05 required taxpayers to pay disputed local taxes under protest prior to instituting an administrative challenge or refund action. In contrast, Section~195 of Republic Act No.~7160 explicitly establishes an administrative protest remedy without imposing prior payment under protest for local taxes; on this ground, the Court of Tax Appeals in \textit{Rock Steel} (\texttt{CTA AC No.~139}) invalidated Section~423's pre-payment condition as ultra vires. The fine-tuned cross-encoder flagged this clash at $P(\text{Contradiction}) = 99.96\%$ (rounded to 100.0\% in Table~\ref{tab:tier3_supreme_court_benchmark}) because the prohibitive condition precedent directly contradicts the national statutory procedure. Similarly, in \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corp.}, Cagayan de Oro City Ordinance Nos.~3375-93 and~3353 prohibited casino gambling and criminalized the operation of gaming establishments within city territory. Presidential Decree No.~1869, however, explicitly franchised and authorized the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR) to operate gambling casinos nationwide. The cross-encoder assigned $P(\text{Contradiction}) = 99.97\%$ to this pair and reproduced the core holding of \textit{Magtajas} that local councils cannot prohibit what Congress has expressly authorized.

Equally important for local legislative screening is the pipeline's ability to clear compliant local police power enactments without generating false alarms. In \textit{Hon. Leoncio Evasco, Jr. v. Alex P. Monta\~nez}, Davao City Ordinance No.~092-2000 established zoning setbacks and building permit requirements for billboards and signboards, which the respondent challenged as ultra vires under the National Building Code (Presidential Decree No.~1096). The Supreme Court upheld the ordinance as a valid exercise of local police power under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160. The model evaluated the pair at $P(\text{Contradiction}) = 19.2\%$ and categorized the relationship as compliant below the dynamic threshold ($\tau^* = 0.375$).

In \textit{City of Davao and Tanjili v. ARC Investors, Inc.}, the text of Section~69(f) of Ordinance No.~0158-05 facially mirrored the local tax categories authorized under Section~143(f) of Republic Act No.~7160. Although the Supreme Court ultimately struck down the tax assessment against a passive holding company, the judicial invalidation was strictly an as-applied ruling against the City Treasurer's administrative execution rather than an invalidation of the written statutory text. The cross-encoder assigned $P(\text{Contradiction}) = 6.2\%$ because the text of the ordinance remained facially consistent with national law. This outcome shows that pure textual inference evaluates written ordinance text without inventing legal invalidity when the underlying judicial dispute arises from executive misapplication. Furthermore, the model cleared the withdrawal of tax exemptions in \textit{City of Davao v. Court of Appeals and GSIS} ($P = 0.1\%$) and the two local control enactments for smoke-free public spaces ($P = 14.6\%$) and localized traffic speed limits ($P = 4.2\%$); these cleared controls show that stricter local standards enacted under the General Welfare Clause do not trigger false preemption alerts in isolated pair checks.


\subsection{Open-Corpus Jurisprudential Screening}

Comparing the judicial reasoning in authentic court decisions against Table~\ref{tab:tier3_open_corpus_conflicts} clarifies both what the system captures accurately and where sentence-level neural inference falls short. In revenue and gaming disputes involving direct procedural or prohibitory opposition, the cross-encoder aligns closely with appellate precedent. No.~240078}, affirming \texttt{CTA EB No.~1607})~\cite{scp2022firstmeridian} resolved passive holding company refunds after protest payment under Sections~131(e) and~143(f). In controlled pair evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:tier3_supreme_court_benchmark}), the Stage~2 cross-encoder flags Section~423 against Republic Act No.~7160 Section~195 at 100.0\% contradiction probability and isolates the opposing operators ``pays'' and ``without.''

When the complete 129-page Revenue Code runs unassisted across all 164,620 national statutory provisions (Appendix~\ref{app:tier3_full_results}), Stage~1 retrieval on Section~423 surfaces older protest-tax statutes and historical city charters instead. Specifically, the cross-encoder flags Republic Act No.~3811 Section~59 at 91.7\%, Republic Act No.~537 Sections~76 and~69 at 90.9\% and 49.6\%, and Presidential Decree No.~464 Section~62 at 50.1\%, with attention centered on ``No'' and ``pays'' against ``not'' and ``consider.'' No.~249668})~\cite{scp2022davaovarc}, both controlled scoring (6.2\%) and the full 423-section sweep return zero conflict flags on Section~69(f) because the written clause tracks Section~143(f) of Republic Act No.~7160; the Supreme Court struck down the Treasurer's as-applied reclassification of a passive holding company rather than holding the ordinance text facially void. In \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corp.} (\texttt{G.R. No.~111097})~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, full-PDF screening assigns 95.5\% to 100.0\% probabilities across all three prohibitory provisions (Sections~1, 4, and~5) against national gaming decrees (Presidential Decree No.~1399 Section~4 and Presidential Decree No.~956 Sections~2 and~8) alongside anti-money-laundering casino definitions (Republic Act No.~10927 Section~3); attention focuses on ``prohibited'' and ``banning'' against ``shall'' and ``authorized.''

In the Davao Mining Ban (\texttt{DAVAO-06}, Ordinance No.~0325-15), evaluating the enactment in Table~\ref{tab:tier3_supreme_court_benchmark} against general mineral ownership provisions (Republic Act No.~7942 Sections~4 and~27) produced a 0.1\% false negative because neither passage expressed a direct syntactic prohibition. When the complete document passes through open-corpus screening, Stage~1 retrieval surfaces Republic Act No.~7942 Section~76 (right of entry onto private lands) and Section~43 (quarry permit application rights) at direct citation proximity ($d_G = 1$, $\tau^* = 0.300$); the cross-encoder flags both collisions on Section~5 at 98.4\% and 98.1\% by pairing ``no approval shall be granted'' against ``shall not be prevented'' and ``may apply.'' A parallel recovery occurs in \textit{City of Manila v. Laguio, Jr.} (\texttt{G.R. No.~118127})~\cite{scp2005manilavlaguio}: whereas isolated Constitutional Due Process evaluation (Article~III, Section~1) stayed at 11.8\% because means-ends proportionality exceeds sentence-level NLI, full-PDF screening on Section~1 retrieves Presidential Decree No.~499 Section~1 (which specifically zoned Ermita-Malate as a commercial area) at 61.1\% ($d_G = 1$, $\tau^* = 0.300$), alongside 100.0\% flags on Section~2 against Republic Act No.~8975 Section~7 and Batas Pambansa Blg.~880 Section~6 where a city ban on permit issuance collides with statutory mayoral duties to issue licenses. Nevertheless, isolated Presidential Decree No.~1144 Section~6 delegation in \textit{Mosqueda} (3.8\%) indicates that agency-exclusivity and equal-protection inquiries remain beyond local cross-attention when explicit prohibitive and permissive verbs are absent.

Across multi-section enactments, unassisted screening exposes both latent statutory collisions and structural noise sources that inflate total System Conflict Flag counts. In \textit{Mosqueda v. PBGEA} (\texttt{G.R. Nos.~189185 \& 189305})~\cite{scp2016mosqueda}, the Supreme Court decided the dispute within the parties' pleadings on equal protection, the unfeasibility of a three-month transition period, Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority jurisdiction under Presidential Decree No.~1144, and substantive due process overbreadth. Unassisted screening over 164,620 national statutory provisions does not replicate those unpleaded constitutional doctrines; instead, Stage~1 matches ``Davao'' and ``aerial spraying'' to Republic Act No.~7296 (Sections~3, 4, 6, and~7) and Republic Act No.~8700 (Sections~3 and~7) and flags Sections~2 and~5 across ten cards at 99.8\% to 100.0\% contradiction while clearing Section~7 under the local penalty cap. Because Congress expressly directed that private franchisee to ``maintain air spraying services within Davao,'' exhaustive open-corpus retrieval surfaces a plausible positive-law entanglement omitted during retrospective judicial digests. By contrast, auditing larger local codes identifies four drivers of spurious or advisory flags: historical city charter leakage (\texttt{DAVAO-02} Sections~36, 38, and~40 retrieving repealed city signboard text from pre-1970 charters such as Republic Act Nos.~170, 409, and~3857, which expands \textit{Evasco} to 66 flags across 22 sections), raw PDF character glyph artifacts in scanned ordinances, cross-domain criminal penalty comparisons (\texttt{DAVAO-06} Section~7 and \texttt{SC-PHIL-09} Section~6 matching unrelated statutory fine tiers), and statutory exception or stricter-standard sensitivity (\texttt{DAVAO-05} Section~8 vs. Republic Act No.~7160 Section~234 at 93.6\% on ``exempted'' versus ``withdrawn,'' \texttt{DAVAO-07} Section~4 vs. Executive Order No.~106 Section~1 at 93.0\%, and \texttt{DAVAO-08} Sections~4, 7, 11, and~12 vs. Republic Act No.~4136 Sections~35 and~36 at 88.1\% to 99.2\%).

From a computer science standpoint, these results mark a clear boundary between computational conflict screening and authoritative legal adjudication. The dual-stage system does not declare an ordinance unconstitutional, nor can cross-attention saliency determine whether stricter local rules remain valid under the General Welfare Clause (Republic Act No.~7160 Section~16) or delegated traffic orders (JAO~2018-01). In \textit{Evasco} (\texttt{DAVAO-02}), \textit{GSIS} (\texttt{DAVAO-05}), and the Davao Anti-Smoking and Speed Limit ordinances (\texttt{DAVAO-07} and \texttt{DAVAO-08}), elevated neural contradiction probabilities frequently reflect genuine textual friction (stricter local bans and mirrored exemption clauses alongside overlapping national standards like Republic Act No.~9211 Section~17 or Executive Order No.~165) that calls for substantive legal judgment rather than automatic rejection. Equally, historical charter matches explain why human legislative researchers cannot treat raw flag counts as verified legal defects. By presenting each candidate conflict as an inspectable evidence card with verbatim statutory text and graph distance alongside attention highlights, the prototype operates strictly as a rapid screening aid for Sangguniang Panlungsod legal staff prior to committee deliberation and plenary passage.


\subsection{Institutional Evaluation Plan and Roadmap}
\label{sec:human_evaluation_protocol}

Following administrative routing through the Human Resource Management Office (HRMO) and City Administrator's Office under the Sangguniang Panlungsod referral, the evaluation guidebooks and review instruments were formally transmitted for administrative review and deployment to the Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers. The evaluation design partitions the 350 multi-panel instances across five balanced panels (Panel~A through Panel~E, 70 pairs each) evaluated across Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction for triple-redundant validation, with participant identities anonymized under Republic Act No.~10173. While administrative follow-ups were maintained, active legislative commitments and committee review obligations prevented concluding external returns within the study's data collection window. Rather than delaying benchmarking, all experimental evaluations were anchored upon the adviser-reviewed ground truth benchmark (350 pairs, 198 unique instances across 22 authentic national statutory sections from 17 Republic Acts) for supervised training and threshold calibration, as well as comparative testing in Section~\ref{sec:results_stage2_training}.

The complete 15-evaluator consensus procedure (spanning Fleiss' Generalized Kappa ($\kappa$)~\cite{fleiss1971measuring,mchugh2012interrater}, Krippendorff's Alpha ($\alpha$), and the 14-item SUS/TAM psychometric survey~\cite{brooke1996sus,davis1989perceived}) is positioned as an institutional post-deployment roadmap. Participating Sangguniang Panlungsod staff remain eligible for official Civil Service 201 Certificates of Contribution once returns are completed. Across the ground-truth benchmark and the Tier~2 and Tier~3 full-document suites, the Coarse-to-Fine system demonstrates effective ex-ante decision support for assisted legislative screening in the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod once city clearances conclude. Chapter~5 summarizes these findings and outlines technical extensions for future work.
