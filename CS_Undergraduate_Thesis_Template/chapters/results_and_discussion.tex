\section{Archival OCR and VLM Evaluation}
\label{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}

Ingesting historical local archives into an automated legal decision-support system requires converting scanned physical documents into structured digital text. In the Davao City collection, enactments from earlier legislative terms mostly exist as scanned PDF files rather than searchable digital documents. These historical records contain common scanning noise, such as crooked pages, uneven ink, staple marks, and official city seal stamps over text. If degraded scans are transcribed with erroneous characters or broken layouts, subsequent search indices and inference models inherit corrupted text. This section introduces an evaluation framework for legal document transcription, presents a stratified sample of archival records, compares four document extraction methods, and explains why a locally hosted vision-language model was selected. Following empirical verification of the extraction pipeline, this section concludes by evaluating the post-digitization linguistic characteristics of the transcribed local archive and examining structural token length compliance across the unified dual statutory search space.


\subsection{Candidate Document Extraction Systems}
To determine the best document extraction method for historical local archives, the proponents evaluated four distinct approaches. Traditional optical character recognition relies on image binarization, polygonal line tracking, and segmented character shape classification~\cite{smith2007overview}, with modern updates in Tesseract v5.x incorporating line-level LSTM recurrent networks. As seen in classical OCR workflows, traditional engines treat individual characters as isolated shapes without considering sentence meaning. While fast on central processing units, traditional engines struggle when encountering slanted lines, faded ink, or paper bleed-through.

Document-layout OCR frameworks separate page layout analysis from text line recognition~\cite{paruchuri2024surya}. Modern tools like Surya v0.6+ use object detection models to locate text columns, tables, and headers before transcribing lines. A separate recognition model then reads the text, and reading order is reconstructed using sorting rules. This modular design preserves layout well on multi-column pages, but requires multiple neural networks that demand significant GPU memory during long batch runs.

Commercial cloud vision platforms run large multimodal transformer models on enterprise server clusters~\cite{google2024documentai}. Cloud services like Google Document AI achieve high accuracy across various scan qualities and complex tables. However, they require continuous internet access, charge ongoing per-page API fees, and require sending local legislative drafts to third-party servers. In a local government setting, recurring subscription costs and external data transfers conflict with operational independence and data privacy mandates.

Local vision-language models combine image encoding and text generation inside a single transformer architecture~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. Using two-dimensional rotary position embeddings (2D-RoPE) and flexible image patches, visual information is passed directly to a language decoder. With 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) compression~\cite{dettmers2024qlora}, models like Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct run completely offline on standard workstation hardware. The language model decoder helps resolve visual blur or scanner noise by using its understanding of grammar and statutory phrasing.


\subsection{Document Evaluation Framework}
Evaluating text extraction on local legislation reveals challenges that general OCR benchmarks often miss. As Lopresti showed~\cite{lopresti2008optical}, character errors in early steps cascade into downstream language processing tasks. In legal drafting, changing a single character or dropping a zero completely changes legal meaning. An effective evaluation framework for legislative text must therefore combine standard string edit distances with domain-specific legal entity accuracy and semantic embedding checks.

The foundational character and word accuracy metrics are formulated via the Levenshtein edit distance $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}$~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}. To assess baseline typographical fidelity across candidate document parsers, Character Error Rate and Word Error Rate are computed as follows:

\noindent\textit{Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{CER} = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{c}_{\text{hyp}})}{|\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}}|} = \frac{S_c + D_c + I_c}{N_c}, \quad
    \text{WER} = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}(\mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{w}_{\text{hyp}})}{|\mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}}|} = \frac{S_w + D_w + I_w}{N_w}
    \label{eq:cer_wer}
    \eqcaption{Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER)}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $S$, $D$, and $I$ denote character or word substitutions, deletions, and insertions relative to the gold-standard reference sequence of length $N$.

Equation~\ref{eq:cer_wer} defines Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) using Levenshtein edit distance. These standard metrics establish a baseline of typing accuracy when transcribing historical paper ordinances. The formulas count character or word substitutions ($S$), deletions ($D$), and insertions ($I$) compared to the total length of the reference text ($N$). In legal documents, however, low overall error rates can hide serious mistakes, such as dropping a single digit in a penalty fine. While CER and WER measure general transcription noise, they treat all characters equally, which makes legal entity accuracy checks necessary.

To evaluate overall document similarity on a bounded scale regardless of clause length, following the normalized edit distance formulation of Marzal and Vidal~\cite{marzal1993computation} and Levenshtein~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}, the proponents compute Normalized Edit Distance:

\noindent\textit{Normalized Edit Distance (NED):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{NED}(\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}}) = 1.0 - \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lev}}(\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}})}{\max(|\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}|, |\mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}}|)}
    \label{eq:ned}
    \eqcaption{Normalized Edit Distance (NED)}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $\mathbf{s}_{\text{ref}}$ and $\mathbf{s}_{\text{hyp}}$ denote the reference and hypothesis string sequences, bounded between 0.0 (maximum dissimilarity) and 1.0 (exact match) to provide a length-invariant evaluation baseline.

Equation~\ref{eq:ned} defines Normalized Edit Distance (NED) as an intuitive similarity score between 0.0 and 1.0. This metric scales the raw edit distance against the maximum length of the two texts, subtracting the result from 1.0 so that an exact match scores 1.0. When evaluating multi-page ordinances, NED prevents long standard clauses from distorting overall error measurements. This score provides a clear summary of text alignment before checking specific legal entities.

Beyond general string alignment, legislative conflict detection requires exact-match fidelity across critical statutory tokens. Adapting the exact-match slot filling evaluation standard from MUC-4~\cite{chinchor1992muc4}, the domain-specific component accuracy is computed as follows:

\noindent\textit{Domain-Specific Legislative Component Accuracy:}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Metric Accuracy} = \frac{1}{|K|} \sum_{k \in K} \mathbb{I}\left( \mathbf{v}_{\text{hyp}}^{(k)} \equiv \mathbf{v}_{\text{ref}}^{(k)} \right)
    \label{eq:legal_metric_acc}
    \eqcaption{Domain-Specific Legislative Component Accuracy}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $K$ represents the set of extracted legal entity instances, and $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is an exact-match indicator function comparing hypothesis values $\mathbf{v}_{\text{hyp}}^{(k)}$ against gold references $\mathbf{v}_{\text{ref}}^{(k)}$.

Equation~\ref{eq:legal_metric_acc} defines Domain-Specific Legislative Component Accuracy across key legal terms. This metric ensures that important statutory details---such as law citations, ordinance numbers, penalty amounts, and effectivity dates---are transcribed exactly. The formula measures the share of legal terms that match the reference text word-for-word using the indicator function $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$. In local ordinances, a typo in a Republic Act number prevents BM25 keyword searches from finding the law and confuses dense vector search. Checking exact component accuracy confirms whether an extraction tool preserves the critical legal references needed for Stage~1 retrieval.

These metrics evaluate exact text matches across several key areas: Legal-Entity Accuracy (LEA) for government offices and private parties; Ordinance-Number Accuracy (ONA) for local ordinance numbers; Date Accuracy (DA) for approval and effectivity dates; Monetary-Value Accuracy (MVPA) for fines and fee limits; Section-Heading Integrity (SHI) for section titles; and Page Completeness (PC) for preserving all pages in order. In addition, table quality is evaluated using Tree-Edit-Distance-based Similarity (TEDS)~\cite{zhong2020image}, measuring row and column accuracy across tax and zoning schedules. Finally, Semantic Embedding Cosine Fidelity (SECF) checks semantic preservation by calculating the cosine similarity between embeddings of the reference and extracted texts.


\subsection{Limitations of Standard Spellchecking}
Standard spellcheckers and automatic dictionary tools cannot reliably check legal document transcription. In legal conflict detection, the impact of word errors is uneven. If an OCR engine writes ``Secton'' instead of ``Section'', the edit distance is only one character and an ordinary spellchecker easily fixes the error. On the other hand, if an engine reads a penalty of ``\textpeso 5,000.00'' as ``\textpeso 50,000.00'', or changes ``shall'' to ``may'', or alters ``Ordinance No. 0667-21'' to ``Ordinance No. 0661-21'', a standard spellchecker flags nothing because all the words are valid dictionary words. Under Philippine legal principles and Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160, turning a \textpeso 5,000.00 penalty into \textpeso 50,000.00 turns a valid local ordinance into an unlawful act.

In addition, Philippine local ordinances contain specialized terms that generic spellcheckers frequently flag as errors. Ordinances regularly include legal Latin phrases (\textit{in pari materia}, \textit{ultra vires}), local agency acronyms (such as CTTMO, CENRO, and CPDO), and local place names. Davao City alone contains 182 barangays, such as Buhangin, Bunawan, Calinan, Marilog, Paquibato, Talomo, and Toril. Standard spellchecking tools flag these legitimate names as mistakes. If automatic autocorrection is turned on, generic software often replaces these unique local names with standard English words, corrupting the legal text.

However, because autoregressive text completion introduces a potential hallucination risk for sensitive numerical values, the system pairs VLM extraction with strict regex penalty validators and routes ambiguous monetary figures to human legal officers for manual confirmation.

Table~\ref{tab:historical_ocr_benchmark} compares word and character error rates across four authentic Davao City legislative records representing different eras and physical conditions. This comparison demonstrates how mechanical typewriter defects and physical paper aging affect automated transcription accuracy relative to clean modern scans. The empirical measurements reveal a sharp performance divergence between modern digital prints and mid-twentieth-century manual typewriter records. On modern digital scans (Ordinance No.~000667-21), the local vision-language model achieves near-perfect fidelity (0.00\% WER and 0.02\% CER). However, on historical typewriter records from 1956, 1968, and 1981, error rates rise substantially to 11.06\%--19.78\% WER and 5.91\%--12.87\% CER. This steep rise in error rates demonstrates that archival transcription difficulty is driven primarily by physical medium defects rather than model capacity limitations.

Detailed inspection of the historical transcripts reveals specific physical degradation mechanisms documented in document image defect literature~\cite{baird1993defect,lopresti2008optical}. In Ordinance No.~000044-56 (1956), mechanical strike velocity variance caused ink to fill closed-loop characters such as \textit{e}, \textit{a}, and \textit{o}, leading the local vision-language model to misread \textit{municipal waters} as \textit{municipal maters} and to transcribe \textit{log pond or for storage of logs} as \textit{lux powd or for straight of lots}. Furthermore, uneven carbon transfer caused the engine to misread the statutory license fee of \textit{\textpeso 200.00} as an American dollar figure (\textit{\$200.00}) and to alter \textit{Penal Provisions} into \textit{Final Provisions}. Large embossed city seal stamps and dark cursive ink signatures also obscured official attestation blocks, causing the model to omit the names of the acting mayor and city secretary.

Similar physical occlusions affected Ordinance No.~0166-81 (1981), where dark circular binder hole punches along the left margin severed section numbering and paragraph catchlines. The mechanical typewriter ribbon also caused heavy ink fill-in across title lines, causing the model to misread \textit{Lungsod ng Dabaw} as \textit{Luneskon ng Darw}, \textit{Series of 1981} as \textit{Series of 1961}, and \textit{liquified petroleum gas} as \textit{liquid profession (cas)}. In the approval block, overlapping cursive ink pen strokes across the city council seal caused the model to misinterpret \textit{Carried, by a majority} as \textit{Cashed: By a majority}, and to misread the Sangguniang Panlungsod secretary's name. In Ordinance No.~000332-10 (1968), horizontal typewriter underscore strikes directly sliced through character descenders (\textit{p}, \textit{g}, \textit{y}, \textit{q}), fragmenting word segmentation and misreading the legislative term.

These empirical findings highlight the principle of asymmetric legal error cost in local statutory auditing. Under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160 and the judicial doctrine of \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, local ordinance penalty provisions are legally invalid if they exceed statutory fine limits. If an optical character recognition engine guesses at smudged mechanical numbers and transcribes a \textpeso 200.00 fee as \textpeso 2,000.00 or drops a decimal zero, it artificially manufactures a statutory preemption defect where none exists in law. When visual glyphs fall below human legibility thresholds due to physical paper acidification, tropical moisture bleed, or hole-punch cuts, an automated system must avoid unconstrained hallucination. This necessitates a two-tier archival quarantine framework where severely degraded enactments are formally flagged for human verification, ensuring that downstream semantic conflict detection operates exclusively upon legally reliable statutory text.


\subsection{Model Selection and Hardware Boundaries}
Selecting Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct as the primary document ingestion engine balances text accuracy, operating cost, and data privacy. While traditional OCR runs quickly on standard CPUs, its difficulty in recognizing section headers and its errors on numbers hurt downstream search and conflict detection. Although commercial cloud platforms deliver strong accuracy, using commercial APIs across 1,925 multi-page ordinances incurs recurring financial costs. In addition, sending internal local government drafts and committee working texts to third-party cloud servers raises compliance concerns under Republic Act No.~10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. A system designed for local government offices should operate independently without requiring ongoing subscription fees.

Running Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct in 4-bit NF4 precision delivers high transcription accuracy while operating completely offline. The model processes pages consistently, preserves data privacy, and stays within the 8~GB memory limit of Host~B. The main trade-off of the vision-language approach is processing speed. On the laptop GPU, transcribing a single page takes about 4.0 to 8.5 seconds, compared to under one second for traditional OCR. However, because digitizing the historical archive is a one-time preparation step done in advance, this extra processing time is manageable. Limiting generation to 1,000 tokens per page and saving progress after each document ensures steady batch processing across the entire local collection.


\subsection{Document Extraction Limitations}
Testing archival transcription identified three practical limitations of the system. First, severe physical document damage sets an upper limit on how much text can be read accurately. On carbon-copy records from the 1950s and 1960s where ink has bled through from the other side of the page, even human readers cannot agree on blurred numbers, showing that the issue stems from damaged paper rather than software flaws.

Second, complex tables in older tax ordinances often have irregular layouts and merged cells. While the vision-language model accurately reads tax rates and numbers in Markdown tables, slight differences in column alignment reduce automated table scores (TEDS) even when the monetary numbers are completely correct. Third, dense embedding models can sometimes overlook small errors; a single wrong digit in a fine might produce only a tiny drop in similarity score, even though it creates a significant legal conflict. These edge cases show why the system is designed as an assistive tool to support legal researchers, rather than an automatic replacement for human review.


\subsection{Local Statutory Corpus Analysis}
\label{sec:results_local_corpus_analysis}
Having selected and executed Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct across all 1,925 archival PDF documents as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:vlm_digitization_pipeline}, the local ordinance collection was unlocked as structured, machine-readable text and standardized into JSON records. With the full text available, the proponents conducted the empirical analysis of local legislative structures, functional archetypes, and vocabulary representations. Across all 1,925 local enactments, ordinances exhibit a median length of 5,827 characters and 914 words, with an interquartile range of 756 to 1,898 words. \section{Stage 1 Candidate Retrieval}
\label{sec:results_stage1_retrieval}

In a two-stage conflict detection system, the main goal of Stage~1 coarse retrieval is to quickly search the 27,357 enactments (178,494 provisions) and return a compact shortlist of top-$k$ candidate laws ($k \in \{5, 10, 20, 50\}$). In legal information retrieval, search performance is evaluated primarily using candidate recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) and Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$), rather than classification accuracy~\cite{manning2008introduction}. Candidate recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) measures the share of queries where the relevant national law appears within the top $k$ results, while Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$) measures how close to the top that law is placed ($1/\text{rank}$). If the correct national statute is missed in Stage~1, the Stage~2 inference model cannot detect the conflict, no matter how capable it is. At the same time, the shortlist must stay compact ($k \le 50$) so that comparing text pairs in Stage~2 runs quickly and fits within standard office computer memory.

\section{Stage 2 Conflict Detection}
\label{sec:results_stage2_training}

After Stage~1 narrows down candidate statutes, Stage~2 evaluates pairs of legal provisions using Transformer Cross-Encoders. Standard language models handle everyday text well, but they are not tuned for the formal structure, exceptions, and preemption rules found in local government law. As recent COLIEE studies show, unadapted models often show affirmative bias---the tendency to assume two texts agree unless they contain obvious negative words---frequently defaulting to neutral or entailment on complex statutory text~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. Fine-tuning updates the model's cross-attention layers, training it to recognize when a local clause conflicts with, broadens, or contradicts a superior national law.

The experiments in this section represent model exploration, screening, and hyperparameter tuning on the 350-pair ground truth benchmark (comprising 198 unique instances over 22 authentic national statutes, curated following Davao City drafting syntax with assistive phrasing from \texttt{Gemini Flash 3.8} and formally verified by the thesis adviser). These evaluations establish baseline model properties, determine optimal training hyperparameters, and calibrate decision thresholds, while providing an empirical foundation for the institutional evaluation protocol planned for the 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers. This section presents screening results across 28 scouted models, supervised fine-tuning comparisons, ablation findings, and model training dynamics.

\subsection{Training Loss and Convergence}
In supervised learning, the loss function measures the difference between the model's predicted probabilities and the actual ground truth labels. For three-class natural language inference, training optimizes standard Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Loss~\cite{goodfellow2016deep}:

\noindent\textit{Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Training Loss Function:}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} y_c \log \hat{P}(c)
    \label{eq:cross_entropy_loss}
    \eqcaption{Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Training Loss Function}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $y_c \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes the binary ground truth indicator for class $c \in \mathcal{C}$, and $\hat{P}(c)$ represents the calibrated softmax probability output by the model.

Equation~\ref{eq:cross_entropy_loss} defines the Multi-Class Cross-Entropy loss used for fine-tuning the Cross-Encoder models. This loss function penalizes mistakes between predicted probabilities and actual relationships (Entailment, Neutral, Contradiction). The formula calculates the negative log-likelihood of the correct class, giving larger penalties when the model makes an incorrect prediction with high confidence. During training on the 245 pairs, gradients from this loss update the model's weights, teaching it to recognize legal conflicts. Minimizing this loss encourages the model to output clear, well-separated class probabilities needed for setting reliable confidence thresholds.

An epoch represents one complete pass through all 245 training pairs. Tracking both training loss and validation loss across epochs is the standard way to check model learning and spot the point between underfitting and overfitting. In healthy training, both training loss and validation loss drop together. When training loss keeps falling while validation loss levels off and starts rising, the model has finished learning general patterns and has begun memorizing the specific training examples.

The fine-tuning results show that the top five models (DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI, DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI, ModernBERT-large, cold deberta-v3-base, and PoL-BERT-Large), together with the standard bert-base-uncased baseline, reach their lowest validation loss and most stable scores at Epoch~3. These six models were selected as finalists for out-of-sample testing and deployment evaluation. To compare training stability across this group, Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_loss_curves} shows the validation loss and Macro $F_1$ curves across all six finalists under their best configurations ($\eta = 2\times 10^{-5}, B = 8$).

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Regularization}
Overfitting is an important concern when fine-tuning large transformer models (ranging from 86~million to 435~million parameters) on a small training dataset ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$). Without regularization, a neural network can easily memorize specific phrases instead of learning general legal conflict rules, leading to low training error but poor performance on new local ordinances. Using three standard regularization methods---AdamW weight decay ($\lambda = 0.01$)~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}, dropout ($p = 0.10$)~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}, and early stopping~\cite{prechelt1998early}---kept the models from overfitting. At the optimal Epoch~3 checkpoint, the gap between training accuracy (92.4\%) and validation accuracy (84.6\%) remained at 7.8\%, showing that the models learned generalizable preemption rules rather than superficial keyword shortcuts.

The candidate models were also sensitive to the initial learning rate. At a low learning rate of $\eta = 1\times 10^{-5}$, models underfitted, learning too slowly across the small dataset and leaving validation loss relatively high ($\ge 0.589$). Raising the learning rate to $2\times 10^{-5}$ or $3\times 10^{-5}$ provided the best balance, allowing the classification layers to adapt smoothly without disrupting the underlying language representations. In contrast, a higher rate of $\eta = 5\times 10^{-5}$ caused instability; large gradient updates over small batches led to loss fluctuations, premature overfitting, and an average 5.8\% drop in validation Macro $F_1$.

\subsubsection{Statutory Premise Deduplication and Grouped Generalization Analysis}
\label{sec:premise_grouped_cv}

To address potential data-leakage vulnerabilities arising from multi-hypothesis generation over statutory premises, the proponents conducted a granular deduplication audit and strict premise-grouped cross-validation. The 350-pair ground truth benchmark reflects the operational workload distributed across the five Sangguniang Panlungsod legal research panels ($5\text{ panels} \times 70\text{ pairs} = 350\text{ pairs}$), where 152 pairs deliberately overlap across panels to measure multi-rater consensus and inter-annotator reliability (Fleiss' $\kappa$). Structurally, these 350 pairs comprise exactly 198 unique (premise, hypothesis) propositional instances constructed across 22 authentic national statutory acts, with exactly 9 balanced hypothesis variants per statute (3 Contradictions, 3 Entailments, and 3 Neutrals evenly distributed across difficulty tiers, totaling exactly 66 instances per class across the unique set).

To evaluate whether the neural Cross-Encoder relies on memorizing specific statutory phrasing or truly generalizes to unseen legislative acts, the proponents executed a strict Premise-Grouped 5-Fold Cross-Validation (\texttt{GroupKFold}) over the 350-pair benchmark (evaluated across its 198 unique instances partitioned by statutory act). In this evaluation regime, all nine ordinance hypotheses associated with a given national statute are strictly quarantined into either the training partition or the evaluation partition, ensuring zero statutory premise overlap across folds. Under this zero-leakage premise-grouped regime, \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI} maintains a mean accuracy of $86.44\% \pm 6.05\%$, a Macro $F_1$-score of $0.8617 \pm 0.0631$, and a Conflict $F_1$-score of $0.8945 \pm 0.0683$ across the 22 held-out statutory premise groups. \section{Algorithmic Scalability and Optimization}
\label{sec:results_scalability_optimization}

In addition to classification accuracy, the deployment of an ex-ante legislative audit system within a municipal government office imposes strict computational constraints. Pursuant to Department of Information and Communications Technology (DICT) administrative standards~\cite{dict2020specs} and Commission on Audit (COA) Circular No.~2022-004~\cite{coa2022circular004}, local government workstations typically operate on 4-to-6-core commodity CPUs with 8~GB of system RAM without access to dedicated graphics processing units. \subsection{Solution Quality and Optimality Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:optimality_gap}

In algorithmic optimization, solution quality is measured by comparing observed objective values against theoretical upper bounds or oracle baselines. In our cascaded architecture, solution quality encompasses two stages: candidate retrieval recall in Stage~1 relative to the complete statutory knowledge base, and classification loss minimization in Stage~2 under cost-sensitive threshold calibration.

In Stage~1 coarse retrieval, the objective is to maximize Candidate Recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) to avoid dropping controlling positive law before cross-attention inference. On the controlled benchmark candidate pool (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_retrieval_ablation}), standalone BM25 achieves 79.7\% Recall@5 and 99.7\% Recall@50, whereas the proposed hybrid pipeline (Reciprocal Rank Fusion with $k=60$ combining BM25 and \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) achieves 88.6\% Recall@5 and 100.0\% Recall@50. When evaluated under unconstrained open-corpus conditions across all 27,357 enactments (178,494 provisions), standalone BM25 Recall@50 is 43.14\% (Table~\ref{tab:full_corpus_retrieval_benchmark}), while unified dual-stream retrieval achieves 65.14\% Recall@50 with an MRR of 0.3462. Setting $k=50$ provides an essential operational buffer, capturing the controlling statutes across all controlled test cases while bounding inference latency on municipal CPU hardware.

In Stage~2 conflict inference, solution quality is governed by the decision threshold $\tau^*$. Standard softmax classification applies an uncalibrated default threshold of $\tau = 0.50$, implicitly assuming symmetric error costs. However, in local government law, failing to detect an illegal ordinance provision (a False Negative) carries an asymmetric cost, as enacting an unlawful measure exposes the local government unit to costly annulment litigation under \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}. Specifically, the optimal decision threshold $\tau^* = 0.45$ was identified empirically on the dedicated development validation split ($N_{\text{val}} = 52$, where validation Contradiction $F_1 = 0.8947$ with zero false negatives) of the static 70/15/15 partition pursuant to Equation~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold}, and then evaluated out-of-sample on both the stratified held-out test split ($N_{\text{test}} = 53$) and across the 22 held-out statutory premise groups under zero-leakage cross-validation (\texttt{GroupKFold}). Table~\ref{tab:app_threshold_sensitivity_sweep} details the corresponding multi-threshold sensitivity sweep across the complete 350-instance ground truth benchmark in-sample operating profile ($N=350$), confirming that $\tau^* = 0.45$ establishes the primary operational operating point balancing precision and recall ($F_1 = 0.929$, 8 false negatives, 8 false positives; 95\% CI for premise-grouped CV accuracy: [80.9\%, 90.6\%]). Across the broader operating region $\tau \in [0.40, 0.45]$, the model maintains $F_1 \ge 0.917$ with at most 8 false negatives (7 false negatives at $\tau = 0.40$ and $\tau = 0.42$; 8 at $\tau = 0.45$), demonstrating that decision quality is robust to minor threshold fluctuations. Furthermore, adjusting to $\tau^* = 0.42$ prioritizes high-recall preemption screening ($F_2$ optimization), raising Conflict Recall on the held-out test set from 82.5\% to 92.4\% and closing the cost-weighted risk gap by 14.8\% relative to the uncalibrated baseline.


\subsection{Critical Analysis of Architectural and Resource Trade-Offs}
\label{sec:tradeoff_analysis}

A core contribution of this thesis is the rigorous empirical balancing of solution quality against latency and hardware footprint. The system design is guided by three central architectural trade-offs:

The primary architectural trade-off centers on model parameter scaling versus inference latency. Comparing \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI} (435M parameters) against \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI} (86M parameters) reveals diminishing marginal returns with scaling. While \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large} achieves a slightly higher Conflict $F_1$-score on the validation set (0.9000 vs. 0.8947, a $+0.53\%$ difference), it requires nearly $3\times$ higher inference latency per pair on CPU hardware (348.5\,ms vs. 120.9\,ms) and consumes over $3.8\times$ more memory footprint. For an interactive municipal decision-support system, sacrificing 0.53\% in theoretical score to gain a $3.3\times$ speedup and fit comfortably inside an 8~GB edge workstation represents a principled engineering choice.

A second critical trade-off balances dense vector semantic accuracy against lexical exact-match retrieval. Standalone dense bi-encoders (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) excel at capturing paraphrased statutory concepts, outperforming BM25 by $+17.3\%$ on Tier~3 jurisprudential queries. However, pure dense retrieval occasionally misses exact numerical references, such as confusing Section~458 with Section~459. Combining BM25 and dense embeddings via Reciprocal Rank Fusion ($k=60$) incurs a negligible 0.07\,ms latency overhead while eliminating the blind spots of both individual paradigms.

Finally, the system balances offline institutional sovereignty against commercial cloud processing. As demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}, commercial cloud multimodal APIs (Google Gemini Flash 3.8) achieve 2.15\% Character Error Rate compared to 3.12\% for local 4-bit vision-language models (\texttt{Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct}). However, cloud APIs require ongoing per-page fees and mandate transmitting confidential legislative drafts across external internet servers. The local 4-bit VLM trades approximately 3 to 7 seconds of page processing time during the one-time archival ingestion phase to guarantee 100\% offline data sovereignty, zero ongoing operational costs, and absolute compliance with Republic Act No.~10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012).


\subsection{Base-Rate Prevalence Shift and Screening Dynamics}
\label{sec:prevalence_shift}

A fundamental methodological consideration when translating benchmark metrics to operational legislative review is the profound shift in class prevalence. On the curated ground truth benchmark, contradictory instances constitute exactly 32.00\% of the 350 evaluation pairs ($112/350$, and 33.33\% across the 198 unique instances, $66/198$), reflecting a balanced experimental distribution designed for statistical power. In authentic legislative drafting, however, overt statutory preemption is an extreme low-prevalence event, occurring in less than 1.0\% of submitted municipal ordinance provisions ($p_{\text{prevalence}} < 0.01$). Under Bayes' theorem, when screening for rare events, Positive Predictive Value (PPV) is heavily suppressed by low base rates according to:
\begin{equation}
    \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{Sensitivity} \cdot p}{\text{Sensitivity} \cdot p + (1 - \text{Specificity}) \cdot (1 - p)}
    \label{eq:ppv_bayes}
    \eqcaption{Bayesian Positive Predictive Value Formulation}
\end{equation}
\noindent Where $p$ represents true event prevalence. Under unassisted open-corpus retrieval (Regime~1), where Stage~1 candidate discovery surfaces 2 of 7 planted conflicts (28.57\% sensitivity; 95\% CI: [8.22\%, 64.11\%]) alongside 86.67\% section specificity (39 of 45 compliant sections cleared; 95\% CI: [73.84\%, 93.74\%]), operational PPV under an assumed 1.0\% preemption base rate ($p = 0.01$) is 2.12\% (with combined extreme Wilson interval spanning 0.32\% to 9.38\%), confirming that unassisted alerts function as a wide-net triage funnel where flags require rapid human inspection. Under controlled candidate retrieval (Regime~2), where candidate premises are pre-identified, applying the benchmark cross-encoder sensitivity of 92.86\% (104 of 112 contradictions recalled at $\tau^* = 0.45$, Table~\ref{tab:app_threshold_sensitivity_sweep}) alongside the 86.67\% section specificity yields an operational PPV of 6.57\% (1 real conflict per $\sim 15$ flags); if evaluated using the 100.0\% sensitivity (7 of 7 caught) observed on the Tier~2 oracle diagnostic, projected PPV reaches 7.04\%. Finally, under high-specificity operational mode (Regime~3, $\tau^* = 0.60$), where specificity reaches 98.32\% and sensitivity is 80.40\% (Table~\ref{tab:app_threshold_sensitivity_sweep}), PPV rises to 32.58\% (1 real conflict per $\sim 3$ flags). Methodologically, Regime~2 pairs benchmark cross-encoder sensitivity with pilot section specificity where legislative boilerplate filtering assists non-conflict clearing, while Regime~3 derives from the full in-sample threshold calibration sweep (Table~\ref{tab:app_threshold_sensitivity_sweep}), serving as an indicative upper-bound operational projection rather than an out-of-sample guarantee.

In ex-ante municipal legal review, this precision-recall trade-off is an intentional and legally sound design choice. In local governance, the institutional cost of an unflagged statutory preemption (a False Negative) is severe, leading to municipal enactment of void legislation, costly taxpayer-funded annulment litigation under \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, and potential administrative sanctions under Republic Act No.~7160. Conversely, the institutional cost of an unneeded preemption alert (a False Positive) is manageable: a legislative researcher performs a rapid manual inspection of the side-by-side explainable AI card to verify compliance before plenary Second Reading, representing an estimated brief review overhead during routine committee intake (subject to empirical timing confirmation in post-study usability trials). To address this base-rate sensitivity dynamically, the web prototype incorporates a user-adjustable decision threshold slider ($\tau \in [0.20, 0.80]$). Legislative researchers conducting rapid routine First Reading intake can set $\tau^* \ge 0.60$ to suppress false alarms, while staff preparing critical regulatory measures for Second Reading floor interpellation can calibrate $\tau^* \le 0.45$ to enforce exhaustive preemption recall.

\section[System Integration and Human Evaluation Roadmap]{System Integration and Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:results_summary_roadmap}

The empirical findings documented across this chapter validate the core computational thesis of this study. A cascaded architecture integrating dual-stream coarse retrieval with cost-sensitive cross-encoder inference provides an accurate and sovereign offline solution for ex-ante legal conflict detection. By decoupling candidate search from deep logical classification, the pipeline processes complex regulatory provisions within standard local government workstation limits. To bridge the gap between algorithmic validation and institutional adoption within the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod, this section presents the system integration architecture and the multi-tier validation progression. The subsequent subsections evaluate the interactive decision-support prototype interface, full-document system stress testing, landmark Supreme Court preemption cases, and the institutional evaluation deployment roadmap.

\label{sec:prototype_interface_design}

\subsection{Institutional Evaluation Protocol and Roadmap}
\label{sec:human_evaluation_protocol}

Following administrative routing through the Human Resource Management Office (HRMO) and the City Administrator's Office pursuant to the Sangguniang Panlungsod referral, the digital evaluation instruments, annotator guidebooks, and review forms were formally transmitted for administrative review and deployment to the cohort of Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers. The planned study protocol partitions the 350 multi-panel instances across five balanced panels (Panel~A through Panel~E), each containing 70 legal provision pairs evaluated across Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction to enable triple-redundant validation. In this protocol, evaluators are equipped with a standardized Legal Annotation Guidebook defining operational triggers for statutory preemption under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine. To maintain strict data privacy compliance under Republic Act No.~10173, participant identities are decoupled from submitted ratings and replaced with anonymized evaluator tokens. This structured framework ensures that external human evaluations adhere to ethical research protocols while capturing authentic practitioner feedback.

To capture authentic practitioner feedback, the proponents maintained an open submission period and conducted regular administrative follow-ups with the legislative staff. However, owing to the researchers' active legislative commitments, ongoing committee review obligations, and the project's overall academic submission timeline, external survey and annotation returns could not be concluded prior to the academic defense cutoff. Rather than delaying model benchmarking or presenting statistically fragile partial subsets, the proponents, in consultation with their thesis adviser, anchored all experimental evaluations upon the authoritative Faculty Adviser-Verified Ground Truth Benchmark (350 pairs, comprising 198 unique instances across 22 authentic national statutory premises) for all supervised training, threshold calibration, cross-validation, and comparative model testing presented in Section~\ref{sec:results_stage2_training}. This decision preserved experimental rigor while preventing incomplete external data from compromising machine learning evaluations. As demonstrated in prior sections, the resulting benchmark provides robust diagnostic visibility across all eight Macro Legal Domains.

The complete 15-evaluator multi-annotator consensus protocol---including Fleiss' Generalized Kappa ($\kappa$)~\cite{fleiss1971measuring,mchugh2012interrater} for nominal categories, Krippendorff's Alpha ($\alpha$) for ordinal confidence weighting, and the 14-item psychometric survey combining Brooke's System Usability Scale (SUS)~\cite{brooke1996sus} and Davis' Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)~\cite{davis1989perceived}---is therefore positioned as an institutional post-deployment evaluation roadmap. In recognition of their ongoing professional advisory assistance, participating legal staff remain eligible for the official Certificate of Contribution structured for their Civil Service 201 records upon concluding full-scale operational rollout. Furthermore, the combination of the faculty adviser-adjudicated ground truth benchmark, the Tier~2 full-document pilot stress test, and the Tier~3 jurisprudential preemption suite demonstrate that the Coarse-to-Fine Semantic Conflict Detection System functions as an effective ex-ante decision-support tool. These empirical evaluations confirm that the proposed architecture establishes a viable decision-support foundation for assisted legislative screening within the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod once municipal clearance procedures are completed. Subsequent chapters summarize the key contributions of this work and establish concrete technical extensions for continuing research in local legislative informatics.
