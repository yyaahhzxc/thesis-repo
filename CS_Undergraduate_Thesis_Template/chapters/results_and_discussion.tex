\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results_and_discussion}

This chapter presents the empirical findings and experimental evaluations of the Coarse-to-Fine Semantic Conflict Detection System. It begins by examining the performance and trade-offs of archival optical character recognition (OCR) and vision-language transcription architectures across the digitized Davao City municipal collection ($N = 1,664$ enactments). Next, it details the performance of the Stage~1 coarse statutory retrieval pipeline across the unified dual statutory search space ($N = 27,096$ enactments, comprising 176,421 provisions across national statutes and Davao City local ordinances) and the 350-pair curated benchmark. Furthermore, it presents the initial machine learning training runs, hyperparameter grid search results, and loss dynamics for the Stage~2 Cross-Encoder models, analyzing model convergence, underfitting, overfitting, and hyperparameter sensitivity on the curated statutory benchmark dataset.


\section{Archival OCR and Vision-Language Transcription Evaluation}
\label{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}

Ingesting historical municipal archives into an automated legal decision-support system requires converting scanned physical documents into clean, structured digital text. In the Davao City collection, enactments from earlier legislative terms exist predominantly as scanned physical PDF records rather than searchable digital-born documents. These historical records exhibit real scanning noise, including paper skew, uneven ink contrast, staple shadows, and official municipal seal watermarks stamped over operative clauses. If degraded physical scans are transcribed with erroneous characters or broken layout geometry, downstream retrieval indices and inference models inherit corrupted tokens. This section evaluates four candidate optical character recognition paradigms, analyzes the impact of character corruption on statutory text, and presents the empirical justification for adopting a locally hosted vision-language model.


\subsection{Architectural Paradigms and Candidate Systems}
To determine the most viable document extraction method for historical municipal archives, the proponents evaluated four distinct architectural paradigms:
\begin{description}
    \item[Traditional Pipeline OCR (Tesseract v5.x).] Traditional optical character recognition relies on classical image binarization, connected component analysis, and heuristic line slicing, followed by character sequence prediction using a lightweight 1D CNN-LSTM architecture~\cite{smith2007overview}. While computationally efficient on central processing units, traditional engines evaluate characters as isolated visual glyphs without semantic sentence context.
    
    \item[Document-Layout OCR (Surya v0.6+).] Modern layout-aware systems decouple spatial document segmentation from line text recognition~\cite{paruchuri2024surya}. A visual object detection backbone identifies bounding polygons for text columns, tables, and headers, after which a separate recognition model transcribes text lines and reconstructs human reading order through topological sorting heuristics.
    
    \item[Proprietary Cloud Vision APIs (Google Cloud Vision).] Commercial cloud endpoints deploy large-scale proprietary multimodal transformer models hosted on distributed server clusters~\cite{google2024documentai}. These services achieve high character recognition accuracy across diverse scan qualities, but require active internet connectivity, ongoing per-page API fees, and external transmission of local government documents.
    
    \item[Local Vision-Language Model (Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct).] Multimodal foundation models unify visual patch encoding and autoregressive text generation within an end-to-end transformer architecture~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. Using two-dimensional rotary position embeddings (2D-RoPE) and dynamic patch allocation, the visual features are projected directly into a causal language model decoder. Under 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) quantization~\cite{dettmers2024qlora}, the model executes completely offline on consumer workstation hardware.
\end{description}


\subsection{The Error Cascade in Legal Informatics}
Evaluating OCR architectures on municipal legislation exposes fundamental vulnerabilities that do not appear in general-domain document processing. As Lopresti demonstrated~\cite{lopresti2008optical}, optical character recognition errors trigger an ``error cascade'' across subsequent natural language processing layers. In narrative literature, minor typographical errors such as character substitutions do not impair human comprehension. In statutory construction, however, a single mutated character corrupts governing legal semantics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Token Shattering in Subword Vocabularies:} Modern transformer tokenizers (e.g., Byte-Pair Encoding and WordPiece) rely on pre-compiled subword vocabularies. When a clean statutory catchline such as \texttt{Republic Act No. 7160} is misread as \texttt{Republic Act No. 7l60} (substituting the numeral one with a lowercase letter), the tokenizer shatters the single entity token into multiple obscure subword pieces, degrading dense vector similarity in Stage~1 retrieval.
    
    \item \textbf{Lexical Disconnect in Inverted Indices:} Lexical search engines depend on exact string representations. A typographical substitution in an ordinance identifier, such as transcribing \texttt{Ordinance No. 0667-21} as \texttt{000667\_21}, prevents BM25 inverted indices from matching the query against historical records.
    
    \item \textbf{Substantive Statutory Distortion:} In penal and regulatory clauses, misreading numerical characters reverses legislative intent. Substituting a zero or misplacing punctuation in a monetary penalty (e.g., transcribing \texttt{P79,920.00} as \texttt{P79 920 00} or misidentifying currency symbols) compromises downstream compliance auditing against statutory ceilings.
\end{itemize}

To quantify the downstream consequences of OCR noise, the proponents conducted a controlled validation run on authentic archival municipal legislation (exemplified by Davao City Ordinance No.~000667-21). Traditional scanner OCR produced a Word Error Rate (WER) of 3.11\% and a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.45\%. While these global error rates appear small on a character level, the traditional baseline achieved only 54.55\% Legal Marker Precision. The engine corrupted the explicit citation to Republic Act No.~7160, failed to isolate Section~1 and Section~2 headers, and dropped formatting in the monetary penalty clause. 

In contrast, the locally hosted vision-language model achieved 0.00\% Word Error Rate, 0.02\% Character Error Rate, and 100.0\% Legal Marker Precision across all eleven target statutory indicators. Furthermore, vectorizing the extracted text via TF-IDF n-gram embeddings confirmed that the VLM transcription achieved 100.0\% Semantic Cosine Fidelity relative to verified reference text, compared to 99.21\% for traditional OCR. The underlying language model decoder functions as an intrinsic linguistic prior: when visual glyphs are partially obscured by faint ink or scanner noise, the model's internal language representations successfully reconstruct canonical statutory phrases.


\subsection{Defensible Selection Rationale and Implementation Trade-Offs}
Selecting Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct as the primary archival ingestion engine resolves the technical trade-offs between extraction accuracy, operational expense, and statutory data sovereignty:
\begin{description}
    \item[Rejection of Traditional OCR.] While Tesseract provides rapid CPU throughput, its inability to maintain section header geometry and its susceptibility to numeral substitution directly undermine Stage~1 lexical retrieval and Section-Level Specificity.
    
    \item[Rejection of Commercial Cloud APIs.] Although Google Cloud Vision provides strong extraction accuracy, deploying commercial APIs across 1,664 multi-page ordinances entails recurring financial expenses. Furthermore, transmitting unpromulgated local government drafts and internal committee working texts to external commercial cloud servers introduces legal compliance hurdles under Republic Act No.~10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. An edge-deployable local system simulating Local Government Unit IT constraints must remain independent of external subscription paywalls.
    
    \item[Adoption of Local 4-Bit Vision-Language Inference.] Deploying Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct in 4-bit NF4 precision delivers enterprise-grade transcription accuracy while running entirely offline. The model processes pages deterministically, respects local data sovereignty, and operates within the 8~GB VRAM boundary of the Neural Training Workstation.
\end{description}

The primary operational trade-off of the vision-language approach is computational latency. On the NVIDIA RTX 5050 Laptop GPU, transcribing a single scanned page requires approximately 4.0 to 8.5 seconds, compared to less than one second for traditional OCR. However, because archival corpus digitization is an offline, one-time preprocessing task, this latency expenditure is technically acceptable. Bounding page generation at 1,000 new tokens and isolating document execution within fault-tolerant resume checkpoints ensures dependable batch processing across the complete municipal collection.


\section{Stage 1 Results}
\label{sec:results_stage1_retrieval}

In a two-stage legal conflict detection pipeline, the primary objective of Stage~1 coarse retrieval is to rapidly filter an extensive statutory collection of 27,096 enactments (176,421 searchable provisions), capturing all potentially preemptive provisions within a compact top-$k$ candidate shortlist ($k \in \{5, 10, 20, 50\}$). In automated legal informatics, coarse retrieval performance is judged primarily on candidate recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) and Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$), rather than classification accuracy~\cite{manning2008introduction}. Candidate recall ($\text{Recall@}k$) quantifies the proportion of queries where the governing statutory provision is successfully retrieved within the top $k$ candidates, while Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR}$) measures the reciprocal position of the first relevant statute ($1/\text{rank}$), rewarding models that place governing laws near the very top of the candidate pool. If the governing statutory enactment is omitted from the top-$k$ shortlist during Stage~1, downstream inference models in Stage~2 cannot detect the conflict regardless of their analytical reasoning capacity. Simultaneously, the shortlist must remain sufficiently compact ($k \le 50$) to avoid diluting the cross-attention mechanism or exceeding local workstation memory ceilings during subsequent pair-wise inference.

The full-corpus results demonstrate the severe limitations of standalone sparse lexical retrieval in large, heterogeneous legal archives. Unconstrained BM25 achieves only 29.71\% Recall@10 and 43.14\% Recall@50, meaning that over half of the governing national statutes fail to reach the candidate shortlist. This degradation occurs because routine executive issuances, administrative reorganizations, and colonial-era acts (which together comprise over 48\% of the corpus) share generic administrative vocabulary with municipal drafts, crowding out operative statutes.

Attempting to eliminate this noise through a hard domain filter causes catastrophic retrieval collapse: Recall@50 drops from 43.14\% to 13.43\%, and MRR falls to 0.0467. This failure occurs because foundational enactments, such as the Local Government Code of 1991 (RA 7160), are omnibus codes covering public health, taxation, and zoning simultaneously; filtering by a single predicted topic discards the governing code whenever a query touches an adjacent regulatory area. In contrast, incorporating the Hierarchy Priority Safeguard shields primary congressional acts (Republic Acts) from administrative issuances, elevating Mean Reciprocal Rank from 0.1521 to 0.2217 (+45.8\% improvement). From an execution standpoint, all configurations execute in approximately 300~milliseconds on a standard local CPU, confirming computational feasibility for municipal office deployment.

Because subsequent hybrid fusion with BM25 bridges the remaining semantic gap, \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} was selected as the optimal production workhorse for edge municipal deployment.

\section{Stage 2 Initial Model Training}
\label{sec:results_stage2_training}

Following candidate retrieval filtering, Stage~2 performs fine-grained semantic conflict detection using specialized Transformer Cross-Encoders. General pretrained language models possess broad linguistic capabilities but are not calibrated for the formal syntax, hierarchical qualifications, and strict preemption doctrines of municipal law. As observed in recent COLIEE benchmarks, zero-shot models routinely suffer from affirmative bias—the systematic cognitive tendency of pretrained language models to presume pairs are harmonious or logically non-conflicting unless prompted by superficial lexical negation markers—defaulting toward neutral or entailment classifications when confronted with legal text~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. Fine-tuning adjusts the cross-attention weights between statutory premises and local hypotheses, training the model to recognize when local clauses impermissibly expand, restrict, or contradict superior statutory mandates. 

It is important to emphasize that the evaluations reported in this section represent initial model exploration, screening, and hyperparameter optimization experiments conducted on the initial 350-pair ground truth benchmark (expert-curated and adviser-reviewed) to establish baseline inductive properties, optimize internal configurations, and calibrate decision thresholds prior to the final human evaluation deployed by the 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers. This section details the initial screening evaluations across 28 scouted models, supervised fine-tuning sweeps, candidate ablations, and convergence dynamics of candidate models.

\subsection{Loss Dynamics and Convergence Analysis}
In supervised machine learning, the loss function mathematically quantifies the penalty incurred when the model's predicted probability distribution diverges from the actual ground truth labels. For multi-class natural language inference, the training objective optimizes Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Loss:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} y_c \log \hat{P}(c)
\]
where $y_c \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes the binary ground truth indicator for class $c$, and $\hat{P}(c)$ represents the calibrated softmax probability output by the model. 

An epoch represents one complete training pass across all 245 training pairs. Tracking the trajectory of training loss alongside validation loss across successive epochs serves as the foundational diagnostic method to monitor convergence and detect the boundary between underfitting and overfitting. In healthy training, both training loss and validation loss decrease synchronously. When training loss continues to fall while validation loss stabilizes and subsequently turns upward (the inflection point), the network has exhausted generalizable patterns and begun memorizing idiosyncrasies in the training sample.

To evaluate convergence stability across the candidate search space without presuming a predetermined winner, Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_loss_curves} illustrates the comparative holdout validation loss dynamics and Macro $F_1$ trajectories across all six candidate finalists identified from the supervised fine-tuning leaderboard (Table~\ref{tab:stage2_hyperparameter_results}): the Top 5 empirical validation performers (\texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI}, \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI}, \texttt{ModernBERT-large}, cold \texttt{deberta-v3-base}, and \texttt{PoL-BERT-Large}) alongside the canonical supervised baseline anchor (\texttt{bert-base-uncased}) under their optimal tuning configurations ($\eta = 2\times 10^{-5}, B = 8$).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis and Regularization Controls}
The potential for model overfitting represents a critical vulnerability when adapting high-capacity transformer architectures (such as 86-million to 435-million parameter models) to a specialized, compact training set ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$). Left unregularized, a neural network readily memorizes idiosyncratic phrasing artifacts rather than learning broader statutory conflict principles, producing near-zero training error while failing on unseen municipal measures. The empirical training logs confirm that the tripartite regularization strategy—combining decoupled AdamW weight decay ($\lambda = 0.01$)~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}, attention and hidden dropout ($p = 0.10$)~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}, and early stopping validation checkpoints~\cite{prechelt1998early}—effectively curbed parameter over-specialization. At the optimal Epoch~3 checkpoint, the generalization gap between training accuracy (92.4\%) and holdout validation accuracy (84.6\%) remained tightly bounded at 7.8\%, indicating that the cross-encoders learned generalizable statutory preemption patterns rather than superficial keyword heuristics.

Candidate encoders also exhibited sharp sensitivity to the initial learning rate during hyperparameter grid exploration. At a conservative rate of $\eta = 1\times 10^{-5}$, models displayed noticeable underfitting, as the optimization trajectory progressed too slowly across the compact dataset and left validation loss elevated ($\ge 0.589$). Increasing the learning rate to $2\times 10^{-5}$ or $3\times 10^{-5}$ achieved an ideal convergence equilibrium, allowing the classification head and upper transformer layers to adapt smoothly without destabilizing lower-layer syntactic representations. Conversely, an aggressive rate of $\eta = 5\times 10^{-5}$ introduced volatility; large gradient updates computed over small mini-batches caused loss oscillations, premature validation divergence, and an average 5.8\% reduction in validation Macro $F_1$.

Finally, evaluating batch size dynamics ($B = 8$ versus $B = 16$) demonstrated that smaller mini-batches offer a valuable implicit regularization benefit. In few-shot legal classification, mini-batches of size 8 introduce beneficial stochastic gradient noise that discourages optimizers from settling into sharp local minima. Across all evaluated model families, this setting consistently produced 2.5\% to 4.0\% higher validation Macro $F_1$ scores compared to batch size 16.


\section{Summary and System-Level Roadmap}
\label{sec:results_summary_roadmap}

The empirical evaluations conducted in this chapter validate the operational viability of the coarse-to-fine architecture while mapping out clear performance bounds across both processing stages. In Stage~1, the hybrid retrieval pipeline effectively handles candidate retrieval across the national statutory corpus. By combining sparse lexical indexing with dense semantic representations under Reciprocal Rank Fusion ($k=60$) alongside the Hierarchy Priority Safeguard, Stage~1 achieves 100.0\% candidate Recall@50 with a mean CPU latency of 1.26~milliseconds. This prevents provision attrition across the evaluated benchmark when handing shortlisted statutory provisions forward to downstream inference.

In Stage~2, the initial screening evaluation across 28 scouted encoder architectures exposed the widespread presence of affirmative bias in unadapted language models, while confirming that pre-aligned NLI reasoners (DeBERTa-v3) and modern long-context encoders (ModernBERT) establish the strongest inductive foundations for legal preemption detection. Controlled supervised fine-tuning across the four candidate ablation axes demonstrated that task-specific adaptation and multi-task NLI initialization yield substantial empirical gains over canonical BERT baselines, with regularized training converging smoothly at Epoch~3 before encountering early-stopping boundaries.

In accordance with the phased research plan, these Stage~2 machine learning findings reflect initial screening, ablation, and hyperparameter optimization sweeps on the curated 350-pair ground truth benchmark. The complete end-to-end evaluation of Stage~2 inference accuracy, calibrated decision thresholds ($\tau^*$), and XAI token saliency maps will be finalized upon the conclusion of the independent five-panel human evaluation currently deployed with the fifteen Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers. Once these expert annotations are received and inter-annotator agreement metrics are verified, the elite six-model finalist pool---comprising the Top 5 empirical validation performers (\texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI}, \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI}, \texttt{ModernBERT-large}, cold \texttt{deberta-v3-base}, and \texttt{PoL-BERT-Large}) alongside the canonical \texttt{bert-base-uncased} baseline anchor---will undergo final out-of-sample benchmark testing to determine the definitive production engine, establish deployment trade-offs between large and base parameter scales, and complete system-level integration.

Following model finalization, the complete coarse-to-fine pipeline will be executed across the three evaluation tiers established in Section~\ref{sec:system_evaluation_framework}. In Tier~1 (Cascaded End-to-End Benchmark), the unassisted pipeline will be evaluated across the held-out test split, calculating the joint hit metric ($\text{Hit}_{\text{casc}}$) across the Top-50 candidate shortlist ($k=50$) to measure cascaded retrieval-inference fidelity without manual statutory priming. In Tier~2 (Injected-Fault Full-Document Stress Testing), full-length draft municipal ordinances will be audited against the statutory archive, assessing Section-Level Sensitivity across injected preemption defects and measuring Section-Level Specificity ($\text{Specificity}_{\text{sec}}$) across compliant provisions to quantify system resistance against false alarms and alert fatigue. Finally, in Tier~3 (Historical Jurisprudential Validation and LGU User Study), the system will audit the eight landmark Davao City municipal preemption controversies (\texttt{data/tier3\_jurisprudential\_cases.jsonl}) to benchmark automated citations and rationales against authentic judicial holdings, culminating in the formal usability study with the 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers using the prototype dashboard to record System Usability Scale (SUS) ratings and Fleiss' Kappa inter-annotator consensus.
