\chapter{Literature Review}
    
\section{Semantic Conflict Detection}
In jurisprudence and legal philosophy, the fundamental requirement for a functional legal order is internal consistency, where positive enactments provide unambiguous guidance without subjecting citizens to contradictory commands~\cite{fuller1964the}. In legal theory and formal deontic logic, a \textit{normative conflict} arises when legal norms impose mutually incompatible obligations or prohibitions on the same subject under the same conditions~\cite{li2012detecting, agotnes2007on}, as when one law mandates an act that another law forbids~\cite{agotnes2007on, italiani2026clash}. When statutory systems generate conflicting obligations, citizens cannot conform their conduct to the law without incurring liability under an opposing mandate; this contradiction destabilizes legal predictability and public trust. Consequently, establishing systematic mechanisms to identify and resolve normative collisions represents a vital safeguard for the integrity of any rule-of-law framework.

In practice, enacted statutes are drafted as unstructured natural language texts with wide syntactic variation and implicit exceptions rather than formal symbolic rules~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Computational legal research therefore formulates the automated identification of statutory contradictions between legislative texts as \textit{Semantic Conflict Detection}~\cite{italiani2026clash}. By modeling statutory obligations, permissions, and prohibitions from raw legislative prose, automated conflict detection systems aim to locate opposing directives across multi-generational statute collections without hand-built legal ontologies~\cite{italiani2026clash}. As Ruhl and Katz~\cite{ruhl2015measuring} established, positive law grows in volume and structural complexity far faster than human auditing capacity, motivating automated screening tools for modern legislatures.

   \subsection{Unstructured Legal Corpora}
   Managing unstructured legal text, defined as statutory material not stored in structured relational databases, poses a persistent maintenance burden for legislative systems. Automated auditing must handle continuous textual growth alongside the syntactic density of legal drafting. Unstructured enactments vary widely across historical eras in formatting and citation style. These structural realities motivate neural approaches for statutory verification.
   
        \subsubsection{Legal Inflation}
        The term \textit{legal inflation} describes the continuous growth in volume and structural complexity of statutory enactments over time~\cite{ruhl2015measuring}. Ruhl and Katz~\cite{ruhl2015measuring} show that modern legislative bodies pass new rules at a pace and interconnectedness that exceed human capacity for manual consistency checks. Over time, uncoordinated or overlapping enactments accumulate when new measures are adopted without harmonizing them against earlier statutory codes~\cite{ruhl2015measuring}. In local government administration, this problem intensifies because local ordinances are rarely compiled into centralized statutory codes~\cite{conchas2025digital}. Conchas and Salapa~\cite{conchas2025digital} emphasize that converting historical local enactments into machine-readable data creates major engineering hurdles because records remain scattered across codified ordinances and transitory executive orders.

        Consequently, the statutory search space forms a fragmented archive spanning multiple council eras instead of a structured relational database. Zhong et al.~\cite{zhong2020how} characterize this challenge as a dynamic consistency problem in which governing obligations shift across successive administrations. Because local statutory data lacks centralized indexing, local governments discover preemption defects reactively after an invalid measure is challenged in court. This backlog has shifted legal informatics research away from rule-based systems toward language models that learn semantic representations directly from unstructured text~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Automated tools are therefore needed to inspect extensive legislative collections that exceed human reading throughput.

        \subsubsection{Limits of Manual Review}
        Once physical legislative records are digitized, the main operational hurdle becomes checking whether newly introduced provisions align logically with governing positive law. As legislative volume rises, manual review workflows are strained by the mental load of cross-referencing multi-volume statutory digests. Human reviewers must also distinguish benign regulatory overlap (adjacent laws governing distinct aspects of the same policy area) from destructive statutory conflict (an enactment contradicting a superior mandate). Recent surveys confirm that conventional keyword search platforms consistently miss these subtle legal distinctions~\cite{ariai2025natural}. As a result, latent statutory contradictions frequently slip past legislative staff during drafting and surface only during adversarial court challenges~\cite{ariai2025natural, scp1994magtajasvpryce}.

        \subsubsection{Semantic Ambiguity}
        Traditional search systems rely on exact character matching, operating on the premise that legal concepts can be retrieved by searching for specific vocabulary strings. However, natural legal language exhibits pervasive \textit{semantic ambiguity} driven by \textit{synonymy} (different words expressing the same concept, such as \textit{prohibited} versus \textit{unlawful}) and \textit{polysemy} (one word assuming different meanings in context, such as a \textit{party} referring to a litigant instead of a social gathering)~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue, Greco2023Bringing}. In statutory drafting, binding legal force is governed by deontic modal verbs (such as \textit{shall} and \textit{may}) whose obligations cannot be isolated through simple string matching. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} demonstrate that exact-match techniques fail on such syntactic ambiguity: a local ordinance regulating ``motor vehicles'' and a national statute governing ``two-wheeled conveyances'' address the same activity, yet a Boolean query for ``automobile'' misses both provisions.

        Furthermore, legal consistency frequently depends on implied normative boundaries instead of explicit textual declarations. Katz et al.~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp} establish that identifying subtle contradictions requires evaluating logical relationships and contextual semantics beyond surface character overlap. Transformer language models address this need by mapping text sequences into high-dimensional vector spaces that capture contextual semantics~\cite{zheng2021when}, though Sovrano et al.~\cite{Sovrano2021Making} warn that uninspected neural activations act as black boxes that cannot explain why an alert fired. In administrative and legislative settings, articulating the verifiable \textit{ratio decidendi} behind an alert is as important as the detection itself~\cite{savelka2021discovering}.

    \subsection{Local Conflict Detection}
    International legal informatics benchmarks such as LexGLUE advance automated analysis across Western legal corpora, yet corresponding research in post-colonial developing jurisdictions like the Philippines remains sparse~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Since the passage of the Local Government Code of 1991, Philippine local government units have enacted thousands of local ordinances that remain largely unindexed in machine-readable formats~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. These decentralized enactments govern everyday commerce and public health, yet lack standardized cross-referencing to national statutes. Theoretical algorithms for conflict detection exist in artificial intelligence literature, but their empirical application to Philippine subnational legislation remains largely unaddressed.

        \subsubsection{Philippine Legal NLP}
        Within domestic computer science, research applying NLP to Philippine law has historically focused on document classification and extractive summarization instead of deductive logical reasoning. Early investigations used supervised classifiers to forecast Supreme Court appellate outcomes~\cite{virtucio2018predicting} or condensed jurisprudence via extractive algorithms~\cite{sagum2023philippine}. More recent studies explore dense transformer embeddings that map statutory passages into geometric vectors for topical search~\cite{roque2024legal}. Dense embeddings outperform keyword search in retrieving domain statutes such as tax regulations, yet remain correlational: they spot shared subject matter without testing whether two provisions contradict one another.

        Analyzing these research patterns exposes a clear structural gap in local literature. Philippine NLP research has automated national statute retrieval and shown that inference models can detect contradictions across informal local fact-checking claims~\cite{virtucio2018predicting, sagum2023philippine, roque2024legal, nunez2026pomi}. However, national benchmarks do not account for the decentralized drafting habits and archival degradation common in subnational legislation. Extending automated conflict detection to the multi-generational ordinances of a Highly Urbanized City like Davao therefore remains unexplored.

        \subsubsection{Logic and Conflict Research}
        Within Philippine computational linguistics, NLI has been applied in adjacent tasks such as flagging misinformation and rumors in low-resource text. Nu\~nez et al.~\cite{nunez2026pomi} demonstrated that transformer models resolve directional contradictions across Philippine English and code-switched Taglish claims. Local government record systems still rely predominantly on Boolean keyword searches and static rule heuristics, which miss synonymous drafting terms (such as ``firecrackers'' versus ``pyrotechnic devices'') and fail on optical character recognition noise~\cite{lopresti2008optical}; in Philippine text processing, Naguio and Roxas~\cite{naguio2024hybrid} similarly show that pure rule-based pipelines struggle with high lexical variation unless paired with neural representations. Manual keyword queries therefore cannot isolate statutory contradictions reliably across thousands of local ordinances.

    
        \subsubsection{Research Gaps}
        Existing studies confirm that subnational legislation poses distinct challenges compared to compiled national codes. Four interrelated barriers hinder automated local ordinance auditing: scarce annotated local datasets, constrained workstation hardware inside local offices, multilingual phrasing, and institutional distrust of opaque neural outputs. Addressing these barriers defines the practical design space for ex-ante legislative screening.

        The primary structural barrier is the lack of curated ground-truth datasets for Philippine local enactments. Unlike national statutes stored in centralized repositories, local ordinances form a low-resource textual environment~\cite{ranathunga2022some}. Standard legal NLP models train on large Western benchmarks like LexGLUE, leaving no equivalent annotated dataset of conflicting Philippine local ordinances~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis, nrcp2024review}. Without a standardized benchmark to measure detection accuracy, domestic legal informatics has struggled to advance beyond keyword search to deductive reasoning~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}.

        Compounding the dataset deficit is a computational resource constraint inside LGUs. Deep cross-encoder inference models demand compute and memory resources that standard municipal and city offices lack. Studies on Philippine public administration show that local digital adoption is limited by tight budgets and legacy office desktop hardware~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}. In a local legislative council readiness survey, only 20\% of council members had formal ICT training and 40\% owned capable digital devices, with offices still relying on paper workflows and basic text editors instead of structured databases~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}. This infrastructure deficit rules out heavy architectures that exceed the capacity of everyday LGU workstations~\cite{huangetal2025integrated}.

        Hardware budgets in local government procurement reflect this constraint directly. Under Commission on Audit guidelines (COA Circular No.~2022-004), agencies classify ICT hardware under \textpeso 50,000.00 as semi-expendable property~\cite{coa2022circular004}, and standard public-sector workstation specifications list Intel Core i3 or Core i5 CPUs with 8~GB of DDR4 RAM and no discrete GPU accelerators~\cite{dict2020specs}. Alongside local ICT training gaps~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}, these hardware limits rule out deploying multi-billion-parameter generative models locally, while cloud APIs introduce recurring costs and confidentiality risks for unpublished draft ordinances. Ex-ante conflict detection for local councils must therefore operate offline within commodity 8~GB CPU workstation limits.

        At the linguistic level, local legislative texts reflect a multilingual environment in which formal legal English coexists with Cebuano and Filipino expressions~\cite{dreisbach2021language}. Cebuano vocabulary also contains Spanish loanwords whose meanings have shifted over decades~\cite{maxilom2008semantic}, creating lexical variation that monolingual English tokenizers frequently split or misread. Recent studies show that transformer models can parse regional expressions in informal text, yet extending that capability to dense local ordinances remains largely untested in domestic literature~\cite{citygov2025lissp, Greco2023Bringing}.

        Finally, institutional caution slows the adoption of automated consistency tools in legal offices. As Supreme Court Senior Associate Justice Marvic Leonen noted, practitioners remain wary of artificial intelligence when neural systems act as black boxes unable to explain their outputs~\cite{leonen2024keynote}. Legislative counsel worry that unverified tool outputs could allow subtle statutory errors to slip into enacted ordinances~\cite{conchas2025digital}. As a result, prior systems favored passive document indexing, leaving semantic conflict detection largely unaddressed in local governance~\cite{ashley2017artificial}.

\section{Philippine Legislative Domain}
The Philippine legal system operates under a unitary constitutional framework, wherein LGUs possess purely delegated legislative powers conferred by Congress~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. This jurisdictional structure establishes a strict hierarchical subordination that bounds local lawmaking beneath national legislative authority. Any computational system engineered to audit local ordinances must operationalize these jurisdictional constraints. This section analyzes the statutory and structural conditions governing Philippine local law across constitutional hierarchy, historical syntax, plenary enactment rules, ex-ante review, and consistency tools.

    \subsection{Hierarchical Constraints}
    The Philippine legal system operates under a unitary constitutional framework in which national laws strictly control subordinate local enactments~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. At the apex stands the 1987 Constitution, followed by national statutes enacted by Congress alongside international treaties, presidential executive issuances, and local ordinances. Under this constitutional structure, no LGU can enact measures that contradict an Act of Congress. The doctrine of national preemption establishes an absolute boundary: whenever the national legislature enacts comprehensive statutory standards governing a given regulatory domain, local governments are legally precluded from enacting conflicting or diluting local rules~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. Consequently, any automated conflict detection system for local drafting must reflect these structural rules subordinating local ordinances beneath national statutes.

    The primary organic charter governing local lawmaking is the Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No.~7160). Section~16 of the Code, known as the General Welfare Clause, delegates police powers to local legislative councils to enact ordinances promoting constituent welfare, provided such measures remain strictly within statutory boundaries~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. Because Davao City is classified as a Highly Urbanized City (HUC), it operates autonomously from provincial administrative supervision and its ordinances are not reviewed by a provincial board. This autonomy places the entire burden of ex-ante consistency auditing directly upon the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod before measures proceed to final enactment~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    The definitive judicial test governing the validity of local legislation is established by the Supreme Court in the landmark ruling \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corporation, Inc.}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}. To withstand judicial scrutiny, an ordinance must satisfy six substantive requirements:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item It must not contravene the Constitution or any statute;
        \item It must not be unfair or oppressive;
        \item It must not be partial or discriminatory;
        \item It must not prohibit but may regulate trade;
        \item It must be general and consistent with public policy; and
        \item It must not be unreasonable.
    \end{itemize}
    Because local legislative bodies possess purely delegated authority, local councils cannot prohibit activities that national legislation has expressly authorized or regulated~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. This principle of statutory subordination forms the primary legal benchmark against which automated conflict detection systems must evaluate candidate local drafts.

    When a local ordinance impermissibly enters an area governed by national legislation, Philippine courts consistently apply the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine to declare the local measure invalid. In \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corporation, Inc.}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, the City Council of Cagayan de Oro enacted ordinances prohibiting casino operations to protect public morals. The Supreme Court struck down the local enactments as \textit{ultra vires}, ruling that a local council cannot prohibit commercial gambling activities that a national statute expressly authorized through the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR) charter. Similar preemption disputes have directly affected Davao City legislation. In \textit{Mosqueda v. Pilipino Banana Growers \& Exporters Association, Inc.}~\cite{scp2016mosqueda}, the Supreme Court invalidated Davao City Ordinance No.~0309-07, which imposed a local ban on aerial pesticide spraying across agricultural plantations, holding that the ordinance exceeded local police powers and collided with the regulatory authority granted to the national Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority under Presidential Decree No.~1144. In \textit{City of Manila v. Laguio, Jr.}~\cite{scp2005manilavlaguio}, the Court likewise struck down an ordinance prohibiting motels and massage parlors, holding that local police powers cannot arbitrarily prohibit lawful commercial activities under the guise of zoning regulation.

    In contrast to vertical statutory preemption, local lawmaking also involves horizontal consistency, where newly proposed ordinances must align with existing fellow local enactments. When drafting local legislation, the Sangguniang Panlungsod uses the Three-Reading legislative process, particularly the committee debates and line-by-line amendments during the Second Reading, to harmonize new proposals with older local enactments~\cite{spdc2022ra00122}. Unlike vertical conflicts that represent fatal legal defects, inconsistencies between a new draft and an older local ordinance frequently reflect intentional legislative updates, such as revising local tax rates or adjusting traffic routes. To manage these horizontal modifications systematically, legislative drafters rely on standardized legislative saving clauses established under Philippine local legislative practice~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}. Every formal ordinance incorporates an express or general repealing clause, declaring that prior inconsistent local enactments are amended or repealed accordingly. Moreover, both national statutes and local ordinances include a separability clause so that if judicial authorities invalidate any individual section, the remaining provisions retain full legal force~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. However, because local legal researchers must review hundreds of active enactments under strict legislative session schedules, subtle inconsistencies often go unnoticed during the Second Reading and proceed to final enactment.

    A fundamental design decision in legal informatics is delineating which positive legal instruments establish binding preemption standards. Under Article~VII, Section~17 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution and Book~III, Title~I, Chapter~2, Section~2 of the Administrative Code of 1987 (Executive Order No.~292)~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, Executive Orders (EOs) are acts of the President providing general or permanent rules in execution of constitutional or statutory mandates. Unlike Supreme Court decisions (which resolve adversarial controversies retrospectively under the doctrine of \textit{stare decisis} in Article~8 of the Civil Code), Executive Orders operate as prospective statutory instruments carrying the force of law. Furthermore, during both the Martial Law period (1972--1986) and the Provisional Government under the Freedom Constitution (Proclamation No.~3, 1986--1987), the President exercised direct legislative powers through Executive Orders and Presidential Decrees to establish substantive national enactments that remain operative law today. Modern Executive Orders frequently establish binding nationwide regulatory standards, such as Executive Order No.~26 (s.~2017) establishing nationwide public smoking prohibitions or Executive Order No.~168 (s.~2014) creating the Inter-Agency Task Force for the Management of Emerging Infectious Diseases (IATF), that legally preempt conflicting local legislation. Excluding Executive Orders would therefore omit critical nationwide regulatory mandates that local ordinances are legally bound to respect.

    \subsection{Historical and Stylistic Heritage}
    The structural complexity of Philippine legislative text stems directly from the nation's historical experience as a mixed legal jurisdiction~\cite{agpalo2009statutory, cruz2007political}. Philippine jurisprudence combines continental civil law traditions with Anglo-American statutory common law conventions. Understanding why Philippine statutes and local ordinances are drafted in their characteristic syntax requires examining how these colonial and post-colonial traditions fused over more than a century of codification. Tracing that syntactic evolution clarifies why automated statutory parsers encounter long nested clauses and mixed terminology in local ordinances.

    During more than three centuries of Spanish rule, the Philippine legal system was rooted firmly in the Roman-Germanic civil law tradition~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. This continental tradition emphasized systematic codification and generalized legal mandates organized into structured books and titles instead of granular factual definitions. Furthermore, this civilian heritage embedded Latin doctrinal maxims into Philippine jurisprudence, including \textit{in pari materia} (construing related laws together), \textit{generalia specialibus non derogant} (a general law does not override a specific one), and \textit{ultra vires} (acts beyond legal authority)~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. Modern Philippine codes retain this civilian structure, expecting adjudicators to derive specific legal duties from broad normative commands.

    Following the Treaty of Paris and the establishment of the American Insular Government (1900--1935), American legal administrators introduced common law statutory drafting habits without fully displacing the civilian codification base~\cite{cruz2007political}. Enacted by the Philippine Commission and later the Philippine Legislature, early statutes introduced formal conventions borrowed from United States federal and state legislation, including title preambles and explicit repealing provisions~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. Significantly, American administrators introduced extensive conditional provisos (``\textit{Provided, that...}'' and ``\textit{Provided, further, that...}'') to restrict or qualify preceding operative clauses within the same section. The Revised Administrative Code of 1917 (Act No.~2711) and the contemporary Administrative Code of 1987 (Executive Order No.~292) solidified this mixed tradition and established English as the controlling statutory language while keeping civil law institutional categories~\cite{philgov1987eo292}.

    This dual heritage produces a legislative drafting style marked by high syntactic density and multi-clause complexity. Unlike standard conversational English, a single Philippine statutory provision often packs multiple dependent clauses and recursive provisos inside one grammatical sentence~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}. At the local level, legislative staff adopt this formal national drafting template while adding procedural boilerplate, so verbose city ordinances often separate the operative command from its governing subject across long spans of intervening text~\cite{spdc2022ra00122}. Archaic legal terms, long-distance dependencies, and inverting provisos therefore pose persistent challenges unless text is read in full structural context.

    \subsection{Plenary Legislative Procedure}
    Besides the hierarchy of laws, the actual parliamentary mechanism through which laws are drafted, debated, and approved determines how statutory text evolves over time. In the Philippine legal architecture, positive law enactment is governed by a mandatory three-reading procedure conducted during formal plenary sessions of the legislative body. At the national level, Article~VI, Section~26, Paragraph~2 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution explicitly dictates that no bill passed by either the House of Representatives or the Senate shall become law unless it has passed three readings on separate days, with printed copies in final form distributed to members at least three days prior to passage~\cite{philconst1987, philhouse2022rules}. At the local government level, Sections~53 and 54 of the Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No.~7160)~\cite{philgov1991ra7160} and the Internal Rules of Procedure of the 20th Sangguniang Panlungsod of Davao City (Resolution No.~001-22)~\cite{spdc2022ra00122} replicate this parliamentary doctrine. Under these statutory rules, every proposed municipal or city ordinance must undergo three distinct readings before the full assembly in plenary session. This parliamentary mechanism is designed to guarantee institutional transparency and deliberate democratic accountability before any regulatory constraint attains the force of law.

    The legislative progression begins on the plenary floor with the First Reading. In both national congressional chambers and the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod, the First Reading is ministerial: the presiding officer directs the Secretary to read only the assigned measure number and title before the plenary body~\cite{philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. Under parliamentary rules, no substantive debate or floor amendment is permitted during First Reading. Instead, the presiding officer refers the measure immediately to the appropriate standing committee (such as the Committee on Rules, Laws, and Ordinances) to start committee review. In that stage, legal counsel and committee researchers examine the draft and prepare the formal committee report. Because local ordinance drafting is decentralized, with individual councilors authoring separate proposals instead of using a single central drafting bureau~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}, drafting conventions and legal terminology vary widely. Legal researchers bear the heavy burden of manually cross-referencing proposed clauses against national statutes and prior city enactments under tight session schedules. When staff lack automated semantic search tools, potential preemption defects and horizontal contradictions frequently survive committee scrutiny undetected.

    Following committee review, the proposed ordinance returns to the plenary floor for the Second Reading, which is the main substantive and deliberative phase of the legislative lifecycle. The Second Reading begins with a sponsorship speech by the committee chair or measure proponent summarizing the policy objectives and legal basis of the enactment. Immediately afterward, the plenary assembly enters the Period of Interpellation and General Debate, during which colleagues interrogate the sponsor, probe statutory ambiguities, and raise substantive legal objections regarding whether the measure contravenes superior national legislation. Once general debate closes, the body transitions into the Period of Amendments, where committee amendments and individual floor amendments are debated and voted upon section by section~\cite{philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. This period is the decisive procedural window where proposed clauses are revised or deleted to align with governing law.

    The Period of Interpellation and Amendments on Second Reading is the exact procedural nexus where an automated semantic conflict detection system delivers its highest institutional utility. Because the Second Reading is the sole plenary phase where textual alterations and statutory reconciliations can be introduced, legal researchers and sponsors require granular, evidence-backed conflict diagnostics before and during floor deliberations. If a draft ordinance clause encroaches upon a national statutory domain (such as violating the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine by prohibiting what national law permits, or exceeding statutory penalty caps under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160), an automated screening tool can pinpoint the conflicting statutory premise and flag contradictory deontic terms within an explainable audit trail. Equipping Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers with these findings lets them draft precise floor amendments during the Period of Amendments, resolving preemption flaws before the measure advances past the amendable phase.

    After the assembly adopts all floor amendments and finalizes the draft on Second Reading, the measure is engrossed, printed in its final form, and scheduled for Third Reading in plenary session. In stark contrast to the debates of Second Reading, the Third Reading is procedurally rigid and unmodifiable. Under Article~VI, Section~26, Paragraph~2 of the 1987 Constitution and corresponding local Sanggunian rules, no amendment whatsoever is permitted upon the Third Reading of a bill or ordinance~\cite{philconst1987, philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. Plenary action is confined to a nominal roll-call vote accompanied by a brief oral explanation from each voting member. This procedural finality creates concrete institutional risk: if a statutory collision goes undetected before or during the Second Reading, the flawed provision is locked into positive law upon Third Reading approval. Once enacted, the ordinance becomes vulnerable to judicial nullification under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine or administrative challenges before national executive departments. This structural irreversibility shows why ex-ante computational conflict detection belongs in committee review and Second Reading preparation.

    Compounding the procedural complexities of the Three Readings is the statutory mandate requiring local legislation to be accessible across languages. Under Section~469 of the Local Government Code, the Secretary to the Sanggunian is mandated to translate approved ordinances into local dialects, such as Cebuano for Davao City~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122, dreisbach2021language}. However, this creates legal friction because the Administrative Code of 1987 establishes that the English statutory text strictly controls in cases of conflict~\cite{philgov1987eo292}. If an error occurs in regional translation, a citizen relying on the Cebuano text may inadvertently violate the authoritative English version; this disparity conflicts with the public notice doctrine in \textit{Tañada v. Tuvera}~\cite{sc1985tanadavtuvera}. Despite policy initiatives such as the proposed \textit{Batas sa Sariling Wika} legislative measure (House Bill No.~3863)~\cite{reganit2025diokno}, subtle dialect differences often produce semantic drift where the exact legal obligation in English is distorted in regional vernacular text~\cite{maxilom2008semantic}. Consequently, consistency checking systems must operate primarily on the authoritative English statutory provisions while accounting for regional linguistic artifacts generated during local committee debates and drafting iterations.

    \subsection{Ex-Ante Legislative Review}
    The term ``ex-ante'' derives from Latin meaning ``before the event,'' and in administrative jurisprudence it denotes the evaluation of a proposed law or local regulation prior to official enactment~\cite{fried2015exanteexpost}. This prospective review aims to spot and fix potential statutory collisions and unintended regulatory burdens while the draft remains open to revision. In the Philippines, ex-ante regulatory review gained institutional prominence under Republic Act No.~11032, the Ease of Doing Business and Efficient Government Service Delivery Act of 2018~\cite{philippines2018ra11032}. Under this statute, the Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA) alongside the DILG mandates that LGUs conduct a formal Regulatory Impact Assessment (RIA) for proposed commercial regulations to ensure valid statutory authority and avoid contradictory rules~\cite{philippines2018ra11032}.

    Ex-ante evaluation differs from ex-post evaluation in when review occurs and what legal effect it carries. Ex-ante evaluation inspects prospective legislative texts (such as draft local ordinances and pending congressional bills) before they take binding effect. In contrast, ex-post evaluation operates retrospectively through adversarial court litigation and Supreme Court decisions governed by \textit{stare decisis}. Ex-post judicial adjudication rectifies legal injuries only after harm has occurred and imposes heavy litigation costs, since repealing an active ordinance requires prolonged court proceedings~\cite{gesteletal2011exanteevaluation}. Ex-ante auditing is therefore substantially more efficient, shielding local governments from administrative deadlock and judicial invalidation.

    Automated computational tools help local legislative researchers spot normative collisions during drafting. Because legislatures accumulate large statutory volumes, manual review often overlooks subtle clashes between new drafts and active statutes~\cite{ruhl2015measuring}. Operating as an assistive screening layer, automated systems flag candidate preemption risks while the ordinance remains in committee. Early screening supports alignment with superior national statutes and reduces the chance of enacting measures that are void \textit{ab initio}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}.

    \subsection{Legislative Consistency Checking}
    Because local governments exercise purely delegated authority within a decentralized drafting environment, consistency checking is an essential institutional safeguard. Checking whether an ordinance aligns with superior national statutes cannot rely on document titles alone and requires inspecting substantive meaning alongside delegated statutory limits~\cite{agpalo2009statutory, italiani2026clash, scp1994magtajasvpryce}. Current manual workflows remain constrained by syntactic ambiguity and reliance on basic document tracking platforms. Addressing these limits requires inspection tools that examine substantive provisions across local and national repositories.

    Discourse analysis of Philippine statutory language reveals recurring ambiguity in normative keywords and modal verbs~\cite{scp1970villacortavbernardo}. In particular, the modal auxiliary ``shall'' creates interpretive friction because it can signal an absolute command, an administrative instruction, or a directory permission depending on context. Conjunctions such as ``and/or'' and ambiguous modifier placement add syntactic uncertainty even when enactments follow formal drafting manuals~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}. These linguistic hazards make surface-level keyword searches unreliable for verifying compliance.

    Philippine jurisprudence also recognizes that statutory conflicts seldom appear as overt declarations~\cite{cruz2007political}. A local ordinance virtually never states an explicit intent to contradict Congress, so preemption disputes typically arise by implication when a council adds unauthorized local permits or fees to activities already licensed by a national statute~\cite{scp1970villacortavbernardo}. Supreme Court rulings consistently invalidate such local restrictions as \textit{ultra vires} exercises of police power. Uncovering implied preemption across divergent statutory vocabularies exceeds keyword search and requires semantic analysis.

    In Davao City, the Sangguniang Panlungsod passes nearly 400 ordinances and over 2,000 resolutions within a single three-year council term~\cite{patumbon2025landmark}. To manage document flow, the council deploys the Resolution Ordinance Tracking System (ROTS) for metadata routing and the Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP) to store digital enactments~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. Digital tracking streamlines routing, yet substantive consistency verification across provisions remains manual, and archival scans frequently contain OCR noise that causes keyword searches to miss governing provisions~\cite{lopresti2008optical}.

\section{Digital Legislative Archives}
Building a functional Semantic Conflict Detection system requires access to comprehensive and machine-readable legal corpora. In the Philippines, digital legislative data is highly fragmented between national statutory repositories and local city archives. This structural split creates major engineering hurdles for data ingestion before logical comparison can even begin. Examining how each repository stores and exposes statutory text helps explain the requirements for reliable preprocessing.

   \subsection{National Sources}
   National legal repositories sit at the apex of the Philippine legislative hierarchy and house laws that preempt conflicting local ordinances. These databases are maintained centrally and contain formal statutory texts such as Republic Acts and Commonwealth Acts. They form the authoritative reference corpus against which local drafts are evaluated. Because national legislation follows standardized drafting manuals, its vocabulary and structure remain relatively consistent, giving retrieval systems a clear view of state mandates.

   The Official Gazette is the constitutionally mandated journal of record for national legislative text~\cite{officialgazette}. It publishes newly enacted laws, presidential executive orders, and administrative issuances as required by Republic Act No.~453~\cite{act453_1902} and Commonwealth Act No.~638~\cite{ca638_1941}. Because it holds the legally binding text of national statutes, statutory preemption tools anchor baseline rules to this archive. It preserves the exact phrasing approved by Congress and the President across decades.

   Complementing the official journal, developers use the Open Congress API from the House of Representatives Legislative Information System (LEGIS) to pull structured metadata on bill routing and amendments~\cite{hrep2023legis}. This interface tracks a statute's formal lifecycle from committee filing to executive approval. Standardized metadata lets software pipelines trace statutory dependencies without parsing every header manually. Combining structured metadata with full-text archives helps systems distinguish active laws from superseded acts.

   Finally, the Lawphil Project is a privately maintained digital repository housing statutory records and judicial decisions~\cite{lawphil2000}. Unlike government sites whose page templates change periodically, this repository maintains a pre-digitized plaintext corpus spanning historical and contemporary Philippine law. This stable formatting supports domain-specific vectorization and pre-training for local legal text so language models can learn statutory syntax and deontic phrasing directly~\cite{zheng2021when}.

   \subsection{Local Sources}
   In contrast to centralized national repositories, local records are maintained independently by each LGU. Local archives store local ordinances and executive issuances governing specific city jurisdictions. Because ordinance output is decentralized, archiving formats and search features vary widely across city councils. These repositories hold the existing regulatory constraints needed to verify consistency within a municipality or city.

   The local archive in Davao City centers on the Sangguniang Panlungsod portal, the council's official digital repository~\cite{davaocity_sp_dept}. This portal preserves historical city ordinances and council resolutions enacted within city limits. It expands continually as the council passes new measures in plenary sessions, supplying the city laws required when checking proposed drafts for local consistency.

   To manage its growing docket, Davao City introduced LISSP as an administrative tracking platform~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. LISSP stores structured metadata covering ordinance routing and plenary approval status and provides real-time visibility into measure progress~\cite{patumbon2025lissp}. Digitized workflow tracking supplies helpful lifecycle context and addresses several interoperability limits noted in regional e-governance studies~\cite{conchas2025digital}.

    \subsection{Data Extraction Challenges}
    Acquiring statutory text from national and local repositories exposes ingestion pipelines to noise and structural gaps along two main dimensions. Many archived documents exist only as scanned page images that suffer from OCR degradation. Public portals also restrict full-corpus browsing, isolating local records from automated collection.

        \subsubsection{Archival Scanning and OCR Degradation}
        A major barrier to automated statutory auditing is the lack of machine-readable text across archival collections. Both the Official Gazette and the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod public archive predominantly host scanned image PDFs instead of selectable digital text. Archived scans frequently exhibit slanted pages and faded ink. As Lopresti~\cite{lopresti2008optical} documents, noisy scans cause OCR degradation, where standard extraction tools output broken alphanumeric strings. Those errors fragment legal semantics unless pipelines apply dedicated extraction models and conservative text normalization.

        Character and word corruptions hinder downstream automated checks directly. In statutory auditing, one corrupted character can flip an affirmative obligation into a negative prohibition or scramble a penalty integer. Standard subword tokenizers also shatter garbled words into out-of-vocabulary fragments that distort vector representations~\cite{rust2021how}. Scanning artifacts further obscure section headers and numbering and prevent parsers from separating independent statutory rules.

        \subsubsection{Repository Scarcity and Digital Isolation}
        Archival gaps create a second risk for statutory retrieval. Missing even one contradicting ordinance can cause a screening system to overlook a conflict simply because the governing record was absent from the index. The Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod public archive exhibits intermittently missing ordinance files and unorganized chronological listings~\cite{spdavao2026}, and even broad national mirrors such as Lawphil occasionally omit historical statutes or decrees. As Westermann et al.~\cite{Westermann2019computer} note, patchy repositories force developers to build fault-tolerant collection pipelines instead of simple web scrapers.

        Public portals also constrain bulk access to complete city records. Public transparency portals such as LISSP~\cite{citygov2025lissp, patumbon2025lissp} support individual lookup: users can search for a known ordinance and open its PDF document directly, yet no browsable visual list of all enacted ordinances is exposed publicly, and full-text bulk repositories stay behind authenticated council interfaces. Conchas and Salapa~\cite{conchas2025digital} note that such administrative silos leave historical enactments disconnected from bulk search indices, requiring direct institutional coordination and offline document reconciliation to build an exhaustive local archive.


\section{Exact and Symbolic Methods}
Before the adoption of neural networks, computational legal analysis relied heavily on deterministic systems to process text. Deterministic systems apply fixed rule matrices in which an input produces an exact output without statistical estimation. Early legal search and validation methods assumed legislative rules could be mapped cleanly to literal keyword strings or hand-coded logic formulas. Those deterministic pipelines remain transparent and easy to trace, yet they break down on unstructured local ordinances phrased in varied everyday language.

    \subsection{Boolean Information Retrieval}
    Understanding why legacy legal portals miss normative conflicts requires examining how deterministic search engines process statutory text. Boolean Information Retrieval (BIR) underlies traditional legal databases, treating each enactment as an unordered multiset of words while discarding sentence syntax and clause order. At its core lies an \textit{inverted index} mapping every vocabulary term to document identifiers and frequencies. Because an inverted index records only literal string presence, BIR cannot infer semantic relationships or underlying regulatory intent.
    
    In operational search engines, BIR retrieval relies on set theory and formal Boolean logic. A user constructs a query using operators like \texttt{AND} ($\land$), \texttt{OR} ($\lor$), and \texttt{NOT} ($\neg$) to intersect document sets, as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:boolean_query}:

    \noindent\textit{Boolean Query Set Intersection Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
        Q = (\text{fireworks} \lor \text{pyrotechnics}) \land \neg \text{exempt}
        \label{eq:boolean_query}
        \eqcaption{Boolean Query Set Intersection Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where the compound query $Q$ joins disjunctive keyword terms with a negated exemption filter. The engine retrieves files containing every requested term and discards any document missing a single query word. Consequently, BIR scores literal token hits while ignoring statutory meaning~\cite{blair1985an}.

        The main weakness of BIR in legal auditing is exact string matching, which creates a ``vocabulary mismatch'' problem. Natural language displays synonymy (for instance, ``prohibited'' and ``unlawful'') and polysemy (one term carrying different meanings across contexts). Because BIR matches literal characters instead of concepts, it cannot link paraphrased obligations across statutes.
        
        A classic legal retrieval study measured this mismatch in an active litigation setting. Attorneys querying a full-text BIR system estimated they were recovering over 75 percent of relevant case documents because returned results looked pertinent. In reality, the Boolean engine retrieved under 20 percent of relevant documents~\cite{blair1985an}; the missing 80 percent used valid legal wording outside the researchers' exact search terms~\cite{opijnen2017on}.

        Missing a single contradicting ordinance during local legislative review creates legal exposure for a city council. Legislative vocabulary also shifts across decades, so a 1990s ordinance and a 2026 draft may regulate the same civic activity with disjoint keywords. Resolving vocabulary mismatch therefore requires neural models that map distinct phrasings onto shared vector coordinates~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    \subsection{Symbolic AI}
    Because BIR misses semantic equivalence, early artificial intelligence researchers modeled statutory rules directly using Symbolic AI, also called ``Good Old-Fashioned AI'' (GOFAI)~\cite{vellino1986artificial}. Instead of scanning unstructured text strings, Symbolic AI translates statutes into explicit production rules and first-order deontic logic so deductive engines can evaluate compliance deterministically.

    Symbolic systems combine first-order predicates with deontic modalities governing legal conduct: obligations ($\mathcal{O}$), permissions ($\mathcal{P}$), and prohibitions ($\mathcal{F}$). A local zoning ban can be formalized as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:first_order_deontic}:

    \noindent\textit{First-Order Deontic Prohibition Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \forall x (\text{Business}(x) \land \text{ResidentialZone}(x) \rightarrow \text{Prohibited}(x))
        \label{eq:first_order_deontic}
        \eqcaption{First-Order Deontic Prohibition Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where the universal quantifier ($\forall$) assigns prohibition ($\rightarrow$) to any business entity ($x$) operating inside a residential zone ($\land$). When Rule~A requires $\mathcal{O}(X)$ and Rule~B enforces $\mathcal{F}(X)$, a solver detects a formal logical contradiction ($\bot$)~\cite{athan2013legalruleml}. Complete formalization delivers exact deductive guarantees on unambiguous facts.

        In practice, manual rule encoding stops symbolic systems from scaling across real-world municipal archives. Building an expert system runs into the knowledge acquisition barrier: engineers and lawyers must translate thousands of unstructured ordinance clauses by hand into formal predicates, a slow and error-prone workflow~\cite{lucos2025neurosymbolic}. Every subsequent council amendment also forces manual rewrites of all affected logic rules.

        Rigid binary predicates also clash with what legal philosopher H.L.A.~Hart called the \textit{open texture of law}~\cite{hart1961concept}. Because statutes cannot foresee every future dispute, lawmakers intentionally write discretionary phrases such as ``reasonable care'' and ``good moral conduct.'' Those context-dependent standards resist binary IF-THEN rules~\cite{ashley2017artificial}. Symbolic engines achieve exact precision inside narrow formal environments, yet they break down on the linguistic variance and unformalized exceptions of local legislative archives~\cite{zhong2020how}.


    
\section{Graph-Based Architectures}
Because isolated string matching misses structural ties across statutes, legal informatics studies model enactments as interconnected graphs instead of flat documents. A graph representation captures how laws cite, amend, and supersede one another across a broader regulatory hierarchy. Literature develops these structures at two main levels: corpus citation networks and sentence semantic predicate graphs.

    \subsection{Knowledge Graphs and GNNs}
    In computer science, a Knowledge Graph (KG) is a structured knowledge representation that stores real-world entities as nodes and typed relationships as edges in the form of semantic triples $(e_1, r, e_2)$~\cite{li2024construction}. Entities $e_1$ and $e_2$ represent concrete objects, concepts, or informational resources, while $r$ denotes the directional relation linking them. Extending this general data model to legislation yields Legal Knowledge Graphs (LKGs), which model statutory ecosystems as directed multi-relational graphs $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$. In an LKG, vertices $v \in \mathcal{V}$ represent statutory enactments, administrative agencies, or operative provisions, while edges $(u, r, v) \in \mathcal{E}$ encode legal relationships $r \in \mathcal{R}$ such as delegation, citation, or amendment. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) then learn node embeddings over these graphs by aggregating neighbor features~\cite{ma2025gnn, yang2021legalgnn}. Frameworks like GNN-ERE extract entities and links directly from statutory prose~\cite{ma2025gnn}, improving retrieval across related legal texts even without shared keywords~\cite{harde2025lecnet}.

        \subsubsection{Graph Modeling and Multi-Hop Reasoning}
        Graph Neural Networks propagate structural context across multi-hop relational paths. In statutory interpretation, normative conflicts often emerge indirectly across chains of administrative definitions and delegated authorities instead of direct single-clause collisions~\cite{yang2021legalgnn}. Through iterative neighborhood aggregation (message passing), the vector $\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}$ of node $v$ at layer $l$ updates from adjacent 1-hop neighbors. This inductive propagation incorporates multi-hop links so models can trace whether a local clause clashes with a superior statutory command through intermediate agencies or delegated powers~\cite{ma2025gnn}.

        Graph representations also encode institutional links absent from isolated sentence inspection. Philippine local ordinances operate inside an administrative web of national departments and municipal codes. Relational node embeddings reflect those shared regulatory domains so algorithms can connect related statutory clusters that keyword search misses~\cite{ma2025gnn}.

        \subsubsection{Structural Vulnerabilities in Unstructured Archives}
        Despite their theoretical appeal, practical deployment of Graph Neural Networks depends strictly on the completeness and accuracy of the underlying graph~\cite{humphreys2020populating}. GNNs rely on explicit cross-references to construct edges, yet Philippine local ordinances frequently contradict superior Republic Acts without citing them in text. Whenever an enactment omits an explicit citation, deterministic graph traversal has no path connecting the conflicting provisions and leaves message passing blind to the collision.

        Missing citations are compounded by OCR errors and unlinked archival PDFs. Platforms like Davao City's LISSP store enactments as discrete PDF files instead of linked structured records~\cite{conchas2025digital}. Automated edge extractors run on noisy OCR transcripts can generate spurious or misattributed citations~\cite{lopresti2008optical}, which then propagate across GNN layers and distort node embeddings. Without costly manual edge curation, pure graph reasoning proves brittle on noisy local archives.

    \subsection{Abstract Meaning Representation}
    GNNs model macro links across distinct laws, whereas Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) parses semantic roles inside individual sentences. AMR encodes a sentence as a rooted directed acyclic graph whose nodes represent concepts and whose edges mark semantic roles~\cite{banarescu2013abstract}. In legal text, AMR abstracts over surface grammar shifts such as active versus passive voice~\cite{banarescu2013abstract, trong2018an}. Both ``The City Mayor shall enforce the penalty'' and ``The penalty shall be enforced by the City Mayor'' yield the same logical graph identifying the City Mayor as agent (\texttt{:ARG0}) and the penalty as patient (\texttt{:ARG1}).

        \subsubsection{Semantic Graph Representations and Role Labeling}
        A key strength of AMR in statutory analysis is isolating predicate-argument structure alongside deontic modalities. Statutory rules depend on obligations, permissions, and prohibitions to assign civic duties~\cite{agotnes2007on, humphreys2020populating}, and semantic parsing surveys highlight how graph role labeling captures modality alongside event roles~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. Standard dependency parsers often blend modal auxiliaries into generic verb groups, whereas AMR isolates the regulated actor and modal restriction explicitly~\cite{trong2018an}. This explicit role graph helps clarify when a local ordinance permits an activity forbidden by Congress.

        \subsubsection{Sentence Boundary Constraints and Parser Degradation}
        Operational constraints limit AMR when applied to Philippine local legislation, starting with its single-sentence parsing scope~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. Preemption conflicts frequently span multiple sections, such as an operative restriction in one paragraph modified by an exception several paragraphs later, instead of fitting inside a single sentence. Because standard AMR parsers operate sentence by sentence without cross-sentence coreference resolution~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}, they miss multi-paragraph provisos and leave logical blind spots.

        AMR parsers are also sensitive to complex statutory nesting and scanning errors. Philippine codes such as Republic Act No.~7160~\cite{philgov1991ra7160} routinely stack deep conditional provisos inside long sentences~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}. Empirical evaluations by Trong and Le~\cite{trong2018an} show that AMR parsers degrade on dense civil code prose and preprocessing noise, matching sequence-to-sequence sensitivity results reported by van Noord and Bos~\cite{vannoord2017the}. Without heavy domain-specific parser retraining, AMR does not reliably handle noisy local ordinances.

    \subsection{Statutory Citation Networks and Legislative Lineages}
    Beyond sentence role graphs, legal informatics models positive law through statutory citation networks and legislative lineages. Statutory codes form an evolving network of cross-references and amendments linking parent acts with local rules, formalized by standards such as Akoma Ntoso~\cite{vitali2007towards} and LegalRuleML~\cite{palmirani2021legalruleml, athan2013legalruleml}. Mapping statutory links into directed networks reveals how authority flows downward and how amendments modify earlier sections over time.

        \subsubsection{Statutory Network Deployments and Operational Granularity}
        Large-scale legal networks have been built across multiple public jurisdictions. The European Union's European Legislation Identifier (ELI) in EUR-Lex links Directives and Regulations via amendment and transposition edges, while United States initiatives compile tens of thousands of federal statutory sections for structural modeling~\cite{bommarito2025kl3m, katz_2023_legal_nlp}. Benchmark resources such as LeCNet~\cite{harde2025lecnet} and Chinese Legal Knowledge Graphs~\cite{li2024construction} similarly connect statutory sections and judicial rulings to support retrieval and legal reasoning.

        Choosing graph node granularity controls both accuracy and tractability. Document-level graphs merge hundreds of distinct sections into one node, hiding which exact fee schedule or permit clause an ordinance modified. Conversely, token-level graphs create tens of millions of sparse edges that add noise without isolating statutory duties.

        Legal knowledge engineering commonly resolves this trade-off at the numbered provision or section level~\cite{humphreys2020populating}, complementing case-level citation networks such as LeCNet~\cite{harde2025lecnet}. Each numbered statutory section binds a regulated actor and deontic operator with its substantive rule and qualifying provisos. Modeling graphs at section granularity preserves rule boundaries cleanly for downstream retrieval.

        \subsubsection{Relational Edge Typologies and Extraction Workflows}
        Constructing a statutory graph from raw legal text relies on staged normalization and citation parsing. Standardization scripts first collapse surface aliases (such as ``Republic Act No. 7160'' and ``The Local Government Code of 1991'') onto a single canonical node identifier. Regular expression parsers then extract explicit statutory citations to instantiate directed vertical edges linking ordinances to parent national acts.

        Amendatory parsing adds horizontal lineage edges governed by \textit{lex posterior derogat legi priori}. Philippine drafting manuals standardize amendment wording such as ``is hereby amended to read as follows'' and ``is hereby repealed in its entirety.'' Automated extractors convert those formulas into directed modification edges annotated with enactment dates, separating active provisions from superseded rules.

        Unlike single-relation graphs, statutory networks act as heterogeneous multi-relational graphs $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$. Distinct edge types $r \in \mathcal{R}$ separate structural containment from substantive citations and express repeals, keeping traversal routines from treating repealed historical rules as binding mandates.

        \subsubsection{Neuro-Symbolic and Graph-Augmented Transformers}
        Topological networks capture formal statutory relationships, yet pure graph representations miss preemption conflicts across unlinked enactments. In local legislative practice, draft ordinances frequently collide with superior national statutes without citing them directly, relying on localized phrasing instead. Deterministic graph traversal fails under these conditions because no citation edge connects the conflicting provisions.

        To overcome this disconnection, contemporary legal NLP literature investigates neuro-symbolic and graph-augmented architectures that combine topological knowledge graphs with dense transformer models~\cite{li2024construction, ma2025gnn}. In these hybrid frameworks, the statutory knowledge graph provides structural priors, such as mapping governing legislative hierarchies and removing obsolete repealed enactments. Concurrently, deep transformer encoders evaluate continuous cross-attention across candidate text spans, resolving semantic equivalence and deontic collisions even without formal citation edges. Symbolic graph topology thus enforces structural authority and legislative lineage, whereas continuous neural inference resolves unstructured textual contradictions.

        Within this neuro-symbolic interface, legal informatics literature explores two primary paradigms for integrating knowledge graph structure with transformer cross-encoders: \textit{Prompt-Conditioned Metadata Conditioning} and \textit{Graph-Guided Decision Thresholding}. Under prompt-conditioned metadata injection, structured graph node properties (such as jurisdictional level, governing macro-domain, and formal statutory titles) are serialized into standardized textual prefixes prepended directly to the input sequence (e.g., \texttt{[JURISDICTION: NATIONAL | DOMAIN: ...]} and \texttt{[JURISDICTION: LOCAL | DOMAIN: ...]}). Studies in legal document classification and NLI demonstrate that prepending structured metadata provides explicit contextual framing and allows transformer self-attention layers to condition token representations on institutional hierarchies without custom model architectures~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue, li2024construction}. At the same time, work on legal prompt variants~\cite{alshehri2026cross} observes that prompt structure and prefix tokens influence how attention and classification heads behave. When cross-encoders encounter prefix schemas that differ from their primary training data distribution, attention heads can become sensitive to contrasting lexical tokens in the prompt. This sensitivity reflects a recognized methodological trade-off between contextual guidance and input representation consistency.

        In parallel, an alternative design paradigm decouples graph-based legal representation~\cite{ma2025gnn} from post-hoc decision calibration based on Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) threshold analysis~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. Building on those two components, this study evaluates a topological calibration scheme in which the original verbatim statutory sequence is preserved for neural inference while shortest-path geodesic distance $d_G(u, v)$ and authority hierarchy $H(u, v)$ modulate the post-softmax decision boundary $\tau^*(u, v)$. In statutory knowledge graphs containing typed authority and domain links, topological proximity between two provisions captures institutional closeness: enactments connected by direct citation links ($d_G = 1$) or shared macro-domains ($d_G = 2$) operate within a common regulatory framework. Separating linguistic cross-attention from relational graph traversal lets the cross-encoder evaluate pure textual semantics while graph topology adapts decision sensitivity according to structural distance. Together, these two paradigms test whether knowledge graph structure is captured more effectively within the model's linguistic input representation or along its operational decision boundary.

\section{Deep Learning and Transformers}
The application of NLP in the legal domain has shifted from rule-based systems and basic word-matching toward Deep Learning~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. Legacy algorithms treat legal texts as simple character strings, which frequently fail to capture the dense and interconnected nature of statutory drafting. Deep Learning, conversely, uses multi-layer artificial neural networks to map out the semantic meaning of legal language. This allows systems to process complex syntax and perform the deductive reasoning required to spot conflicts. This section examines the Transformer architecture, covering its underlying mechanics and computational constraints in auditing unstructured local archives.

    \subsection{Transformers and Self-Attention}
    A major breakthrough was the Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} in 2017. Prior neural sequence models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, processed text sequentially from left to right, one token at a time. This sequential dependency created substantial computational delays during training and suffered from vanishing gradient dynamics over long sequences. As sequence length expanded, contextual information from earlier tokens attenuated, which hindered the model's capacity to resolve dependencies across multi-clause statutory provisions. 
    
    Transformers eliminate this sequential constraint through the \textit{Self-Attention} mechanism. Instead of reading words sequentially, self-attention evaluates all tokens in an input sequence in parallel. For every word in a provision, the mechanism computes attention weights measuring how much every other word in the text contributes to its contextual meaning, regardless of distance in the sentence. For example, in a provision stating that ``Any licensee who operates without a valid health clearance shall be penalized,'' self-attention connects ``licensee'' to ``penalized'' across the intervening relative clause in one step. Because it processes data in parallel, the Transformer trains substantially faster on modern hardware and enabled foundational language models. Modern iterations introduce refinements like Disentangled Attention to represent token content and relative positional offsets separately, alongside Windowed Attention for long legal documents. Generative Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at text summarization, yet they remain prone to ``hallucinations'' (fabricating non-existent statutory citations or misinterpreting cross-references)~\cite{deroy2023how}. To keep legal auditing reliable, a system must classify whether two statutory provisions stand in Entailment, Neutrality, or Contradiction instead of probabilistically generating text.

    \subsection{Transformer Challenges}
    Despite their architectural versatility, deploying transformer models within the computational constraints of Philippine LGUs introduces substantial operational hurdles. The foremost engineering constraint arises from quadratic memory scaling and context length limits. The standard self-attention mechanism scales quadratically ($O(n^2)$) with input sequence length $n$. Because computing pairwise attention scores requires constructing an explicit $n \times n$ attention matrix across all query-key token pairs, memory consumption scales quadratically with sequence length, such that doubling sequence length quadruples the memory footprint required for attention computation. Consequently, standard foundational architectures like BERT are constrained to a 512-token context window~\cite{devlin2019bert}, whereas Philippine ordinances frequently span multiple pages. Practitioners employ sliding-window techniques to segment documents into overlapping spans~\cite{anand2020explainable, seo2025sliding}, yet this segmentation severs the semantic dependency between general regulatory mandates and distant provisos~\cite{savelka2021discovering}. Sequence truncation therefore creates structural blind spots that can lead to false conflict classifications.

    A secondary challenge involves morphological noise and domain adaptation limits. Pretrained transformers require large corpora of clean text, and Western legal models like Legal-BERT perform well on foreign benchmarks~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}, yet no dedicated foundation model exists for Philippine local legislation. Applying general tokenizers to local archives containing scanning noise and code-switched expressions triggers heavy token fragmentation. Domain-specific terms split into sub-tokens that degrade contextual comprehension.

    Finally, edge deployment is restricted by local government hardware realities. Executing deep logical inference across thousands of document pairs demands substantial GPU compute and memory bandwidth~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Most LGUs operate standard desktop workstations with modest hardware specifications, so deploying unoptimized neural models leads to system latency spikes or memory exhaustion. These resource limits motivate decoupled, multi-stage architectures suited for local inference.

    \subsection{Suitability of Transformers}
    Notwithstanding these hardware limitations, Transformer models remain well suited for Semantic Conflict Detection in local ordinances. General-domain language models typically struggle with legal text because common words frequently take on specialized legal definitions. For instance, ``party'' refers to a litigant instead of a celebration, and ``suit'' signifies a legal action instead of clothing. Greco et al.~\cite{Greco2023Bringing} emphasize that this high degree of polysemy is why domain-specific models are required. Older static systems assign one fixed meaning per word~\cite{Greco2023Bringing}, whereas a Transformer dynamically adjusts its reading of a word from the surrounding sentence context~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2019large} demonstrated that this contextual awareness allows a model to distinguish between amending a law and violating it; keyword searches miss this distinction.

    The transition to Deep Learning moves legal review past earlier computational barriers. Boolean keyword searches fail when legislative drafting vocabulary evolves across decades. Conversely, manually encoding unlinked Republic Acts and local ordinances into deterministic graph rules creates an unsustainable administrative load. As Li et al.~\cite{li2024construction} observed, hand-crafting legal knowledge graphs does not scale for unstructured local records. Transformers offer a practical alternative by mapping semantic relationships in raw legal text without manual graph construction.

    

\section{Single-Stage and Multi-Stage Architectures}
In mainstream NLP, end-to-end single-stage neural architectures serve as the conventional baseline for document classification and question answering. These systems funnel raw input sequences directly into a single neural network that outputs the final classification without intermediate filtering. However, in legal informatics, detecting statutory conflicts demands fine-grained deductive accuracy across large archives that a monolithic model cannot easily sustain. Forcing one model to scan tens of thousands of statutory records and evaluate deontic logic simultaneously introduces steep mathematical and operational trade-offs.

    \subsection{Single-Stage Monolithic Pipelines}
    The appeal of a single-stage neural architecture is its conceptual simplicity: a single model handles the entire input-output mapping without requiring intermediate data handoffs. In standard NLP tasks operating over small document collections, this approach minimizes engineering complexity and eliminates pipeline handoff delays. However, deploying a monolithic model to audit draft local legislation against expansive national repositories introduces major operational barriers. Such designs inevitably sacrifice either candidate recall or deductive precision.

    One common monolithic approach involves prompting generative LLMs to directly declare legal preemption. Autoregressive language models act as probabilistic token predictors instead of deterministic logical discriminators, which leaves them vulnerable to legal hallucinations where non-existent statutory citations are fabricated~\cite{linna2026challenges}. Furthermore, their large parameter counts make offline deployment on standard local government hardware infeasible without heavy quantization. Aggressive quantization degrades deductive reasoning fidelity in specialized statutory domains~\cite{dettmers2022llmint8}. 
    
    Conversely, relying exclusively on single-stage bi-encoders introduces semantic compression limits. In a bi-encoder (or dual-tower) architecture, the search query and the candidate statute are processed through separate neural networks into independent vector coordinates. Because the two texts never interact directly during encoding, the model can pre-compute and store embeddings for tens of thousands of statutes in advance, which enables fast nearest-neighbor search across large archives~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert, chen2024bgem3}. However, compressing an entire multi-clause provision into a single fixed-dimensional vector discards granular syntactic nuance~\cite{karpukhin2020dense, thakur2021beir}. Bi-encoders optimize for broad topical similarity instead of logical consistency, causing them to conflate permission and prohibition provisions that share identical topical vocabulary~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    Furthermore, executing a single-stage cross-encoder across an entire statutory repository is computationally prohibitive. In contrast to bi-encoders, a cross-encoder concatenates the draft query and the candidate statute into one sequence so every token in the draft attends directly to every token in the statute. Joint comparison achieves high logical sensitivity, yet full cross-attention scales quadratically ($O(n^2)$) with input length. Comparing a proposed local draft against tens of thousands of national statutes directly would require hundreds of thousands of forward passes, exhausting workstation memory and causing high system latency~\cite{beltagy2020longformer}. Breaking texts into sliding windows across thousands of enactments also severs inter-clause dependencies. This computational ceiling confirms that isolating a compact candidate shortlist via coarse retrieval is necessary prior to fine-grained sequence classification.

    \subsection{Two-Stage Retrieve-then-Entail Architectures}
    To resolve the mathematical and computational limitations of monolithic architectures, contemporary legal informatics adopts decoupled two-stage systems. In the retrieve-then-entail paradigm, the workflow splits into an initial coarse IR phase scanning the entire corpus for a top-$k$ candidate shortlist, followed by a fine NLI phase executing cross-attention on the candidate pairs~\cite{koreeda2021contract, goebel2026an}. Prior investigations in legal contract verification and statutory entailment show that this decoupled design substantially contracts the search space of pairwise comparisons~\cite{koreeda2021contract}. Restricting cross-attention to shortlisted pairs preserves inferential accuracy while keeping runtime manageable.

    The primary engineering advantage of this decoupled architecture is its alignment with edge computing realities. By offloading broad corpus search to pre-computed vector indices that require minimal runtime memory, the computationally intensive $O(n^2)$ matrix operations of the cross-encoder are restricted exclusively to the top-$k$ candidate pairs. Khattab and Zaharia~\cite{khattab2020colbert} established that this decoupled structure enables neural language processing to execute locally on consumer-grade hardware without distributed GPU clusters. Furthermore, decoupling retrieval from inference provides a pragmatic resolution to the vector compression trade-off. The initial retrieval stage accepts the loss of granular syntactic nuance to maximize search throughput across extensive statutory archives. In contrast, the subsequent inference stage bypasses vector pooling entirely, performing joint token-level cross-attention over uncompressed raw text spans to achieve high-precision deontic reasoning~\cite{humeau2019poly}.

    \subsection{Comparative Architectural Trade-offs}
    Comparing single-stage and dual-stage architectures highlights a fundamental division of labor in statutory preemption analysis. A single-stage retriever maximizes search speed but remains blind to subtle modal negators, whereas a single-stage cross-encoder possesses high logical sensitivity but cannot scale beyond a few dozen provisions without memory failure. Chaining both mechanisms in a coarse-to-fine hierarchy combines the complementary strengths of each model family. Stage One absorbs corpus volume and context length, while Stage Two resolves logical nuance and morphological noise~\cite{louis2022a}.

    The empirical superiority of this decoupled design is reinforced by over a decade of findings from the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE). As Goebel et al.~\cite{goebel2026an} document, COLIEE divides legal reasoning into statutory information retrieval (Task~3) and statutory textual entailment (Task~4). Year after year, top-performing teams reject single-stage monoliths in favor of pipelined approaches~\cite{althammer2021dossiercoliee}. Early benchmarks in COLIEE~2021 established this separation, and modern iterations through COLIEE~2025~\cite{coliee2025proceedings} and COLIEE~2026~\cite{coliee2026proceedings} confirm that decoupled retrieval and inference remain the dominant paradigm. For statutory retrieval, hybrid dense-sparse systems achieve high candidate recall~\cite{ngo2026nowj}, while cross-encoder entailment systems surpass 95\% accuracy by separating fine reasoning from corpus retrieval~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. This sustained validation confirms that separating retrieval from entailment provides an established design pattern for statutory conflict detection.

    
\section{Information Retrieval}
In the coarse stage of a dual-pipeline system, IR filters relevant documents from large unstructured datasets based on a query, isolating candidate statutes from thousands of unrelated records before intensive logical verification begins. Candidate filtering directly limits the pairwise comparison load passed downstream.

    \subsection{Information Retrieval Challenges in Statutory Corpora}
    Applying information retrieval techniques to statutory collections introduces domain-specific hurdles that general-domain search engines are not engineered to handle. A primary challenge is lexical divergence and vocabulary mismatch across legislative eras~\cite{blair1985an}. Statutes governing the same civic activity may be enacted decades apart with outdated terminology or specialized administrative terms. When newly drafted local ordinances use modern regulatory expressions, exact keyword matching misses older governing statutes and creates blind spots in preemption review~\cite{opijnen2017on}.

    Furthermore, statutory corpora exhibit extreme length asymmetry and structural complexity that disrupt standard retrieval scoring. Statutory enactments frequently contain verbose, multi-clause provisions exceeding one thousand tokens, where general commands are qualified by distant provisos and statutory exceptions~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Standard passage retrievers struggle to balance term saturation across these extended provisions, frequently penalizing lengthy, comprehensive enactments. Compounding this structural challenge is administrative hierarchy noise. In national legal archives, subordinate issuances (such as executive orders and departmental circulars) outnumber primary legislative acts, frequently diluting candidate shortlists unless protected by statutory priority safeguards~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    \subsection{Relevance in Retrieval}
    To understand how computational systems extract documents, it is useful to examine the human cognitive process that defines relevance in practice. In cognitive science, relevance forms a dynamic relationship between information and a user's task. According to Relevance Theory, humans filter information by seeking cognitive benefit for minimal mental effort~\cite{sperber1996relevance}. When checking for conflicts, legal researchers evaluate the intent and scope of a rule against background statutory knowledge instead of counting repeated terms. A retrieved passage is therefore relevant only when it supplies new context altering the reviewer's legal assessment~\cite{saracevic2007relevance}.

    Beyond the cognitive baseline, the theoretical definition of relevance in legal information retrieval is anchored in the philosophical requirements of a functional legal system. According to the jurisprudence established by Lon Fuller in \textit{The Morality of Law}, a primary requirement for the internal morality of law is consistency; a system that issues contradictory rules fails to be a legal system at all~\cite{fuller1964the}. Within this philosophical framework, relevance is not simply about finding documents that talk about the same subject matter; it is about identifying provisions that share a ``normative overlap.'' This means that a retrieved document is relevant if it occupies the same jurisdictional or behavioral space as a draft local ordinance, potentially creating a situation where a citizen cannot obey one law without violating another. Grounding the retrieval stage in this quest for consistency treats relevance as the systematic identification of potential normative collisions and threats to the rule of law~\cite{fuller1964the}.

    However, traditional computational methods struggle to replicate this human judgment because they rely on a rigid mathematical definition of relevance. In deterministic systems, relevance is treated as a binary set intersection~\cite{blair1985an}. Given document $D$ and query $Q$, lexical relevance is evaluated as $R(D,Q) \in \{0,1\}$, where a passage scores $1$ only when it contains the exact character sequence queried~\cite{opijnen2017on}. Consequently, this rule groups documents strictly by shared surface vocabulary~\cite{blair1985an}. That mathematical simplicity enables fast database indexing, yet it strips away legal context.

    This stark discrepancy between superficial lexical matching and actual legal consequence represents a major stumbling block for legacy search architectures. In statutory law, two documents discussing the same topic might be lexically relevant to one another, but they are only \textit{legally} relevant if one provision actively modifies, permits, restricts, or preempts the other~\cite{opijnen2017on}. For instance, a retrieved document might use identical vocabulary but apply to a completely different jurisdiction or regulatory scope; this jurisdictional divergence renders it unhelpful for an ex-ante conflict check. Conversely, a document regulating the same civic behavior using different phrasing (such as substituting ``motor vehicle'' for ``automobile'') receives a relevance score of zero in a strict Boolean system~\cite{blair1985an}. Therefore, to audit local ordinances against superior law, statutory retrieval systems must move beyond binary keyword matching in favor of dense semantic representations capable of capturing jurisprudential relationships~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    To resolve these limitations, modern computational pipelines redefine relevance spatially over continuous vector coordinates instead of discrete string matching. Dense retrieval architectures apply Bi-Encoder models to project legal provisions into high-dimensional vector spaces~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}, scoring relevance via geometric proximity (such as cosine similarity)~\cite{kim2024legal}. When a proposed ordinance and a national statute govern the same civic activity using different terms, dense vectors map both provisions closely together and resolve vocabulary mismatch~\cite{wu2025aiir}. Representing relevance as a graded similarity score surfaces conceptually linked statutes that exact keyword indices overlook.

    \subsection{Long-Context Retrieval} 
    Relying on standard BERT-based architectures introduces an artificial constraint on legal analysis: the 512-token limit. Because standard Self-Attention mechanisms scale quadratically with sequence length, processing long texts requires heavy truncation. To work around this limit, researchers like Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} use Sliding Window techniques to segment documents into overlapping chunks. However, applying this to Philippine ordinances (which are historically wordy and often bury exceptions deep within 1,000-token provisions) disrupts statutory interpretation. It severs the substantive semantic link between a primary rule and its distant exception.

    Consequently, the model operates with a logical blind spot. Beltagy et al.~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} note that this causes logical errors, where a rule may be flagged as an absolute prohibition simply because its exempting clause was pushed into a separate processing window. Furthermore, retrieval systems must balance Sparse Retrieval (such as BM25) for exact keyword matching against Dense Retrieval for capturing conceptual meaning without direct word overlap. Combining both paradigms helps cover exact statutory citations alongside paraphrased local provisions.

    Resolving this fragmentation means moving beyond short sliding-window chunking where possible. The architectural solution requires an initial retrieval mechanism capable of native long-context retention. Recent Transformer models incorporate advanced positional encoding, such as Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), alongside sparse or optimized attention matrices. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} demonstrated that these upgrades extend context windows up to 8,192 tokens without triggering memory failures. Deploying this class of architecture allows the system to read entire local ordinances as cohesive semantic units, preserving the law's internal structure and preventing sliding-window truncation errors.

    \subsection{Retrieval and Fusion Strategies}
    \label{sec:retrieval_paradigms_fusion}
    In computational legal informatics, isolating candidate enactments from extensive statutory collections requires understanding the theoretical foundations and complementary trade-offs of diverse retrieval strategies. Findings from the COLIEE benchmarks and legislative consistency literature identify five methodological dimensions governing coarse statutory search. The foundational retrieval strategies encompass sparse probabilistic scoring and dense semantic embeddings, formalized in Equation~\eqref{eq:okapi_bm25} and Equation~\eqref{eq:dense_cosine_sim}.

    \noindent\textit{Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Relevance Scoring Function:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q) = \sum_{q \in Q} \text{IDF}(q) \cdot \frac{f(q, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(q, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
        \label{eq:okapi_bm25}
        \eqcaption{Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Relevance Scoring Function}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $f(q, D)$ denotes term frequency of query token $q$ in document $D$, $|D|/\text{avgdl}$ normalizes for provision length, and standard default parameters $k_1 = 1.5$ and $b = 0.75$ lie within the empirical ranges ($1.2 < k_1 < 2$, $0.5 < b < 0.8$) reported in Robertson and Zaragoza's probabilistic relevance framework~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}. 
    
    The Okapi BM25 formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:okapi_bm25} balances three core retrieval properties within a unified probabilistic relevance framework. First, Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{IDF}(q)$) assigns higher numerical weight to rare discriminative terms (such as specific statutory numbers and penal sanctions) while suppressing ubiquitous statutory stopwords that appear across all enactments. Second, Term Frequency Saturation ($k_1 = 1.5$) enforces sub-linear term weighting to capture diminishing marginal relevance, so repeating a keyword multiple times does not disproportionately inflate relevance over concise provisions. Third, Document Length Normalization ($b = 0.75$) penalizes exceptionally verbose provisions so lengthy multi-page legislative digests do not dominate search rankings simply through word volume. In statutory retrieval competitions such as COLIEE Task~3, BM25 is an essential baseline (as demonstrated by Team AIIR~\cite{wu2025aiir}) because legislative drafting relies on exact statutory citations and verbatim penal amounts, even though exact lexical matching still suffers from vocabulary mismatch when local ordinances paraphrase statutory commands~\cite{blair1985an, manning2008introduction}.

    \noindent\textit{Dense Semantic Cosine Proximity Relevance Function:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{dense}}(D, Q) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_Q \cdot \mathbf{e}_D}{\|\mathbf{e}_Q\| \|\mathbf{e}_D\|}
        \label{eq:dense_cosine_sim}
        \eqcaption{Dense Semantic Cosine Proximity Relevance Function}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathbf{e}_Q$ and $\mathbf{e}_D$ represent pooled contextual embeddings generated by dense bi-encoders such as all-MiniLM-L6-v2~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Instead of counting literal token overlap, Equation~\eqref{eq:dense_cosine_sim} measures semantic cosine proximity to retrieve governing statutes even when local drafts use different phrasing.

    In heterogeneous statutory databases spanning tens of thousands of enactments, researchers frequently investigate topic classification or domain partitioning to reduce search space complexity. However, empirical literature highlights the risk of hard categorical filtering in legal domains. Foundational statutory instruments, such as the Philippine Local Government Code of 1991 (RA~7160) or comprehensive penal codes, function as omnibus legislation governing local administration, public safety, and environmental sanitation simultaneously. Hard filtering by one predicted regulatory category discards the governing omnibus code whenever a query touches an adjacent policy area, causing sharp drops in candidate recall. In contrast, information retrieval theory establishes soft domain priors~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}, which apply a multiplicative score boost to candidate documents matching the predicted topic or latent semantic cluster while preserving access to multi-domain statutory codes.

    Because lexical and semantic retrievers exhibit orthogonal failure modes, leading computational law frameworks combine both streams through hybrid fusion. In score-level linear combination, popularized by Team JNLP in the COLIEE competitions~\cite{goebel2026an}, weighted interpolation combines normalized sparse and dense scores according to $S_{\text{hybrid}}(D, Q) = \alpha \hat{S}_{\text{dense}}(D, Q) + (1 - \alpha) \hat{S}_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q)$, where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ balances semantic and lexical bias. Because BM25 produces unbounded positive relevance scores while dense bi-encoders output bounded cosine similarities within $[-1, 1]$, score-level linear combination requires sensitive calibration and min-max score normalization across heterogeneous query lengths. To eliminate score normalization artifacts, Cormack et al.~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal} formulated Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), defined as $\text{RRF}(D) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} (k_{\text{rrf}} + \text{rank}_m(D))^{-1}$, where $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{BM25}, \text{Dense}\}$ and $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$. RRF abstracts away disparate score distributions by operating strictly on ordinal rank positions, where the smoothing constant $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$ stabilizes reciprocal scoring and rewards candidates with high concordance across both lexical matching and dense embeddings.

    Finally, statutory retrieval systems must respect constitutional hierarchies of authority. Anchored in the doctrine of legislative supremacy and the hierarchy of legal norms~\cite{kelsen1967pure, philgov1991ra7160}, statutory systems operate under strict formal ordering. In national digital archives, subordinate administrative issuances (such as executive orders and administrative circulars) frequently constitute a large share of indexed records. Because administrative issuances share broad governmental vocabulary with local drafts, unconstrained retrieval often allows routine circulars to crowd out superior national statutes. Literature in legal informatics therefore emphasizes structural safeguards that prioritize primary legislative acts (Republic Acts) over subordinate executive issuances. Implementing a hierarchical statutory priority safeguard shields governing statutes from administrative noise; this prevents dilution of candidate shortlists and improves Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).

    \subsection{Retrieval Benchmarks}
    Selecting effective retrieval architectures from current open-weight models requires objective empirical benchmarking. In contemporary legal information retrieval, candidate models are evaluated against standardized leaderboards that test zero-shot transfer across specialized statutory text. The Benchmarking Information Retrieval (BEIR) suite evaluates out-of-domain generalization across specialized retrieval datasets~\cite{thakur2021beir}. Because Philippine local legislation lacks large annotated training corpora, candidate retrievers must transfer effectively without fine-tuning. Strong zero-shot accuracy on BEIR indicates that an encoder maps unfamiliar legal terminology into structured vector spaces reliably~\cite{thakur2021beir}.

    Expanding on BEIR, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) establishes an international standard for ranking embedding systems across more than one hundred datasets and diverse linguistic tasks~\cite{muennighoff2023mteb}. In information retrieval literature, candidate architectures are frequently filtered using MTEB's retrieval and clustering leaderboards. Models excelling on these benchmarks demonstrate an empirical capacity to cluster conceptually related doctrines and retrieve relevant provisions based on continuous semantic proximity. Complementing general embedding benchmarks, the annual COLIEE Task~3 evaluation provides a specialized testbed for statute law retrieval~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}. Systems evaluated on Task~3 must extract precise governing articles from civil code collections in response to legal queries; success requires balancing broad candidate recall with computational throughput~\cite{wu2025aiir, goebel2026an}.

    \subsection{Candidate Retrieval Models}
    \label{sec:ir_candidate_models}
    Surveying leading open-weight architectures documented across recent retrieval literature and standardized benchmarks reveals eight prominent bi-encoder models relevant to statutory candidate discovery. Among distilled edge baselines, Reimers and Gurevych~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} established the Sentence-BERT framework, mapping text into fixed-dimensional dense vector spaces optimized for cosine proximity. Building on this framework, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 compresses a 6-layer BERT architecture down to 22.7~million parameters through knowledge distillation to produce 384-dimensional dense vectors. Despite its compact footprint, it retains strong semantic clustering capabilities with low-latency inference on consumer CPUs. Complementing this baseline, Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2024bge} introduced the BGE family (including the compact 384-dimensional small variant, released as bge-small-en-v1.5 at 33.4M parameters); this architecture optimizes contrastive representation learning for high rank sensitivity on MTEB. In public-sector edge deployment, these distilled bi-encoders provide high-throughput candidate filtering without requiring dedicated GPU accelerators.

    Scaling bi-encoder capacity to standard 110M-parameter base dimensions enables more expressive semantic representations across general retrieval benchmarks. Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2024bge} developed the 768-dimensional base BGE encoder (released as bge-base-en-v1.5 at 109M parameters), using retrofitted contrastive pretraining and instruction-tuned query prefixes to excel across multi-domain retrieval leaderboards. Similarly, Wang et al.~\cite{wang2022text} introduced the E5 embedding family (including e5-base-v2 at 109M parameters), trained on approximately 1.3~billion weakly-supervised text pairs via contrastive objectives; E5 demonstrated strong generalization on unseen technical and legal search tasks. Furthermore, Song et al.~\cite{song2020mpnet} developed MPNet (110M parameters in its base configuration), combining masked language modeling with permuted language pretraining to eliminate positional dependence, which is the foundational architecture for all-mpnet-base-v2 (109M parameters).

    Beyond general-domain models, legal-domain adapted encoders capture specialized statutory taxonomies. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2020legal} demonstrated that pretraining bidirectional encoders on specialized legal repositories enables dense representations to capture statutory taxonomy and regulatory jargon that general-domain embedders conflate. Mean-pooled sentence embeddings derived from legal-bert-base-uncased (110M parameters) map statutory provisions into vector spaces that reflect legislative hierarchy and administrative codification instead of colloquial word associations. Unlike general web text encoders, legal-domain pretraining exposes the tokenizer and transformer weights to statutory citations and formal procedural clauses. This domain-specific pretraining provides a structured baseline to evaluate whether legal pre-training improves candidate provision ranking over general embeddings.

    To overcome the quadratic attention complexity of traditional 512-token encoders, modern architectures incorporate native long-context and multilingual designs. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} developed ModernBERT-base (149M parameters), which integrates FlashAttention-2, Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), GeGLU activations, and sequence unpadding to natively process up to 8,192 tokens. In statutory retrieval, this allows direct encoding of verbose multi-paragraph enactments without destructive sliding-window chunking. Expanding to the multilingual domain, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024bgem3} introduced bge-m3 (568M parameters, 1,024 dimensions), supporting 8,192-token contexts alongside dense and multi-vector scoring modes. In statutory consistency checking, these multi-functionality representations provide resilience across code-switched local records and bilingual administrative issuances.

\section{Natural Language Processing}
NLP provides computational techniques to analyze and represent text across linguistic levels~\cite{liddy2001natural}. As Jurafsky and Martin~\cite{jurafsky2024speech} explain, it bridges human language and machine logic so algorithms can parse unstructured prose. In law, NLP shifts document auditing from slow manual reviews toward scalable screening. However, applying standard NLP to legislation reaches past grammar checking because systems must navigate complex sentence syntax and deontic terms such as obligations, permissions, and prohibitions.

Early Legal NLP relied on Symbolic Artificial Intelligence (AI)~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}, using hand-built ontologies and strict Boolean logic to extract legal information. Because local legislative phrasing shifts over decades, exact-match rules proved too brittle. Modern NLP maps provisions into high-dimensional vector spaces instead of literal keyword lists so downstream models can evaluate logical relations directly across unstructured enactments.

    \subsection{NLP Challenges}
    Deploying NLP frameworks against local archives quickly exposes the mechanical limits of standard Language Models. A primary hurdle is the Global North bias in pre-training data and legal benchmarking. As Ranathunga et al.~\cite{ranathunga2022some} point out, most Legal NLP benchmarks, like LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} and CaseHOLD, train strictly on digitally native Western legal texts. These models degrade on Philippine LGU archives containing OCR errors, American colonial phrasing, and local code-switched expressions. Rust et al.~\cite{rust2021how} showed that standard WordPiece tokenizers split unrecognized words into meaningless sub-tokens that obscure intent. Handling localized noise requires large Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) vocabularies that absorb surface spelling variants alongside Disentangled Attention architectures separating word content from corrupted positions~\cite{he2023debertav3}.

    Beyond geographic and morphological bias, traditional NLP methods suffer from structural limits in lexical similarity. Early NLP systems identify topical overlap via surface token intersection using TF-IDF or shallow word embeddings. Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli} explain that pure lexical similarity cannot detect legal contradictions because surface overlap ignores deontic logic and negation. An ordinance permitting an activity and a statute forbidding it share high word overlap yet express opposing commands. Detecting normative conflicts therefore requires moving from lexical proximity to directional logical inference.

    \subsection{From NLP to NLI}
    Historically, checking legislative consistency relied on Symbolic rules and Statistical NLP classifiers using TF-IDF or N-grams~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. Those models depended on hand-engineered features; statistical weights allowed probabilistic decisions, yet the classifiers struggled with legal lexical ambiguity.
    
    The field largely transitioned to Neural NLP models in the mid-2010s~\cite{quevedo2024legal}. Neural networks operate end-to-end, mapping raw text through hidden layers without manual feature crafting. Still, models can overfit narrow training distributions, making domain pre-training important for rigorous legal classification.

    Within neural NLP, NLI was formalized under the Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE) challenges by Dagan et al.~\cite{dagan2006pascal} and provides a standard paradigm for testing algorithmic deduction. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024improving} define NLI as classifying the directional relationship between a governing \textit{Premise} and a candidate \textit{Hypothesis}. In statutory preemption auditing, the Premise maps to the superior national statute and the Hypothesis maps to the proposed city ordinance clause. Because statutory compliance is directional instead of symmetric, reversing Premise and Hypothesis alters the governing legal obligation.
    
    Under this formulation, the neural network classifies each pair into one of three mutually exclusive logical states. In an Entailment relationship, the draft ordinance directly carries out or compliantly adheres to the governing national statute. In a Contradiction relationship, the draft ordinance collides with the national statute (for example, permitting what national law prohibits or exceeding statutory penalty ceilings under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine). In a Neutral relationship, the two provisions govern non-overlapping matters without conflict. This directional formulation corrects statistical overlap assumptions~\cite{yang2024improving}, and modern encoders capture coreference and modality missed by rule-based engines~\cite{lehong2023a}.

    \subsection{NLI Benchmarks}
    Selecting effective Cross-Encoder architectures for inference requires benchmarks evaluating pre-trained deductive logic. Wang et al. introduced the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE)~\cite{wang2019glue} and SuperGLUE~\cite{wang2020superglue} as standard benchmarks testing RTE and coreference resolution. High scores on SuperGLUE establish baseline deductive ability, yet they do not guarantee success in law because statutory vocabulary shifts word meanings.

    To evaluate specialized legal logic, Legal NLP relies on LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}, which tests how well encoders navigate legalese where everyday terms carry narrow statutory meanings. Strong LexGLUE performance indicates foundational readiness on legislative drafting and separates general language fluency from statutory precision.

    Complementing domain-specific suites, Hugging Face NLI leaderboards test candidate models on adversarial entailment tasks pooling MultiNLI (MNLI), FEVER, Adversarial NLI (ANLI R1--R3)~\cite{nie2020anli}, and WANLI~\cite{liu2022wanli}. Benchmarking across these suites measures resilience against lexical heuristics and negation traps; pre-aligned DeBERTa-v3 checkpoints from Laurer et al.~\cite{laurer2024less} lead these leaderboards and provide strong foundations for statutory conflict detection. Within computational law specifically, COLIEE Task~4 is the main international statutory entailment benchmark~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}, testing whether architectures resolve civil code conditions and deontic exceptions reliably.

    \subsection{Candidate NLI Models}
    \label{sec:nli_candidate_models}
    Evaluating deontic relationships across statutory provisions demands specialized inductive biases across candidate sequence encoders. General language models trained only on web prose frequently misread specialized statutory terminology. Researchers address this divergence through continuous pretraining on legal repositories: Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2020legal} introduced LEGAL-BERT on legislation and court judgments, and Henderson et al.~\cite{henderson2022pileoflaw} scaled pretraining with PoL-BERT across a 256~GB statutory and regulatory corpus. Paul et al.~\cite{paul2022inlegalbert} (InLegalBERT), Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2021when} (CaseLaw-BERT), and Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2021lawformer} explore common-law and long-document legal transfer as testbeds for domain protection against misinterpretation.

    A major vulnerability of unadapted sequence encoders on deontic tasks is \textit{affirmative entailment bias}, defaulting toward Entailment or Neutral when encountering subtle prohibitions~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. Because true contradictions are rare in everyday corpora, masked baselines like bert-base-uncased~\cite{devlin2019bert} learn a strong prior favoring textual agreement. Initializing classifiers from warm multi-task NLI checkpoints breaks that bias~\cite{laurer2024less}, while Disentangled Attention in DeBERTa-v3~\cite{he2023debertav3} and Replaced Token Detection in ELECTRA~\cite{clark2020electra} sharpen sensitivity to subtle deontic shifts.

    Verbose preambles and nested penal qualifications further test the 512-token limit of standard transformers~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Sparse attention designs such as Longformer~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} and BigBird~\cite{zaheer2020bigbird} lowered memory complexity, yet sparse attention patterns can weaken links between distant provisos and main commands. Modern architectures such as ModernBERT~\cite{warner2024modernbert} (FlashAttention-2, RoPE, GeGLU, and sequence unpadding up to 8,192 tokens) and Nomic-BERT~\cite{nussbaum2024nomic} encode long statutory clauses jointly without sparse approximation artifacts.

    Unlike bi-encoders that pool texts independently, cross-encoders run full bidirectional self-attention across the concatenated pair simultaneously so tokens interact directly across texts. Multilingual cross-encoders such as BGE-Reranker-v2-m3~\cite{xiao2024bge} score long statutory passages in COLIEE, and Nogueira and Cho~\cite{nogueira2020passage} showed with MonoBERT that retrieved passages can be scored efficiently in parallel hardware mini-batches.

    Serving cross-encoders on live documents involves sequence-padding trade-offs. Static padding fills tensors to a global 256- or 512-token cap~\cite{devlin2019bert}, wasting quadratic $O(L^2)$ attention operations on zero tokens when clauses are short. Dynamic sequence padding caps tensor dimensions at the longest clause in each mini-batch without altering hidden representations, and cross-section mini-batch flattening amortizes CPU overhead.

    Philippine archives also introduce multilingual complexity, mixing legal English with Latin maxims and occasional English-Tagalog code-switching. Conneau et al.~\cite{conneau2020xlmr} introduced XLM-RoBERTa across 100 languages, He et al.~\cite{he2023debertav3} added Disentangled Attention to mDeBERTa-v3, and Cruz and Cheng~\cite{cruz2020tagalog} trained roberta-tagalog-base on Philippine text so evaluations can measure whether localized phrasing degrades statutory comprehension.

    Edge LGU workstations without GPU clusters also motivate lightweight distilled encoders. DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} cuts parameters by 40\% while running 60\% faster, ALBERT~\cite{lan2020albert} shares weights across layers down to 12M base parameters, and SBERT~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} pairs all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (22.7M parameters) for fast CPU execution. Benchmarking distilled models quantifies the accuracy cost traded for lower CPU latency.

\section{Ground Truth}
In supervised machine learning, a Ground Truth dataset provides verified target labels used to compute training loss and evaluate model generalization~\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. Expert-adjudicated labels keep encoders from exploiting surface wording shortcuts that fail on authentic statutory logic.

In evaluation, held-out test subsets remain strictly unseen during tuning and checkpoint selection so reported metrics (including precision, recall, and Macro $F_1$) reflect real generalization to unseen drafts~\cite{bowman2015snli}.

    \subsection{Ground Truth in NLI}
    In NLI, datasets classify the directional logical relationship between a Premise and a Hypothesis as Entailment, Contradiction, or Neutral~\cite{yang2024improving}.

    Large general benchmarks such as Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) rely on crowdsourced annotators writing short everyday sentences~\cite{bowman2015snli}. Crowdsourcing yields hundreds of thousands of cheap pairs, yet embeds unintended lexical artifacts~\cite{gururangan2018annotation}. Models trained strictly on short everyday pairs struggle on multi-layered statutory reasoning.

    \subsection{Annotation and Reliability Metrics}
    Converting statutory text into an NLI dataset requires strict annotation rules rooted in deontic logic so raters apply legal hierarchy instead of subjective judgment. Following Agotnes et al.~\cite{agotnes2007on} and Li et al.~\cite{li2012detecting}, guidelines map clauses into obligations, permissions, and prohibitions: a ``Contradiction'' holds when an ordinance mandates what a national law forbids, forbids what it permits, or imposes unauthorized fees and conditions on nationally licensed activities. Permissible local fees within devolved authority receive ``Neutral,'' anchoring labels in statutory preemption.

        \subsubsection{Inter-Rater Reliability Metrics}
        Evaluating annotation consistency relies on Inter-Rater Reliability (IRR) metrics~\cite{mchugh2012interrater} instead of raw percentage agreement. Percentage agreement ignores chance alignment ($p_e \approx 33.3\%$ across three classes) and inflates reliability estimates. Subtracting random agreement yields Cohen's Kappa~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} for two judges and Fleiss' Kappa~\cite{fleiss1971measuring} for multi-rater panels, defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:cohens_kappa} and Equation~\eqref{eq:fleiss_kappa}.

    \noindent\textit{Cohen's Pairwise Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}
        \label{eq:cohens_kappa}
        \eqcaption{Cohen's Pairwise Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $p_o$ represents observed agreement between two raters and $p_e$ represents chance-expected agreement. Equation~\eqref{eq:cohens_kappa} measures agreement between two judges.

    \noindent\textit{Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa = \frac{\bar{P} - \bar{P_e}}{1 - \bar{P_e}}
        \label{eq:fleiss_kappa}
        \eqcaption{Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\bar{P}$ denotes mean observed pairwise agreement and $\bar{P_e}$ denotes expected chance agreement across the rater pool. Equation~\eqref{eq:fleiss_kappa} generalizes chance correction to panels of three or more judges.

    Table~\ref{tab:kappa_interpretation} maps numeric $\kappa$ intervals onto six qualitative reliability bands from Poor to Almost perfect agreement. Higher $\kappa$ separates shared expert judgment from random chance. In legal annotation studies, reaching $\kappa \ge 0.61$ (Substantial agreement) indicates that labeled pairs reflect shared legal reasoning instead of subjective bias.

    Objective tasks routinely exceed $\kappa = 0.90$, whereas textual inference tasks show lower ceiling agreement. On SNLI, Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli} reported an overall Fleiss' Kappa of $0.70$ across validated pairs ($0.77$ Contradiction, $0.72$ Entailment, $0.60$ Neutral). Reasonable raters occasionally differ on borderline clauses, making substantial agreement a realistic benchmark ceiling for statutory NLI.

        \subsubsection{Sub-Threshold Reliability}
        When $\kappa$ falls below $0.61$, Klebanov and Beigman~\cite{klebanov2009from} show that split votes often reflect ambiguity in ``hard'' items instead of rater carelessness. In large corpora such as the disaster-tweet dataset by Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing}, omitting disputed items preserves label cleanliness without losing statistical power~\cite{klebanov2009from}.
        
        However, omitting disputed items from a small legal dataset sharply shrinks sample size and Minimum Detectable Effect. Borderline clauses also capture the exact statutory friction an inference engine must flag. Instead of deleting disputed pairs, Gao et al.~\cite{gao2022hierarchical} demonstrate a hierarchical adjudication workflow where a senior expert resolves split votes under governing doctrine.

    \subsection{Ground Truth in Legal NLP}
    In Legal NLI, matching short isolated sentences breaks down because statutory sections embed nested provisos and multi-paragraph exceptions. Proving entailment or contradiction frequently requires reading across an entire section.

    Recent legal datasets mirror realistic document structures. COLIEE pairs civil code statutes with bar examination queries~\cite{rabelo2022coliee}, while Koreeda and Manning's ContractNLI~\cite{koreeda2021contract} requires locating supporting spans inside full non-disclosure agreements instead of pre-trimmed sentence pairs.

    Legal benchmarks also depend on trained legal annotators instead of general crowdsourcing. CaseHOLD~\cite{zheng2021when} and LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} rely on law students and legal professionals so labels reflect actual jurisprudence instead of amateur guesses.

    These hurdles intensify for local legislation. National codes follow uniform templates, whereas local ordinance texts vary widely in structure and phrasing across jurisdictions~\cite{campos2026nlp} and lack curated NLP benchmarks~\cite{campos2026citilink}. Evaluating models on Philippine city ordinances therefore requires building a specialized expert-annotated dataset.

    Selecting premise granularity also governs benchmark signal quality. Evaluating the local draft hypothesis as a multi-sentence block preserves qualifying context, whereas keeping the national statutory premise tight to its governing rule limits extraneous clutter. Holzenberger et al.~\cite{holzenberger2020a} observe that statutory inference matches focused rules against factual scenarios while raw full articles exceed encoder token limits, keeping cross-attention centered on the conflict point.

    Expert scarcity further bounds dataset size because trained lawyers have limited review capacity~\cite{zhong2020how}. Instead of massive crowdsourced collections, researchers construct curated few-shot Gold Standards: Gao et al.~\cite{gao2021making} show dependable fine-tuning with $K = 16$ or $32$ examples per class when validation guards against overfitting.

    \subsection{Multi-Sentence and Cross-Document Limits}
    Traditional NLI compares one sentence against one sentence, which breaks down in law due to multi-paragraph intra-document exceptions and cross-document dependencies across separate acts.

    Legal frameworks handle intra-document caveats through Asymmetric Span-Level Pairing. Yin et al.~\cite{yin2021docnli} and Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} keep the governing statute focused as the premise while passing the full multi-sentence draft section as the hypothesis so cross-attention sees distant exceptions together.

    Cross-document multi-hop reasoning across multiple independent laws remains computationally expensive for standard workstations. Recent multi-hop pipelines either mine entity graphs~\cite{lu2023multi} or invoke large generative LLMs in systems like HopWeaver~\cite{shen2025hopweaver}, both exceeding edge LGU hardware limits. Practical edge screening therefore stays scoped to single-premise asymmetric pairs.

    \subsection{Dataset Partitions and Grouped Leakage Prevention}
    Evaluating models on datasets under 1,000 pairs with naive random $k$-fold cross-validation introduces strong optimistic bias when shared premises leak across folds~\cite{vabalas2019machine}. Legal benchmarks avoid leakage using static Train/Validation/Test partitions (e.g., 70/15/15) alongside premise-grouped cross-validation (GroupKFold), isolating every pair sharing a statutory premise inside a single fold.

    A separate validation split also prevents memorization on small training sets. Goodfellow et al.~\cite{goodfellow2016deep} establish early stopping on validation loss, and Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2021revisiting} confirm that grouping by source document blocks clause overlap across splits.

    \subsection{Minimum Detectable Effect}
    Static splits on smaller benchmarks still hold statistical power depending on effect size. Card et al.~\cite{card2020with} show that statistical power ($1 - \beta = 0.80$ at $\alpha = 0.05$) depends on the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) instead of sample count alone. Detecting 1\%--2\% differences requires $N > 10^4$ items, whereas large domain-adaptation shifts ($\delta > 0.80$) reach adequate power on test partitions around 50 pairs.

    In CaseHOLD, Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2021when} report a 7.2\% $F_1$ gain (12\% relative gain) over unadapted BERT baselines. Because unadapted models perform poorly on specialized statutory wording, holdout sets near 50 pairs capture genuine fine-tuning gains reliably.

\section{Explainable AI}
In legal informatics, opaque ``black box'' predictions cannot support legislative review regardless of accuracy. Conflict alerts must expose verifiable textual evidence (\textit{ratio decidendi}) so legal staff can verify and defend recommendations~\cite{doshi2017towards}. Explainable AI (XAI) in legal NLP therefore favors faithful mathematical traces over ungrounded generated text.

    \subsection{Intrinsic Explainability}
    Explainability methods split into Post-Hoc Surrogate Models and Intrinsic Mechanisms. Surrogates such as LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016why} and SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} treat models as black boxes and run thousands of input perturbations to fit linear approximations, adding high latency and approximation errors~\cite{rudin2019stop}. Intrinsic methods extract native attention weights and hidden activations directly during inference.
    
    Attention interpretability remains debated: Jain and Wallace~\cite{jain2019attention} noted that raw attention does not always mirror gradient attributions, whereas Wiegreffe and Pinter~\cite{wiegreffe2019attention} showed under diagnostic testing that attention distributions can faithfully reflect model prioritization. In statutory preemption checks, surfacing peak cross-attention weights anchors human inspection inside opposing modal phrases without surrogate runtime overhead.
    
    Confidence calibration complements attention saliency. Unadapted transformers often emit overconfident probabilities on out-of-domain text~\cite{guo2017calibration}. Post-hoc temperature scaling divides pre-softmax logits by a learned positive scalar $\tau > 0$, lowering Expected Calibration Error (ECE) without altering prediction rankings.

    \subsection{Edge Deployment Feasibility}
    Post-hoc Shapley approximations require many perturbed forward passes per pair and strain edge workstations~\cite{kumar2020problems, wang2020edge}. Extracting intrinsic attention maps and calibrated logits during the single inference pass keeps explanation cost aligned with commodity LGU hardware limits.

    \subsection{Explanation Quality Metrics}
    Evaluating explanations separates Plausibility (whether rationales sound convincing to readers) from Faithfulness (whether attributions reflect true internal model weights)~\cite{jacovi2020towards, doshi2017towards}. Jacovi and Goldberg~\cite{jacovi2020towards} show that optimizing plausibility alone risks hiding model failures, making faithfulness mandatory in statutory screening.
    
    This trade-off limits generative LLM explainers. Auto-regressive summaries read smoothly yet suffer from legal hallucinations and fabricated citations~\cite{linna2026challenges, katz2024gpt4}. Decoupled text generation fails faithfulness checks~\cite{jacovi2020towards}, favoring extractive attention heatmaps over verbatim statutory text instead.

    \subsection{Explainability in Multi-Stage Pipelines}
    Multi-stage legal pipelines place interpretability inside Stage~2 inference instead of Stage~1 coarse retrieval~\cite{anand2020explainable}. Computing token saliency over hundreds of thousands of Stage~1 corpus passages adds heavy latency for minimal gain, whereas Stage~2 cross-attention over shortlisted candidate pairs maps directly to human verification~\cite{vig2019bertviz}.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
Across recent COLIEE benchmarks~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}, the Retrieve-then-Entail architecture is the standard framework for automated statutory screening, combining scalable retrieval with cross-encoder entailment verification. This study synthesizes Team AIIR Lab's dense statutory retrieval~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}, Team JNLP's multi-stage pruning and rank ensembling~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, and Team KIS's ModernBERT entailment classification~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}.

        \subsubsection{Pre-Retrieval}
        Pre-retrieval prunes clearly unrelated statutes using distance metrics and gradient-boosted candidate filtering before passage re-ranking~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, countering high class imbalance without discarding valid candidate provisions.

        \subsubsection{Model Fine-Tuning}
        Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA adapt re-ranking encoders on imbalanced statutory pairs with manageable memory overhead, pairing asymmetric loss weighting with negative sampling to keep positive conflict signals sharp.

        \subsubsection{Result Ensemble}
        Weighted ensemble aggregation combines calibrated lexical and neural rankings via hyperparameter optimization (such as Optuna) to stabilize candidate shortlists for downstream verification.

    \subsection{Theoretical Synthesis}
Integrating AIIR retrieval~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}, JNLP multi-stage pruning~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, and KIS cross-encoding~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert} forms a non-generative retrieve-then-entail pipeline suited to local statutory screening.
