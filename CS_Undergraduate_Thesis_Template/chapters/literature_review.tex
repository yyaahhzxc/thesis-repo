\chapter{Literature Review}
    
\section{Semantic Conflict Detection}
In jurisprudence and legal philosophy, the fundamental requirement for a functional legal order is internal consistency, where positive enactments provide unambiguous guidance without subjecting citizens to contradictory commands~\cite{fuller1964the}. In legal theory and formal deontic logic, this phenomenon is strictly defined as a \textit{normative conflict}---a structural clash between legal norms wherein an action is simultaneously commanded, prohibited, or permitted across distinct enactments, violating the principle of non-contradiction~\cite{li2012detecting, agotnes2007on}. Specifically, normative conflict occurs when two or more valid laws impose incompatible legal duties on the same subject under the same conditions, as when one law mandates an act that another law forbids~\cite{agotnes2007on, italiani2026clash}. When statutory systems generate conflicting obligations, citizens cannot conform their conduct to the law without incurring liability under an opposing mandate, destabilizing legal predictability and public trust. Consequently, establishing systematic mechanisms to identify and resolve normative collisions represents a vital safeguard for the integrity of any rule-of-law framework.

However, in real-world legislative governance and local government archives, statutory enactments do not exist as pre-formalized formulas in symbolic logic. Rather, statutes are drafted and promulgated as unstructured, multi-generational natural language texts characterized by high syntactic variance, legal jargon, and implicit exceptions~\cite{katz2024gpt4}. Consequently, from a computational perspective, this research formulates and operationalizes the challenge as \textit{Semantic Conflict Detection}. By translating abstract deontic clashes into a computable natural language processing problem, semantic conflict detection leverages continuous vector representation spaces and cross-encoder Natural Language Inference (NLI) to identify directional contradictions between local drafts and governing statutory provisions without relying on brittle, hand-crafted legal ontologies~\cite{shao2021bert, italiani2026clash}. As Ruhl and Katz~\cite{ruhl2015measuring} established, the volume of positive law expands far faster than human auditing capacity, making automated semantic auditing an operational necessity for modern legislative bodies.

   \subsection{Unstructured Legal Corpora}
   Managing unstructured legal text—text not stored in structured databases—represents a fundamental maintenance challenge for statutory systems. Practical automated auditing requires addressing rapid textual accumulation alongside the linguistic complexity of legislative drafting. These structural realities drive the research transition toward neural retrieval and inference tools for statutory verification.
   
        \subsubsection{Legal Inflation}
        The term \textit{legal inflation} describes the continuous, exponential expansion in the sheer volume and interconnected complexity of statutory enactments over time. Katz et al.~\cite{katz2024gpt4} observe that as modern societies grow more complex, legislative bodies enact new laws at a pace that vastly outpaces the capacity of human legal staff to manually review them for consistency. Paralleling the concept of technical debt in software engineering, this rapid accumulation generates \textit{regulatory debt}---the compounding burden of uncoordinated, obsolete, or overlapping legal enactments that accumulate when new rules are adopted without systematically reconciling them against pre-existing legislation~\cite{katz2024gpt4}. In local government administration, this phenomenon is particularly acute because municipal ordinances are rarely compiled into centralized, cross-referenced statutory codes~\cite{dias2022state}. Dias et al.~\cite{dias2022state} emphasize that transforming historical municipal enactments into machine-readable data presents severe engineering hurdles because local records are scattered across disparate administrative formats, ranging from formal codified enactments to transitory executive orders.

        Consequently, the statutory search space for identifying normative conflicts resembles a fragmented, multi-generational archive rather than a structured relational database. Zhong et al.~\cite{zhong2020how} characterize this challenge as a dynamic consistency problem, wherein governing facts and regulatory obligations continually shift across successive council terms. Because local statutory data lacks centralized indexing, municipal agencies are forced into a reactive posture, discovering statutory preemption defects only after an invalid measure is challenged in court. This systemic backlog motivates the transition in legal informatics from brittle, rule-based systems toward neural language models capable of learning semantic representations directly from unstructured text~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Such automated systems are required to process extensive legislative corpora that exceed the working memory and analytical throughput of human researchers.

        \subsubsection{Limits of Manual Review}
        In contemporary legal informatics, digitizing physical documents into electronic archives no longer represents the primary operational bottleneck; rather, the central hurdle is auditing whether newly introduced provisions logically align with governing positive law. As legislative throughput accelerates, manual review workflows become overwhelmed by the cognitive burden of cross-referencing multi-volume statutory digests. A primary challenge for human reviewers is distinguishing between beneficial regulatory overlap—where multiple enactments legitimately address adjacent dimensions of a public policy issue—and destructive statutory conflict, where an enactment inadvertently contradicts a superior mandate. Recent surveys confirm that conventional keyword-based search platforms consistently fail to capture these subtle legal distinctions~\cite{ariai2025natural}. Consequently, latent statutory contradictions frequently remain undetected throughout the drafting lifecycle, shifting the burden of conflict discovery from legislative staff during pre-enactment review to judicial authorities during adversarial litigation~\cite{tsai2025can}.

        \subsubsection{Semantic Ambiguity}
        Traditional search systems rely on exact character matching, operating on the premise that legal concepts can be retrieved by searching for specific vocabulary strings. However, natural legal language exhibits pervasive \textit{semantic ambiguity} driven by two primary linguistic phenomena: \textit{synonymy} (where different words express the identical legal concept, such as \textit{prohibited} versus \textit{unlawful}) and \textit{polysemy} (where the same word assumes drastically different meanings depending on context, such as a \textit{party} referring to a litigant rather than a social gathering)~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue, Greco2023Bringing}. In statutory drafting, normative directives are governed by deontic modal verbs---such as \textit{shall}, \textit{must}, or \textit{may}---whose binding legal force cannot be determined by simple string matching. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} demonstrate that this syntactic ambiguity causes exact-match techniques to fail when processing complex legal tasks. For example, a local ordinance regulating ``motor vehicles'' and a national statute governing ``two-wheeled conveyances'' might address the exact same civic activity, yet a Boolean query for ``automobile'' will fail to retrieve either provision due to vocabulary divergence.

        Furthermore, legal consistency frequently depends on implied normative boundaries rather than explicit textual declarations. Rigano et al.~\cite{rigano2018using} establish that identifying these subtle contradictions requires computational systems that evaluate logical relationships and contextual semantics rather than surface character overlap. Transformer-based language models address this limitation by projecting text sequences into high-dimensional vector spaces that capture deep contextual semantics~\cite{zheng2021when}. However, deploying neural architectures introduces an explainability trade-off. As Sovrano et al.~\cite{Sovrano2021Making} argue, while deep neural models achieve high classification accuracy, their uninspected internal activations function as opaque black boxes that fail to articulate the underlying legal reasoning behind a flagged conflict. In administrative and legislative settings, articulating the verifiable \textit{ratio decidendi} behind an alert is just as vital as the detection itself~\cite{savelka2021discovering}.

    \subsection{Local Conflict Detection}
    While international legal informatics benchmarks such as LexGLUE advance automated conflict detection across Western legal corpora, corresponding research within post-colonial, developing jurisdictions like the Philippines remains in its nascent stages~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Since the passage of the Local Government Code of 1991, Philippine local government units have enacted thousands of local ordinances that remain largely unindexed in machine-readable formats~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. Although theoretical algorithms for conflict detection are established in mainstream artificial intelligence literature, their empirical application to localized Philippine statutory text remains largely unaddressed.

        \subsubsection{Philippine Legal NLP}
        Within domestic computer science, research applying Natural Language Processing to Philippine law has historically focused on administrative classification and text summarization rather than deductive logical reasoning. Early investigations utilized basic supervised classifiers to forecast appellate outcomes in Supreme Court decisions~\cite{virtucio2018predicting} or applied extractive algorithms to condense extensive jurisprudence~\cite{sagum2023philippine}. More recently, domestic legal informatics has explored transformer-based dense embedding architectures, which map text sequences into continuous mathematical vectors where geometric distance corresponds to topical relatedness~\cite{roque2024legal}. While dense embeddings significantly outperform traditional keyword searches in retrieving domain-specific statutes (such as tax regulations), they remain fundamentally correlational: they identify that two provisions address the same subject, but lack the deductive reasoning layers required to determine whether the two provisions contradict each other.

        Analyzing these research patterns reveals a pronounced structural gap in local literature. The Philippine NLP community has successfully automated the categorization, summarization, and retrieval of national statutory text, while also proving that inference models can resolve semantic contradictions in local dialects for general-domain tasks such as online fact-checking~\cite{virtucio2018predicting, sagum2023philippine, roque2024legal, nunez2026pomi}. However, extending these advanced inference models to detect normative conflicts within the decentralized, multi-generational ordinances of a Highly Urbanized City like Davao remains entirely unexplored.

        \subsubsection{Logic and Conflict Research}
        Within Philippine computational linguistics, the modeling techniques required for automated conflict detection—specifically Natural Language Inference (NLI)—have been successfully deployed across adjacent domains, most notably in detecting misinformation and rumors in low-resource vernacular text. Nuñez et al.~\cite{nunez2026pomi} demonstrated that transformer architectures effectively resolve directional logical contradictions across code-switched Tagalog and administrative Taglish.

        However, when domestic researchers analyze local government records, methodology routinely regresses to legacy information retrieval frameworks. Document indexing across Philippine local government units relies predominantly on Boolean keyword matching and rigid rule-based heuristics. As Naguio et al.~\cite{naguio2024hybrid} demonstrate, these traditional systems suffer from severe \textit{lexical fragility}: they fail when synonymous terms are used across enactments (such as ``firecrackers'' versus ``pyrotechnic devices'') and collapse entirely when encountering optical character recognition (OCR) noise in historical scans. Relying on manual rules to isolate statutory contradictions across thousands of localized city ordinances is mathematically unviable for human researchers and rule-based software alike.

    
        \subsubsection{Research Gaps}
        While the Philippine AI community has successfully automated the search and summarization of national laws, a significant gap remains in using deep logic models to audit local ordinances. Research indicates this gap persists because local legislation is substantially more challenging for automated systems to process than national statutory compilations.

        The primary structural barrier to local legislative auditing is the critical dataset gap, characterized by the complete absence of curated ground-truth datasets for Philippine local enactments. Unlike national statutes that benefit from centralized repositories, local ordinances represent low-resource textual environments~\cite{ranathunga2022some}. Standard legal NLP models are trained on massive Western corpora like LexGLUE, but there are no equivalent standardized benchmarks providing annotated pairs of conflicting local ordinances~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis, nrcp2024review}. Without a standardized benchmark to measure progress, local legal informatics research has historically struggled to move beyond superficial keyword search to deductive logical reasoning~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}.

        Compounding the lack of training data is a pronounced computational resource gap within Local Government Units (LGUs). Deductive reasoning models, particularly deep cross-encoders, demand substantial compute and memory resources that local government offices typically do not possess. Studies on Philippine public administration indicate that local digital adoption remains constrained by limited funding, scarce machine learning personnel, and legacy office desktop hardware~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}. In many local legislative councils, fewer than twenty percent of legislative personnel have received formal ICT training, with most offices still relying on paper records and basic text editors rather than structured databases~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}. This lack of digital infrastructure makes it difficult to justify complex models that would be too computationally demanding to execute on actual LGU workstations~\cite{huangetal2025integrated}.

        At the textual level, local legislative conflict detection faces a complex sociolinguistic gap. Local ordinances exhibit higher linguistic heterogeneity than national statutes because local drafting frequently blends formal legal English with regional dialects like Cebuano and administrative Taglish~\cite{dreisbach2021language}. Standard NLP tokenizers designed for monolingual English often break down when encountering localized legal idioms or archaic Spanish loanwords embedded in Philippine local codes~\cite{maxilom2008semantic}. While recent studies confirm that neural inference models can parse regional dialects in informal social media text, extending this capacity to dense, multi-generational local enactments requires specialized domain adaptation that remains largely unexplored in local literature~\cite{citygov2025lissp, Greco2023Bringing}.

        Finally, the slow adoption of computational consistency checking is driven by an institutional trust gap across the legal profession. As emphasized by Supreme Court Senior Associate Justice Marvic Leonen, the legal community remains cautious regarding artificial intelligence because conventional deep learning models function as opaque black boxes that cannot articulate their underlying reasoning~\cite{leonen2024keynote}. Legal researchers and legislative counsel legitimately fear that automated tools could introduce subtle statutory errors, leading to the enactment of void legislation~\cite{conchas2025digital}. Consequently, academic research has historically favored low-risk document indexing and retrieval systems, leaving deep semantic conflict detection unaddressed in local governance~\cite{ashley2017artificial}.

\section{Philippine Legislative Domain}
The Philippine legal system is a unitary state. This means that Local Government Units (LGUs) only have powers that are given or delegated to them by the national government \cite{philgov1991ra7160}. This setup creates a strict top-down hierarchy that limits how much freedom cities like Davao City have when making laws. Any computer system built to check these laws must understand this specific legal environment. This section analyzes the statutory and structural conditions governing Philippine local law. First, it details the constitutional and statutory hierarchy subordinating local ordinances beneath national laws. Second, it traces the historical and stylistic roots of Philippine legal syntax. Third, it examines the parliamentary mechanics of the Three Readings legislative procedure during plenary sessions and the challenge of bilingual translation. Fourth, it contrasts ex-ante legislative auditing with ex-post judicial review, and finally reviews existing consistency checking methods to demonstrate the necessity of automated semantic auditing.

    \subsection{Hierarchical Constraints}
    The legal system in the Philippines depends on the fundamental rule that national laws always override local enactments. To understand the legal constraints governing local legislative drafting, automated conflict detection systems must reflect the structural principles that subordinate local ordinances beneath national statutes.

    The Philippine legal system operates under a unitary constitutional framework, establishing a rigid hierarchy where national laws strictly control subordinate enactments~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. At the apex stands the 1987 Constitution, followed by national statutes enacted by Congress, international treaties, presidential executive issuances, and local ordinances. Under this constitutional structure, no Local Government Unit can enact measures that contradict an Act of Congress. The doctrine of national preemption establishes an absolute boundary: whenever the national legislature enacts comprehensive statutory standards governing a given regulatory domain, local governments are legally precluded from enacting conflicting or diluting local rules~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    The primary organic charter governing local lawmaking is the Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No.~7160). Section~16 of the Code, known as the General Welfare Clause, delegates police powers to local legislative councils to enact ordinances promoting the health, safety, and general prosperity of their constituents, provided such enactments remain strictly within statutory boundaries~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. Because Davao City is classified as a Highly Urbanized City (HUC), it operates autonomously from provincial administrative supervision, meaning its ordinances are not reviewed by a provincial board. This administrative independence places the entire burden of ex-ante consistency auditing directly upon the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod before measures proceed to final enactment~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    The definitive judicial test governing the validity of local legislation is established by the Supreme Court in the landmark ruling \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corporation, Inc.}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}. To withstand judicial scrutiny, an ordinance must satisfy six foundational requirements: it must not contravene the constitution or any statute, must not be unfair or oppressive, must not be partial or discriminatory, must not prohibit but may regulate trade, must be general and consistent with public policy, and must not be unreasonable. Because local legislative bodies possess purely delegated authority, local councils cannot prohibit activities that national legislation has expressly authorized or regulated~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. This principle of statutory subordination forms the primary legal benchmark against which automated conflict detection systems must evaluate candidate local drafts.

    When a local ordinance impermissibly enters an area governed by national legislation, Philippine courts consistently apply the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine to declare the local measure invalid. In \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corporation, Inc.}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, the City Council of Cagayan de Oro enacted ordinances prohibiting casino operations to protect public morals. The Supreme Court struck down the local enactments as \textit{ultra vires}, ruling that a local council cannot prohibit commercial gambling activities that a national statute expressly authorized through the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR) charter. Similar preemption disputes have directly affected Davao City legislation. In \textit{Mosqueda v. Pilipino Banana Growers \& Exporters Association, Inc.}~\cite{scp2016mosqueda}, the Supreme Court invalidated Davao City Ordinance No.~0309-07, which imposed a local ban on aerial pesticide spraying across agricultural plantations, holding that the ordinance exceeded local police powers and collided with the regulatory authority granted to the national Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority under Presidential Decree No.~1144. In \textit{City of Manila v. Laguio, Jr.}~\cite{scp2005manilavlaguio}, the Court likewise struck down an ordinance prohibiting motels and massage parlors, holding that local police powers cannot arbitrarily prohibit lawful commercial activities under the guise of zoning regulation.

    In contrast to vertical statutory preemption, local lawmaking also involves horizontal consistency, where newly proposed ordinances must align with existing, fellow local enactments. When drafting local legislation, the Sangguniang Panlungsod routinely uses the Three-Reading legislative process, particularly the committee debates and line-by-line amendments during the Second Reading, to harmonize new proposals with older local enactments~\cite{spdc2022ra00122}. Unlike vertical conflicts that represent fatal legal defects, inconsistencies between a new draft and an older local ordinance frequently reflect intentional legislative updates, such as revising local tax rates or adjusting traffic routes. To manage these horizontal modifications systematically, legislative drafters rely on standardized legislative saving devices recommended by the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG)~\cite{dilg2022drafting}. Every formal ordinance incorporates an express or general repealing clause, declaring that all prior local enactments inconsistent with the newly approved measure are amended or repealed accordingly. Furthermore, ordinances feature a separability clause, acting as a structural legal safeguard ensuring that if any single section is declared invalid by judicial authorities, the unoffending sections remain in full legal force~\cite{dilg2022drafting}. However, because local legal researchers must review hundreds of active local enactments under strict legislative session schedules, subtle inconsistencies often go unnoticed during the Second Reading, allowing problematic clauses to proceed to final enactment.

    A fundamental design decision in legal informatics is delineating which positive legal instruments establish binding preemption standards. Under Article~VII, Section~17 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution and Book~III, Title~I, Chapter~2, Section~2 of the Administrative Code of 1987 (Executive Order No.~292)~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, Executive Orders (EOs) are acts of the President providing rules of a general or permanent character in implementation or execution of constitutional or statutory mandates. Unlike Supreme Court decisions—which function as \textit{ex-post} judicial dispute resolutions settling adversarial controversies under the doctrine of \textit{stare decisis} (Article~8, Civil Code)—Executive Orders operate as \textit{ex-ante}, forward-looking statutory instruments possessing the full force and effect of law. Furthermore, during both the Martial Law period (1972--1986) and the Revolutionary Government under the Freedom Constitution (Proclamation No.~3, 1986--1987), the President exercised direct legislative powers through Executive Orders and Presidential Decrees, creating substantive national enactments that remain operative law today. Modern Executive Orders frequently establish binding nationwide regulatory standards—such as Executive Order No.~26 (s.~2017) establishing nationwide public smoking prohibitions, or Executive Order No.~168 (s.~2014) creating the Inter-Agency Task Force for the Management of Emerging Infectious Diseases (IATF)—that legally preempt conflicting local legislation. Excluding Executive Orders would therefore omit critical nationwide regulatory mandates that local ordinances are legally bound to respect.

    \subsection{Historical and Stylistic Heritage}
    The structural complexity of Philippine legislative text stems directly from the nation's historical experience as a mixed legal jurisdiction~\cite{agpalo2009statutory, cruz2007political}. Philippine jurisprudence combines continental civil law traditions with Anglo-American statutory common law conventions. Understanding why Philippine statutes and local ordinances are drafted in their characteristic syntax requires examining how these colonial and post-colonial traditions fused over more than a century of codification.

    During more than three centuries of Spanish rule, the Philippine legal system was rooted firmly in the Roman-Germanic civil law tradition~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. This continental tradition emphasized systematic codification, deductive legal reasoning, and broad normative principles organized into structured books, titles, and chapters. Foundational enactments, such as the Spanish Civil Code of 1889 and the Penal Code of 1870, relied on generalized legal mandates rather than granular factual definitions. Furthermore, this civilian heritage embedded a rich vocabulary of Latin doctrinal maxims into Philippine jurisprudence, including \textit{in pari materia} (construing related laws together), \textit{generalia specialibus non derogant} (a general law does not override a specific one), and \textit{ultra vires} (acts beyond legal authority)~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. Modern Philippine codes retain this civilian structure, expecting adjudicators to derive specific legal duties from comprehensive normative commands.

    Following the Treaty of Paris and the establishment of the American Insular Government (1900--1935), American legal administrators introduced common law statutory drafting habits without fully displacing the civilian codification base~\cite{cruz2007political}. Enacted by the Philippine Commission and later the Philippine Legislature, early statutes introduced formal drafting conventions borrowed directly from United States federal and state legislation. These conventions included explicit statutory title preambles, exhaustive definitions sections, severability clauses, and general repealing provisions~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. Significantly, American administrators introduced the practice of drafting extensive conditional provisos (introduced by the traditional formula ``\textit{Provided, that...}'' and ``\textit{Provided, further, that...}''), designed to restrict, qualify, or create exceptions to preceding operative clauses within the same section. The Revised Administrative Code of 1917 (Act No.~2711) and the contemporary Administrative Code of 1987 (Executive Order No.~292) solidified this mixed tradition, confirming English as the controlling statutory language while maintaining civil law institutional categories~\cite{philgov1987eo292}.

    This dual heritage produces a legislative drafting style marked by high syntactic density and multi-clause complexity. Unlike standard conversational English, a single statutory provision frequently spans one hundred to two hundred words contained within a solitary grammatical sentence~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}. These sentences embed multiple dependent clauses, qualifying prepositional phrases, and recursive proviso exceptions. At the local level, legislative staff adopt this formal national drafting template while adding procedural boilerplate, resulting in verbose ordinances where the operative regulatory command is separated from its governing subject by dozens of intervening words~\cite{dilg2022drafting, spdc2022ra00122}. From an analytical and document processing perspective, this hybrid syntax presents substantial structural hurdles: archaic legal terms resist straightforward vocabulary normalization, long-distance dependencies obscure the core legal directive, and qualifying provisos invert the practical meaning of preceding clauses unless the text is examined in its full structural context.

    \subsection{Plenary Legislative Procedure and the Three Readings Framework}
    Besides the hierarchy of laws, the actual parliamentary mechanism through which laws are drafted, debated, and approved determines how statutory text evolves over time. In the Philippine legal architecture, positive law enactment is governed by a mandatory three-reading procedure conducted during formal plenary sessions of the legislative body. At the national level, Article~VI, Section~26, Paragraph~2 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution explicitly dictates that no bill passed by either the House of Representatives or the Senate shall become law unless it has passed three readings on separate days, with printed copies in final form distributed to members at least three days prior to passage~\cite{philconst1987, philhouse2022rules}. At the local government level, Sections~53 and 54 of the Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No.~7160)~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}, the Local Legislative Drafting Manual of the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG)~\cite{dilg2022drafting}, and the Internal Rules of Procedure of the 20th Sangguniang Panlungsod of Davao City (Resolution No.~001-22)~\cite{spdc2022ra00122} replicate this parliamentary doctrine. Under these statutory rules, every proposed municipal or city ordinance must undergo three distinct readings before the full assembly in plenary session. This parliamentary mechanism is designed to guarantee institutional transparency, deliberate consideration, and democratic accountability before any regulatory constraint attains the force of law.

    The legislative progression begins on the plenary floor with the First Reading. In both national congressional chambers and the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod, the First Reading is strictly ministerial and introductory: the presiding officer directs the Secretary to read solely the assigned measure number, title, and the name of the author or proponent before the assembled plenary body~\cite{philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. Crucially, parliamentary law forbids any substantive debate, interpellation, or floor amendment during First Reading. Instead, the presiding officer refers the proposed measure immediately to the appropriate standing committee—such as the Committee on Rules, Laws, and Ordinances—initiating the inter-session committee review phase. In this committee stage, technical working groups, legal counsel, and committee researchers examine the draft, hold public hearings, and prepare the formal committee report. Because local ordinance drafting is decentralized—with individual council members authoring distinct proposals rather than relying on a centralized drafting bureau~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}—drafting conventions and legal terminology vary widely. Legal researchers bear the heavy burden of manually cross-referencing proposed clauses against thousands of national statutes and prior city enactments under strict legislative session schedules. When staff lack automated semantic search tools, potential preemption defects and horizontal contradictions frequently survive committee scrutiny undetected.

    Following committee review, the proposed ordinance returns to the plenary floor for the Second Reading, which represents the primary substantive and deliberative phase of the legislative lifecycle. The Second Reading commences with a formal sponsorship speech delivered by the committee chair or measure proponent, summarizing the policy objectives and legal basis of the enactment. Immediately following sponsorship, the plenary assembly enters the Period of Interpellation and General Debate. During this period, colleagues interrogate the sponsor, probe statutory ambiguities, question fiscal or regulatory feasibility, and raise substantive legal objections regarding whether the measure contravenes superior national legislation. Once general debate is formally terminated, the body transitions into the Period of Amendments, where committee amendments and individual floor amendments are presented, debated, and voted upon section by section~\cite{philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. This period of amendments is the decisive procedural window where proposed clauses are actively reshaped, modified, or deleted to align with governing law.

    The Period of Interpellation and Amendments on Second Reading constitutes the exact procedural nexus where an automated semantic conflict detection system delivers its highest institutional utility. Because the Second Reading is the sole phase on the plenary floor where substantive textual alterations, statutory reconciliations, and corrective rewrites can be introduced, legal researchers and sponsors require granular, evidence-backed conflict diagnostics before and during floor deliberations. If a draft ordinance clause encroaches upon a national statutory domain—violating the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine by attempting to prohibit what national law permits, or by exceeding statutory penalty caps under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160—the automated system pinpoints the conflicting statutory premise, flags the contradictory deontic modalities, and provides an explainable legal audit trail. Equipping Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers with these automated findings allows them to draft precise, legally sound floor amendments during the Period of Amendments, rectifying latent preemption flaws before the measure advances past the amendable phase.

    After the assembly adopts all floor amendments and finalizes the draft on Second Reading, the measure is engrossed, printed in its final form, and scheduled for Third Reading in plenary session. In stark contrast to the dynamic debates of Second Reading, the Third Reading is procedurally rigid and unmodifiable. Under Article~VI, Section~26, Paragraph~2 of the 1987 Constitution and corresponding local Sanggunian rules, no amendment whatsoever is permitted upon the Third Reading of a bill or ordinance~\cite{philconst1987, philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. Plenary action is confined strictly to a nominal roll-call vote, where each member registers an affirmative, negative, or abstaining vote, accompanied by a brief oral explanation. This procedural finality creates severe institutional risk: if a statutory collision is not discovered and rectified prior to or during the Second Reading, the flawed provision is permanently locked into positive law upon Third Reading approval. The enacted ordinance becomes vulnerable to immediate judicial nullification under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine, administrative disputes before national executive departments, or citizen litigation. This structural irreversibility underscores why ex-ante computational conflict detection is essential during committee review and Second Reading preparation.

    Compounding the procedural complexities of the Three Readings is the statutory mandate requiring local legislation to be accessible across languages. Under Section~469 of the Local Government Code, the Secretary to the Sanggunian is mandated to translate approved ordinances into local dialects, such as Cebuano for Davao City~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122, dreisbach2021language}. However, this creates legal friction because the Administrative Code of 1987 establishes that the English statutory text strictly controls in cases of conflict~\cite{philgov1987eo292}. If an error occurs in regional translation, a citizen relying on the Cebuano text may inadvertently violate the authoritative English version, conflicting with the public notice doctrine in \textit{Tañada v. Tuvera}~\cite{supreme1985tanada}. Despite policy initiatives such as the \textit{Batas sa Sariling Wika Act}~\cite{reganit2025diokno}, subtle dialect differences often produce semantic drift where the exact legal obligation in English is distorted in regional vernacular text~\cite{maxilom2008semantic}. Consequently, consistency checking systems must operate primarily on the authoritative English statutory provisions while accounting for regional linguistic artifacts generated during local committee debates and drafting iterations.

    \subsection{Ex-Ante Legislative Review}
    The term ``ex-ante'' derives from Latin meaning ``before the event.'' In legislative informatics and administrative jurisprudence, it denotes the rigorous evaluation of a proposed law or local regulation prior to its official enactment~\cite{fried2015exanteexpost}. This prospective auditing process aims to identify and remediate potential statutory collisions, constitutional infirmities, and unintended regulatory burdens while the draft remains amenable to revision.

    In the Philippines, ex-ante regulatory review has achieved heightened institutional prominence following the enactment of Republic Act No.~11032, known as the Ease of Doing Business and Efficient Government Service Delivery Act of 2018~\cite{philippines2018ra11032}. Under this statutory framework, the Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA) alongside the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG) mandates that Local Government Units conduct a formal Regulatory Impact Assessment (RIA) for proposed regulations impacting commerce and public welfare~\cite{philippines2018ra11032}. The objective is to verify that proposed ordinances possess valid statutory authority and do not impose duplicative or contradictory requirements on citizens and businesses.

    The fundamental distinction between ex-ante and ex-post evaluation lies in timing, methodology, and legal impact across the hierarchy of legal instruments. Ex-ante evaluation focuses strictly on prospective, forward-looking legislative texts—such as draft local ordinances, pending congressional bills, and proposed executive issuances—prior to their passage into positive law. In contrast, ex-post evaluation operates retrospectively through judicial dispute resolution, encompassing adversarial litigation, appellate court rulings, and Supreme Court jurisprudence governed by \textit{stare decisis}. While ex-post judicial adjudication rectifies constitutional injuries after harm has occurred, it incurs substantial societal and litigation expenses, as repealing an active ordinance requires protracted court proceedings~\cite{gesteletal2011exanteevaluation}. Ex-ante auditing is therefore substantially more efficient, protecting local governments from administrative deadlock and judicial invalidation.

    Deploying automated computational tools for ex-ante conflict detection empowers local legislative researchers to identify normative collisions during the drafting stage. Because legislative bodies generate extensive statutory volumes, manual review frequently fails to catch subtle inconsistencies between new drafts and existing laws~\cite{katz2024gpt4}. By operating as an automated diagnostic triage tool, computational systems flag potential preemption conflicts while the measure remains in committee deliberation. This facilitates alignment with superior national statutes, mitigating the risk of enacting local measures that are legally void \textit{ab initio}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}.

    \subsection{Legislative Consistency Checking}
    Because local governments hold subordinate legislative authority within a decentralized drafting environment, systematic consistency checking represents an essential institutional safeguard. However, verifying that an ordinance does not contradict superior statutes requires far more than title matching; it demands deep comprehension of legal semantics and subtle jurisdictional boundaries. Current consistency checking methods remain encumbered by syntactic ambiguity and reliance on basic tracking platforms.

    Discourse analysis of Philippine statutory language reveals pervasive ambiguity in how normative keywords and modalities are deployed~\cite{scp1970villacortavbernardo}. In particular, the modal auxiliary ``shall'' introduces frequent interpretive friction, as it may denote an absolute command, an administrative directive, or a directory permission depending on statutory context. Empirical studies confirm that such syntactic ambiguities complicate legal interpretation even when enactments adhere to formal drafting guidelines~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}.

    Philippine jurisprudence recognizes that statutory conflicts rarely present themselves through explicit textual declarations~\cite{cruz2007political}. A local ordinance virtually never declares an overt intent to contradict an Act of Congress; rather, conflicts typically arise as implied preemption. This occurs when a national statute occupies a regulatory field, and a local council introduces unauthorized preconditions or local fees that restrict activities already licensed by the state~\cite{scp1970villacortavbernardo}. In such cases, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the local measure impermissibly modifies national law under the guise of police power regulation. For manual reviewers and keyword-based search tools, detecting implied preemption is exceptionally difficult because the conflicting enactments employ disparate vocabularies, necessitating deep semantic analysis to uncover the contradictory regulatory effect.

    In the local context, the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod processes extensive legislative volume, passing nearly 400 ordinances and over 2,000 resolutions within a single three-year council term~\cite{patumbon2025landmark}. To manage this throughput, the city deploys administrative platforms: the Resolution Ordinance Tracking System (ROTS) manages document routing and metadata, while the Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP) enables council members to access digitized enactments~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. However, while these digital systems streamline document logistics, substantive consistency auditing across provisions remains entirely manual. Furthermore, archival scans frequently contain optical character recognition (OCR) noise, causing keyword search queries to miss critical governing provisions~\cite{lopresti2008optical}.

\section{Digital Legislative Archives}
Building a functional Semantic Conflict Detection system requires access to comprehensive and machine-readable legal corpora. In the Philippines, the digital landscape of legislative data is highly fragmented, split between national statutory repositories and localized city archives. This structural division presents significant technical hurdles for statutory data ingestion. Before deep logical inference can occur, automated auditing pipelines must first secure reliable data streams from these disparate legal environments. Consequently, understanding the specific characteristics of these sources informs the design of statutory retrieval and preprocessing methodologies.

   \subsection{National Sources}
   National legal repositories represent the authoritative apex of the Philippine legislative hierarchy, housing laws that universally preempt local ordinances. These databases are maintained by centralized government agencies and contain highly formalized texts such as Republic Acts, Commonwealth Acts, and Batas Pambansa. They serve as the primary knowledge base for external legal context, providing the authoritative baseline rules against which local drafts are evaluated. Because national legislation undergoes a rigorous, standardized drafting process, the vocabulary within these repositories is generally consistent and adheres to strict legal drafting standards. Accessing these national databases provides computational systems with the necessary macroscopic view of state-level mandates and prohibitions.

   The Official Gazette serves as the constitutionally mandated repository for Philippine statutes, rendering it the primary journal of record for national legislative text~\cite{officialgazette}. It acts as the official publisher for all newly enacted national laws, presidential executive orders, and administrative issuances, as required by Republic Act No.~453~\cite{act453_1902} and Commonwealth Act No.~638~\cite{ca638_1941}. Because it represents the definitive, legally binding version of a statute, any computational system evaluating local statutory preemption must anchor its baseline rules to this repository. This archive effectively acts as the authoritative historical ledger for state-level mandates, preserving the exact phrasing approved by the legislative and executive branches. Consequently, it remains the most critical target for establishing an authoritative statutory knowledge base.

   Complementing the official journal, developers utilize the Open Congress API provided by the House of Representatives Legislative Information System (LEGIS) to access structured, machine-readable metadata regarding legislative routing and statutory amendments~\cite{hrep2023legis}. This programmatic interface tracks a statute's formal lifecycle, charting its progression from initial committee filing to final executive approval. By providing standardized output formats, the API allows computational pipelines to establish relational dependencies between different legislative acts without manually parsing unstructured text.

   Finally, the Lawphil Project functions as a massive, privately maintained digital repository of statutory records and jurisprudential decisions~\cite{lawphil2000}. Unlike official government channels that frequently change web templates, this repository provides a comprehensive, pre-digitized plaintext corpus of historical and contemporary Philippine law. This accessibility makes it mathematically ideal for the unsupervised pre-training and vectorization of foundational language models tailored to the local legal domain. By ingesting this vast corpus of clean text, NLP architectures learn the highly specific syntactic structures and deontic operators inherent in Philippine jurisprudence, establishing the core vocabulary required for transformer models to process specialized statutory drafting~\cite{zheng2021when}.

   \subsection{Local Sources}
   In contrast to centralized national databases, local legal repositories are maintained independently by individual Local Government Units (LGUs). These archives house local ordinances, resolutions, and executive orders that govern specific geographic jurisdictions. The legislative output at this tier is entirely decentralized, resulting in highly variable archiving practices depending on the technological capacity of the specific city council. These local databases form the immediate target corpus for local conflict detection, containing the actual regulatory constraints imposed on city residents. Consequently, they represent a high-volume, dynamic data environment that continuously expands as the local council holds regular drafting sessions.

   The locality's primary corpus consists of the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod website, which serves as the official digital repository for local legislative outputs~\cite{davaocity_sp_dept}. This portal acts as the primary historical record for city regulations, resolutions, and zoning policies enacted within the city's jurisdiction. It represents a dynamic and continuously expanding text environment that directly reflects the decentralized nature of local lawmaking. The texts housed here encapsulate the specific regulatory constraints and policy directions of the city government, providing the exact ordinance text required to evaluate new drafts for logical consistency against the existing legislative framework.

   To manage the extensive volume of local legislation, the city introduced the Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP) as its primary administrative backbone~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. This platform serves as a digital tracking system designed to maintain metadata regarding ordinance routing, committee assignments, and plenary approval statuses. The system provides transparency for monitoring ordinances and resolutions in real time~\cite{patumbon2025lissp}. By digitizing the legislative workflow, LISSP effectively functions as a centralized database of ordinances, providing the structured tracking and contextual data necessary for evaluating the lifecycle of local laws, while addressing the interoperability challenges identified in recent regional e-governance assessments~\cite{conchas2025digital}.

    \subsection{Data Extraction Challenges}
    While the aforementioned repositories contain the necessary legal knowledge, extracting the needed data for computational analysis presents severe technical challenges. Scraping and formatting these diverse sources exposes the pipeline to significant data degradation and structural inconsistencies across multiple dimensions.

        \subsubsection{Archival Scanning and OCR Degradation}
        A primary barrier to automated statutory auditing is the lack of native, machine-readable text across archival collections. Both the Official Gazette and the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod public archive predominantly host scanned, image-based PDFs rather than selectable digital documents. During manual inspection of these collections, enactments were observed to contain varying degrees of visual noise, including slanted scans, faded ink, low contrast, and compression artifacts. As Lopresti~\cite{lopresti2008optical} documents, such noisy visual data results in severe Optical Character Recognition (OCR) degradation, where standard extraction tools produce broken alphanumeric strings and nonsensical tokens. These irregularities suggest that a direct ingestion pipeline suffers from semantic fragmentation, requiring an additional high-precision extraction layer and extensive text-cleaning heuristics to ensure data viability.

        The resulting character and word-level corruptions severely hinder automated semantic analysis. In statutory conflict detection, a single corrupted character can alter the legal interpretation of a provision, such as transforming a negative operator into an affirmative assertion or scrambling a penalty amount. Standard tokenizers struggle to process these degraded sequences, fragmenting broken words into out-of-vocabulary sub-tokens that dilute contextual representations~\cite{rust2021how}. Furthermore, scanning artifacts frequently obscure section demarcations and legislative numbering, preventing standard parsers from isolating self-contained statutory units. Consequently, automated preemption pipelines must incorporate noise-tolerant document normalization to restore structural integrity before downstream retrieval.

        \subsubsection{Repository Scarcity and Digital Isolation}
        Another critical challenge is archival fragmentation, wherein legislative repositories suffer from systemic gaps in their digital records. This poses a significant problem for high-fidelity auditing because the absence of even a single related ordinance can lead to false negatives in conflict detection, where a system declares a draft valid simply because it cannot access the contradicting record. A prominent example is the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod public archive, which features intermittently missing ordinance files and unorganized chronological listings that hinder systematic data collection~\cite{spdavao2026}. Even relatively complete national databases like Lawphil can lack specific Republic Acts or historical amendments. As Westermann et al.~\cite{Westermann2019computer} observe, fragmented legal databases force developers to move beyond simple scraping toward fault-tolerant data engineering to ensure an exhaustive corpus.

        A further obstacle in statutory data acquisition is the discrepancy between the theoretical and actual accessibility of local legislative databases. For example, while administrative systems like Davao City's LISSP are promoted in institutional reports as public transparency portals~\cite{citygov2025lissp, patumbon2025lissp}, empirical verification reveals strict authentication barriers requiring authorized credentials. Technical inspection indicates that local legislative portals often remain computationally isolated behind authentication interfaces, restricting automated extraction by public web scrapers. Correspondence with local legislative administrators confirms that full-text enactments require administrative clearance and manual provisioning. As Conchas and Salapa~\cite{conchas2025digital} observe, isolated administrative data often exists as digitally orphaned artifacts, where metadata about an enactment is disconnected from its full-text searchable content, requiring specialized ingestion pipelines to reconcile sparse public metadata with offline full-text documents.


\section{Exact and Symbolic Methods}
Before the adoption of neural networks, computational legal analysis relied heavily on deterministic systems to process text. Deterministic systems operate on absolute rules, meaning that a specific input will invariably produce the exact same output without any probabilistic guessing. Traditional methods for searching and evaluating legal information assumed that legislative ideas could be cleanly mapped either to specific vocabulary strings or to rigid, hand-coded logic formulas. While highly transparent and easy to debug, these foundational approaches frequently fail when forced to detect hidden conflicts in complex, unstructured legal texts. The core issue is that human language is inherently messy, and rigid systems break down when faced with the ambiguous phrasing typical of local ordinances.

    \subsection{Boolean Information Retrieval}
    To understand why traditional legal databases struggle with semantic conflict detection, we first need to examine how they fundamentally process text at the machine level. Boolean Information Retrieval (BIR) represents the foundational search architecture underlying most commercial legal repositories. Instead of reading a document like a human reader, BIR treats every enactment simply as an unordered ``bag of words,'' stripping away grammar, syntax, and sentence structure. It relies on an \textit{inverted index}---much like the alphabetical subject index at the back of a printed textbook that records which specific page numbers mention a particular keyword, but possesses no understanding of the surrounding narrative or legal argument. Because it registers solely the mechanical presence or absence of character strings, the system is completely blind to the actual context or regulatory intent behind those words.
    
    At its core, the retrieval mechanism in BIR is strictly based on set theory and formal Boolean logic. A user constructs a query using logical operators like \texttt{AND} ($\land$), \texttt{OR} ($\lor$), and \texttt{NOT} ($\neg$) to create intersecting sets of documents. For example, a search for a specific ordinance regarding explosives is formulated mathematically as:

    \noindent\textit{Boolean Query Set Intersection Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
        Q = (\text{fireworks} \lor \text{pyrotechnics}) \land \neg \text{exempt}
        \label{eq:boolean_query}
        \eqcaption{Boolean Query Set Intersection Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where the compound query $Q$ combines disjunctive keyword sets under conjunction with a negated exemption condition.

    The system then retrieves documents by calculating the exact mathematical intersection of these sets, pulling only the files that satisfy every condition simultaneously. If a document contains the specific string of characters requested, it is retrieved and scored as a 1; if it misses even a single required term, it is completely ignored and scored as a 0. It evaluates the raw presence of strings, entirely ignoring the conceptual meaning or legal intent behind them~\cite{blair1985an}.

        \subsubsection{Challenges with BIR}
        The primary limitation of BIR in legal auditing is its absolute, uncompromising reliance on exact string matching. This rigidity creates a severe ``vocabulary mismatch'' problem when processing natural language. Human language naturally features synonymy, where entirely different words possess the exact same meaning, and polysemy, where the identical word takes on drastically different meanings depending on the context. In statutory drafting, one author might use ``prohibited'' while another uses ``unlawful,'' referring to the exact same legal concept. Because BIR operates on character-level matching rather than conceptual mapping, it fundamentally cannot connect these two terms, leading to massive blind spots in the search results.
        
        A landmark empirical evaluation of these systems demonstrated this vocabulary gap in a highly practical setting. Legal researchers conducting queries using standard BIR systems confidently estimated they were finding over 75 percent of the relevant documents for a given case. They experienced an illusion of effectiveness because the documents they did retrieve looked highly accurate, masking the massive volume of relevant files they missed. In reality, the system retrieved less than 20 percent of the actual relevant files contained within the database~\cite{blair1985an}. The researchers missed the remaining 80 percent simply because the authors of those hidden documents used slightly different, completely valid phrasing that did not perfectly align with the strict search query~\cite{opijnen2017on}.

        In a local government context like Davao City, this specific margin of error is administratively unacceptable. Missing even one existing ordinance during an ex-ante review due to a simple vocabulary mismatch could directly result in the passage of an invalid, conflicting law. Furthermore, legislative vocabulary inherently evolves over time as drafting conventions change. An ordinance passed in the 1990s and a modern draft written in 2026 might strictly regulate the exact same local regulatory subject but share absolutely zero common keywords. Recent studies emphasize that resolving this requires the implementation of ``Transformer'' models, which abandon exact matching entirely in favor of continuous vector spaces that recognize when different vocabulary points to the same underlying concept~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    \subsection{Symbolic AI}
    Because BIR fundamentally fails to capture underlying meaning, early artificial intelligence researchers attempted to automate legal reasoning by directly modeling the rules of law themselves. This approach, widely known as Symbolic AI or ``Good Old-Fashioned AI'' (GOFAI), operates on the premise that human cognition can be replicated by manipulating formal symbols~\cite{vellino1986artificial}. It moves beyond simple word matching by teaching computers actual legal logic through explicit, human-coded ``IF-THEN'' formulas. Instead of searching a database for raw text strings, Symbolic AI requires human engineers to manually translate the constraints of a statute into formal logic structures. This theoretically allows the computer to process the actual regulatory mechanics of a law rather than just reading its surface vocabulary.

    To execute this logic, these systems specifically utilize propositional or first-order logic combined with deontic operators. Deontic logic is a specialized branch of mathematical philosophy that formally evaluates normative modalities governing conduct: obligations ($\mathcal{O}$, representing mandatory actions that must be performed), permissions ($\mathcal{P}$, representing discretionary actions that may be undertaken), and prohibitions ($\mathcal{F}$, representing unlawful actions that must not be performed). For example, a system represents a local zoning prohibition mathematically as:

    \noindent\textit{First-Order Deontic Prohibition Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \forall x (\text{Business}(x) \land \text{ResidentialZone}(x) \rightarrow \text{Prohibited}(x))
        \label{eq:first_order_deontic}
        \eqcaption{First-Order Deontic Prohibition Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where the universal quantifier ($\forall$) asserts that for any entity $x$, if it is classified as a business ($\land$) and located in a residential zone, it is strictly prohibited ($\rightarrow$).

    By converting ordinances into these rigid formulas, a system can theoretically use deductive reasoning to spot a conflict. If Rule A dictates $\mathcal{O}(X)$ (an absolute obligation to do X) and Rule B dictates $\mathcal{F}(X)$ (an absolute prohibition against doing X), the system successfully flags a formal logical contradiction ($\bot$)~\cite{athan2013legalruleml}. 

        \subsubsection{Challenges with Symbolic AI}
        While Symbolic AI provides perfectly transparent and mathematically sound reasoning, its absolute reliance on manual programming makes it practically impossible to scale for real-world governance. The primary architectural barrier is the classical ``knowledge acquisition barrier'' in expert systems: human domain experts and software engineers must manually read, interpret, and translate thousands of unstructured city ordinances into rigid computer rules. This manual translation process is unacceptably slow, extremely expensive, and highly prone to human error during the coding phase~\cite{lucos2025neurosymbolic}. Furthermore, every time the city council amends an existing law, the underlying symbolic formula must be manually rewritten, making the system incredibly difficult to maintain over time.

        Beyond the logistical challenge of manual coding, the rigid binary architecture of Symbolic AI fundamentally clashes with what legal philosopher H.L.A.~Hart famously termed the \textit{open texture of law}~\cite{hart1961concept}. As Hart demonstrated in \textit{The Concept of Law}, statutory language cannot anticipate every future factual scenario; consequently, lawmakers deliberately employ open-ended, discretionary standards---such as mandating ``reasonable care,'' ``due diligence,'' or ``good moral conduct''---to allow adjudicators flexibility across changing societal contexts. These subjective legal standards cannot be reduced to binary IF-THEN rules because their meaning shifts depending on circumstance and judicial interpretation~\cite{ashley2017artificial}. While symbolic systems are precise in tightly constrained, toy environments, they immediately fracture when exposed to the linguistic ambiguity and multi-layered exceptions characteristic of municipal legislative archives~\cite{zhong2020how}.


    
\section{Graph-Based Architectures}
Because exact word matching can be unreliable, computational legal studies frequently explore graph-based architectures to model relationships between statutory provisions. Instead of treating legal texts as isolated character sequences, graph-based frameworks represent statutes as interconnected nodes within a structural network. By analyzing how enactments cite, amend, and conceptually reference one another, these architectures capture latent legal context that bag-of-words models invariably miss.

    \subsection{Knowledge Graphs and GNNs}
    Legal Knowledge Graphs (LKGs) model statutory ecosystems by representing legal entities, provisions, and administrative bodies as structured vertices linked by directional, typed edges. A Legal Knowledge Graph can be conceptualized as an interactive roadmap of the statutory system: every positive enactment, administrative agency, and specific regulatory clause acts as an individual \textit{node} (or landmark), while the formal relationships connecting them---such as ``amends,'' ``cites,'' ``implements,'' or ``delegates authority to''---act as directional \textit{edges} (or connecting pathways). Unlike flat text indices, these relational graphs enable Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to learn node embeddings by aggregating feature representations across local structural neighborhoods~\cite{bhattacharya2019a}. Researchers have developed automated frameworks like GNN-ERE to extract legal entities and relations directly from unstructured statutory text, establishing structured taxonomies without manual relational coding~\cite{ma2025gnn}. Furthermore, empirical evaluations demonstrate that graph-based representations improve legal document retrieval by mapping semantically related court judgments even in the absence of verbatim keyword overlap~\cite{harde2025lecnet}.

        \subsubsection{Graph Modeling and Multi-Hop Reasoning}
        The foundational strength of Graph Neural Networks lies in their ability to propagate structural context across multi-hop relational paths. In statutory interpretation, normative conflicts rarely occur through isolated direct collisions; rather, they frequently manifest indirectly across chains of administrative definitions and delegated authorities~\cite{yang2021legalgnn}. Much like a word-of-mouth chain of communication where individuals update their knowledge by conversing with their immediate neighbors, GNNs execute iterative \textit{message passing} across adjacent nodes. In each message-passing layer, a node aggregates the vector representations of its neighboring provisions, allowing the network to evaluate whether an ordinance clashes with a superior mandate through an intermediate regulatory concept. This topological awareness enables systems to distinguish between different legislative functions, such as separating procedural requirements from substantive prohibitions~\cite{bhattacharya2019a}. Consequently, graph neural architectures provide a principled mechanism for structural reasoning across complex, multi-tiered statutory hierarchies.

        Furthermore, graph representations capture institutional relationships that remain hidden during sequential text reading. In decentralized governance frameworks like the Philippines, local legislative acts operate within a matrix of national agencies, provincial guidelines, and local zoning boundaries. Graph neural models learn relational node embeddings that reflect these jurisdictional hierarchies, allowing algorithms to evaluate legislative preemption within its broader administrative network~\cite{ma2025gnn}. By mapping shared regulatory targets across different enactments, the graph structure guides inference models toward relevant statutory clusters that standard search engines fail to connect. This relational capacity makes graph modeling an appealing conceptual paradigm for analyzing legislative coherence across decentralized jurisdictions.

        \subsubsection{Structural Vulnerabilities in Unstructured Archives}
        While Graph Neural Networks offer substantial theoretical utility, their practical deployment depends strictly on the completeness and fidelity of the underlying graph representation. A primary vulnerability is their reliance on explicit statutory cross-references to establish graph edges~\cite{humphreys2020populating}. In Philippine local archives, local ordinances frequently omit formal citations to the governing Republic Acts they inadvertently contradict, relying instead on localized colloquial descriptions. When explicit relational links are missing from the initial graph construction, the GNN remains fundamentally blind to the underlying normative collision, unable to propagate message vectors across disconnected nodes.

        This limitation is exacerbated by systemic disconnection and physical degradation across local government records. City archives like Davao City's LISSP store enactments as isolated scanned documents rather than interconnected, machine-readable data nodes~\cite{conchas2025digital}. When automated relation extraction pipelines encounter noisy optical character recognition outputs, they generate hallucinated edges and misattributed entity linkages~\cite{lopresti2008optical}. These erroneous connections propagate through successive graph convolution layers, contaminating node representations and introducing false conflict signals throughout the network. Consequently, without expensive, manual human curation to verify every edge, graph-based systems prove too brittle for noisy, real-world local archives.

    \subsection{Abstract Meaning Representation}
    While Graph Neural Networks capture macro-level relationships between distinct legal documents, Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) evaluates the semantic and logical structure inside individual sentences. AMR represents the meaning of a sentence as a rooted, directed acyclic graph where nodes correspond to semantic concepts and edges represent semantic roles~\cite{banarescu2013abstract}. In legal informatics, AMR provides a formal representation of ``who is doing what to whom,'' abstracting away superficial syntactic variations like active versus passive voice~\cite{vu2022abstract}. For example, whether a statute declares that ``The City Mayor shall enforce the penalty'' or that ``The penalty shall be enforced by the City Mayor,'' AMR generates the identical semantic graph, identifying the City Mayor as the agent and the penalty as the patient. This semantic grounding allows computational systems to evaluate legal statements based on their core logical assertions rather than surface grammar.

        \subsubsection{Semantic Graph Representations and Role Labeling}
        A principal advantage of Abstract Meaning Representation in statutory analysis is its ability to isolate predicate-argument structures and deontic operators within complex legislative commands. Statutory provisions rely heavily on deontic modalities---such as obligations, permissions, and prohibitions---to govern citizen behavior and administrative authority~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. Standard grammar parsers often conflate these modal verbs with general descriptive predicates, obscuring the primary regulatory command. In contrast, AMR maps deontic operators directly to core predicates, explicitly identifying whether an action is mandatory, permissible, or prohibited~\cite{vu2022abstract}. This structural clarity allows systems to identify subtle policy collisions, such as when a local ordinance authorizes a commercial practice that a national statute expressly forbids.

        Furthermore, AMR graphs facilitate semantic normalization across diverse legislative drafting styles. Because local ordinances are drafted by different local committees across multiple decades, identical regulatory concepts are routinely phrased using disparate vocabulary and sentence structures. AMR normalizes these surface differences into standardized semantic frames, allowing different legislative phrasings to map to identical concept graphs~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. Recent legal NLP benchmarks demonstrate that evaluating semantic graph similarity outperforms raw token comparisons when identifying core regulatory requirements~\cite{vu2022abstract}. This structural consistency positions AMR as a valuable framework for extracting precise semantic representations from dense legislative text.

        \subsubsection{Sentence Boundary Constraints and Parser Degradation}
        Despite its structural precision, Abstract Meaning Representation faces severe operational limitations when applied to Philippine local legislation. The primary architectural constraint is AMR's restriction to single-sentence parsing~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. In statutory drafting, preemption conflicts rarely manifest within a single isolated sentence; rather, normative contradictions typically emerge across distinct sections, such as a general mandate in one clause negated by an unprovided exception several paragraphs later. Because standard AMR parsers evaluate individual sentences independently, they cannot perform cross-sentence coreference resolution or integrate distant provisos, creating significant logical blindspots in statutory conflict detection.

        Compounding this sentence-level restriction is AMR's high sensitivity to complex statutory syntax and optical character recognition noise. Philippine statutory provisions, such as sections within Republic Act No.~7160, frequently exceed one hundred words, featuring deeply nested conditional clauses and multiple provisos within a single period~\cite{trong2018an}. When processing these complex syntactic structures alongside scanning artifacts or localized administrative phrasing, standard AMR parsers frequently fail, producing fragmented or malformed logic graphs~\cite{vannoord2017the}. Without extensive domain-specific parser retraining tailored to Philippine legal drafting, AMR cannot provide a dependable, production-ready foundation for ex-ante local statutory auditing.

\section{Deep Learning and Transformers}
The application of Natural Language Processing (NLP) in the legal domain has shifted from rigid, rule-based systems and basic word-matching toward Deep Learning~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. Legacy algorithms treat legal texts as simple character strings, which frequently fail to capture the dense and interconnected nature of statutory drafting. Deep Learning, conversely, utilizes multi-layer artificial neural networks to map out the actual semantic meaning of legal language. This allows systems to process complex syntax and perform the deductive reasoning required to spot conflicts. This section breaks down the Transformer architecture, explaining its underlying mechanics, computational challenges, and why it remains the most practical framework for auditing unstructured local archives.

    \subsection{Transformers and Self-Attention}
    A major breakthrough was the Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} in 2017. Prior neural sequence models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, processed text sequentially from left to right, one token at a time. This step-by-step processing was computationally slow and created a severe cognitive bottleneck: as the sentence grew longer, the model gradually ``forgot'' earlier contextual cues, making it difficult to connect distant words across verbose legal provisions. 
    
    Transformers eliminate this sequential bottleneck entirely through the \textit{Self-Attention} mechanism. Rather than reading words one by one, self-attention evaluates all tokens in an input sequence simultaneously in parallel. For every word in a provision, the mechanism computes a set of attention weights measuring how much every other word in the text contributes to its contextual meaning, regardless of their physical distance in the sentence. For example, in a provision stating that ``Any licensee who operates without a valid health clearance shall be penalized,'' self-attention directly connects ``licensee'' to ``penalized'' across the intervening relative clause in a single computational step. Because it processes data in parallel, Transformers train substantially faster on modern hardware, directly enabling foundational language models. Modern iterations have introduced refinements like Disentangled Attention to represent token content and relative positional offsets separately, alongside Windowed Attention to process long-context legal documents efficiently. However, while generative Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at text summarization, they are prone to ``hallucinations''---fabricating plausible but non-existent statutory citations or misinterpreting statutory cross-references~\cite{deroy2023how}. To ensure absolute legal reliability, consistency auditing requires a discriminative approach: specifically classifying whether two statutory provisions stand in a relationship of Entailment, Neutrality, or Contradiction, rather than probabilistically generating unverified text.

    \subsection{Transformer Challenges}
    Despite their architectural versatility, deploying transformer models within the computational constraints of Philippine Local Government Units (LGUs) introduces substantial operational hurdles.

    The foremost engineering bottleneck arises from quadratic memory scaling and context length limits. The standard self-attention mechanism scales quadratically ($O(n^2)$) with input sequence length $n$. Intuitively, because every token must compute an attention score against every other token in the sequence, the resulting attention matrix requires $n \times n$ memory entries. Doubling the length of an input sequence quadruples the memory required to compute attention. Consequently, standard foundational architectures like BERT are constrained to a rigid 512-token context window~\cite{devlin2019bert}, whereas Philippine ordinances frequently span multiple pages. While practitioners employ sliding-window techniques to segment documents into overlapping spans~\cite{anand2020explainable, seo2025sliding}, this segmentation severs the semantic dependency between general regulatory mandates and distant provisos~\cite{savelka2021discovering}. Consequently, sequence truncation creates structural blind spots that can lead to false conflict classifications.

    A secondary challenge involves morphological noise and domain adaptation limits. Pretrained transformers require massive corpora of clean text, and while Western legal models like Legal-BERT perform well on foreign statutory benchmarks~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}, no dedicated foundation model exists for Philippine local legislation. Applying general tokenizers to local archives---saturated with scanning artifacts, archaic statutory phrases, and administrative Taglish---triggers severe token fragmentation, where domain-specific terms are broken into meaningless sub-tokens that degrade contextual comprehension.

    Finally, edge deployment is heavily restricted by local government hardware realities. Executing deep logical inference across thousands of document pairs demands substantial GPU compute and memory bandwidth~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Because most local government units operate standard desktop workstations with modest hardware specifications, deploying unoptimized neural models leads to system latency spikes or memory exhaustion, necessitating decoupled, multi-stage architectures optimized for local inference.

    \subsection{Suitability of Transformers}
    Notwithstanding these hardware limitations, Transformer models remain the most viable approach for Semantic Conflict Detection in local ordinances. General-domain language models typically struggle with legal text because common words frequently take on entirely different legal definitions. For instance, ``party'' refers to a litigant rather than a celebration, and ``suit'' signifies a legal action rather than clothing. Greco et al.~\cite{Greco2023Bringing} emphasize that this high degree of polysemy is exactly why domain-specific models are required. While older static systems assign a single, fixed meaning to a word~\cite{Greco2023Bringing}, a Transformer dynamically adjusts its reading of a word based on the surrounding sentence context~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2019large} empirically showed that this contextual awareness is what allows a model to distinguish between amending a law and violating it---a distinction that simple keyword searches invariably miss.

    The transition to Deep Learning necessitates explicitly moving away from legacy methodologies. Boolean keyword searches fundamentally fail because legislative drafting vocabulary constantly evolves over the decades. Conversely, manually mapping thousands of unlinked Republic Acts and local ordinances into deterministic graph structures creates an intractable administrative burden. As Li et al.~\cite{li2024construction} observed, hand-crafting these legal knowledge graphs simply does not scale for volatile, unstructured local records. Ultimately, Transformers represent the ideal method: they possess the capacity to map the deep, latent semantic relationships in unstructured legal text without requiring an intractable amount of manual data entry.

    

\section{Single-Stage and Multi-Stage Architectures}
In mainstream Natural Language Processing (NLP), end-to-end, single-stage neural architectures serve as the conventional baseline for document classification, sentiment detection, and question answering. These systems funnel raw input sequences directly into a single neural network that outputs the final classification without intermediate filtering. However, in legal informatics, detecting statutory conflicts requires a level of deductive precision across vast corpora that a monolithic model structurally cannot provide. Forcing a single architecture to simultaneously scan tens of thousands of statutory records and evaluate fine-grained deontic logic creates severe mathematical and operational trade-offs.

    \subsection{Single-Stage Monolithic Pipelines}
    The appeal of a single-stage neural architecture is its conceptual simplicity: a single model handles the entire input-output mapping without requiring intermediate data handoffs. In standard NLP tasks operating over small document collections, this approach minimizes engineering complexity and eliminates pipeline latency bottlenecks. However, deploying a monolithic model to audit draft local legislation against expansive national repositories introduces severe operational barriers, inevitably sacrificing either candidate recall or deductive precision.

    One common monolithic approach involves prompting generative Large Language Models to directly declare legal preemption. However, autoregressive language models operate as probabilistic token predictors rather than deterministic logical discriminators, rendering them vulnerable to legal hallucinations where non-existent statutory citations are fabricated~\cite{linna2026challenges}. Furthermore, their massive parameter sizes make offline deployment on standard local government hardware infeasible without heavy quantization, which severely degrades deductive reasoning fidelity in specialized statutory domains~\cite{dettmers2022llmint8}. 
    
    Conversely, relying exclusively on single-stage bi-encoders introduces severe semantic compression limits. In a bi-encoder (or dual-tower) architecture, the search query and the candidate statute are processed through separate neural networks into independent vector coordinates. Because the two texts never interact directly during encoding, the model can pre-compute and store embeddings for all 27,000+ laws in advance, enabling sub-second nearest-neighbor search across extensive archives~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert, chen2024bgem3}. However, compressing an entire multi-clause provision into a single fixed-dimensional vector inevitably discards granular syntactic nuance~\cite{karpukhin2020dense, thakur2021beir}. This architecture optimizes for broad topical similarity rather than logical consistency, causing bi-encoders to conflate permission and prohibition provisions that share identical topical vocabulary~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    Furthermore, executing a single-stage cross-encoder across an entire statutory repository is computationally prohibitive. In contrast to bi-encoders, a cross-encoder concatenates the draft query and the candidate statute into a single unified sequence, allowing every token in the draft to interact directly with every token in the statute through full bidirectional cross-attention. While this joint comparison achieves high logical sensitivity, full cross-attention scales quadratically ($O(n^2)$) with input length. Comparing a newly proposed local draft against 25,432 national statutes simultaneously would require hundreds of thousands of complex forward passes, instantly exhausting workstation memory and causing intolerable system latency~\cite{beltagy2020longformer}. Forcing the model to process fragmented sliding windows across thousands of enactments severs inter-clause dependencies. This computational ceiling confirms that isolating a compact candidate shortlist via coarse retrieval is essential prior to executing fine-grained sequence classification.

    \subsection{Two-Stage Retrieve-then-Entail Architectures}
    To resolve the mathematical and computational limitations of monolithic architectures, contemporary legal informatics adopts decoupled, two-stage systems. In the retrieve-then-entail paradigm, the workflow is split into two specialized stages: an initial coarse Information Retrieval (IR) phase that scans the entire statutory corpus to extract a top-$k$ candidate shortlist, followed by a fine Natural Language Inference (NLI) phase that performs deep cross-attention on the candidate pairs~\cite{williamsetal2022anlizing}. Williams et al.~\cite{williamsetal2022anlizing} demonstrate that this two-tier design reduces manual auditing overhead by up to seventy percent while preserving high inferential accuracy.

    The primary engineering advantage of this decoupled architecture is its alignment with edge computing realities. By offloading broad corpus search to pre-computed vector indices that require minimal runtime memory, the computationally intensive $O(n^2)$ matrix operations of the cross-encoder are restricted exclusively to the top-$k$ candidate pairs. Khattab and Zaharia~\cite{khattab2020colbert} established that this decoupled structure enables advanced neural language processing to execute locally on consumer-grade hardware without requiring distributed GPU clusters. Furthermore, decoupling retrieval from inference provides a pragmatic resolution to the vector compression trade-off. The initial retrieval stage accepts the loss of granular syntactic nuance to maximize search throughput across extensive statutory archives. In contrast, the subsequent inference stage bypasses vector pooling entirely, performing joint token-level cross-attention over raw, uncompressed text spans to achieve high-precision deontic reasoning~\cite{humeau2019poly}.

    \subsection{Comparative Architectural Trade-offs}
    Comparing single-stage and dual-stage architectures highlights a fundamental division of labor in statutory preemption analysis. A single-stage retriever maximizes search speed but remains blind to subtle modal negators, whereas a single-stage cross-encoder possesses high logical sensitivity but cannot scale beyond a few dozen provisions without memory failure. By chaining both mechanisms in a coarse-to-fine hierarchy, the system leverages the complementary strengths of each model family: Stage One absorbs corpus volume and context length, while Stage Two resolves logical nuance and morphological noise~\cite{louis2022a}.

    The empirical superiority of this decoupled design is reinforced by over a decade of findings from the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE). As Goebel et al.~\cite{goebel2026an} document, COLIEE divides legal reasoning into statutory information retrieval (Task 3) and statutory textual entailment (Task 4). Year after year, top-performing teams reject single-stage monoliths in favor of pipelined approaches~\cite{althammer2021dossiercoliee}. While early benchmarks in COLIEE 2021 established this separation, modern iterations through COLIEE 2025~\cite{coliee2025proceedings} and COLIEE 2026~\cite{coliee2026proceedings} confirm that decoupled retrieval and inference remain the gold standard. For statutory retrieval, hybrid dense-sparse systems achieve near-perfect candidate recall~\cite{ngo2026nowj}, while cross-encoder entailment systems reach over 95\% accuracy by isolating granular reasoning from broad corpus retrieval~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. This sustained global validation confirms that separating Information Retrieval from Natural Language Inference is the established operational blueprint for resolving semantic conflict across statutory corpora.

    
\section{Information Retrieval}
Information Retrieval (IR) is the process of extracting relevant documents from massive, unstructured datasets based on a specific query. In legal informatics, IR acts as the foundational filtering mechanism. It serves as the ``coarse'' stage of a dual-pipeline system, isolating candidate statutes from thousands of irrelevant local records before any intensive logical analysis takes place.

    \subsection{Information Retrieval Challenges in Statutory Corpora}
    Applying information retrieval techniques to statutory collections introduces domain-specific hurdles that general-domain search engines are not engineered to handle. A primary challenge is severe lexical divergence and vocabulary mismatch across legislative eras~\cite{blair1985an}. In statutory law, statutes governing identical civic activities may be enacted decades apart, using drastically different terminology, archaic phrasing, or specialized administrative acronyms. When newly drafted local ordinances employ modern regulatory expressions, exact keyword matching fails to retrieve governing historical statutes, creating dangerous blindspots in preemption review~\cite{opijnen2017on}.

    Furthermore, statutory corpora exhibit extreme length asymmetry and structural complexity that disrupt standard retrieval scoring. Statutory enactments frequently contain verbose, multi-clause provisions exceeding one thousand tokens, where general commands are qualified by distant provisos and statutory exceptions~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Standard passage retrievers struggle to balance term saturation across these extended provisions, frequently penalizing lengthy, comprehensive enactments. Compounding this structural challenge is administrative hierarchy noise: in national legal archives, subordinate issuances (such as executive orders, circulars, and departmental memos) outnumber primary legislative acts, frequently diluting candidate shortlists unless protected by statutory priority safeguards~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    \subsection{Relevance in Retrieval}
    To understand how computational systems extract documents, we must first examine the human cognitive process that defines relevance in the physical world. In cognitive science, relevance is not a static property but a dynamic relationship between information and a specific task. According to Relevance Theory, humans naturally filter information by seeking the greatest cognitive benefit for the least mental effort~\cite{sperber1996relevance}. When a legal researcher checks for conflicts, they do not simply count matching words; they instinctively evaluate the ``intent'' and ``applicability'' of a rule based on their background knowledge of the legal system. This human-centric view suggests that a document is relevant only if it provides a new piece of information that changes the researcher's understanding of the legal landscape~\cite{saracevic2007relevance}.

    Beyond the cognitive baseline, the theoretical definition of relevance in legal information retrieval is anchored in the philosophical requirements of a functional legal system. According to the jurisprudence established by Lon Fuller in \textit{The Morality of Law}, a primary requirement for the internal morality of law is consistency; a system that issues contradictory rules fails to be a legal system at all~\cite{fuller1964the}. Within this philosophical framework, relevance is not merely about finding documents that talk about the same subject matter, but about identifying provisions that share a ``normative overlap.'' This means that a retrieved document is relevant if it occupies the same jurisdictional or behavioral space as a draft local ordinance, potentially creating a situation where a citizen cannot obey one law without violating another. Grounding the retrieval stage in this quest for consistency treats relevance as the systematic identification of potential normative collisions and threats to the rule of law~\cite{fuller1964the}.

    However, traditional computational methods struggle to replicate this human judgment because they rely on a rigid, mathematical definition of relevance. In standard deterministic systems, relevance is treated as a binary mathematical property based entirely on set intersection~\cite{blair1985an}. If we denote a candidate document as $D$ and a user query as $Q$, this lexical relevance is formally evaluated as a binary function $R(D,Q) \in \{0,1\}$. Under this strict lexical definition, a document is deemed fully relevant (i.e. $1$) if and only if it contains the exact sequence of characters requested by the user~\cite{opijnen2017on}. Consequently, this mechanism establishes a purely topical baseline, effectively grouping documents together simply because they share a surface-level vocabulary~\cite{blair1985an}. While this mathematical simplicity allows for rapid indexing across massive databases, it completely strips away the legal context required for complex legislative analysis.

    This stark discrepancy between superficial lexical matching and actual legal consequence represents a major stumbling block for legacy search architectures. In statutory law, two documents discussing the exact same topic might be lexically relevant to one another, but they are only \textit{legally} relevant if one provision actively modifies, permits, restricts, or preempts the other~\cite{opijnen2017on}. For instance, a retrieved document might use identical vocabulary but apply to a completely different jurisdiction or regulatory scope, rendering it completely useless for an ex-ante conflict check. Conversely, a document that governs the exact same civic behavior but uses entirely different phrasing---such as substituting ``motor vehicle'' for ``automobile''---will be scored as mathematically irrelevant (i.e. $0$) by a strict Boolean system~\cite{blair1985an}. Therefore, to accurately audit local ordinances against superior law, statutory retrieval systems must move beyond binary, keyword-driven matching in favor of dense semantic representations capable of capturing actual jurisprudential relationships~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    To resolve these limitations, modern computational pipelines fundamentally redefine relevance spatially rather than lexically. Instead of scanning a query as a raw string of text, dense retrieval architectures utilize Bi-Encoder models to compress entire legal provisions into high-dimensional mathematical coordinates, commonly referred to as dense vectors~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Under this advanced semantic framework, relevance is no longer determined by counting shared characters, but by calculating the geometric distance---such as cosine similarity---between these coordinate points~\cite{kim2024legal}. If a newly drafted local ordinance and an existing national statute govern the same underlying civic behavior, the neural network learns to map their respective vectors closely together in the mathematical space, completely bypassing the vocabulary mismatch problem~\cite{wu2025aiir}. Consequently, the retrieval mechanism evaluates relevance as a continuous proximity score rather than a rigid binary output, successfully surfacing conceptually intertwined laws that basic keyword searches otherwise miss.

    \subsection{Long-Context Retrieval} 
    Relying on standard BERT-based architectures introduces an artificial constraint on legal analysis: the 512-token limit. Because standard Self-Attention mechanisms scale quadratically with sequence length, processing long texts requires severe truncation. To get around this, researchers like Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} routinely use Sliding Window techniques to segment documents into overlapping chunks. However, applying this to Philippine ordinances---which are historically wordy and often bury exceptions deep within 1,000-token provisions---is structurally destructive. It severs the substantive semantic link between a primary rule and its distant exception.

    Consequently, the model operates with a logical blindspot. Beltagy et al.~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} note that this causes logical hallucinations, where a rule might be flagged as an absolute prohibition simply because its exempting clause was pushed into a separate, unconnected processing window. Furthermore, these systems must navigate the trade-off between Sparse Retrieval (e.g., BM25) for exact keyword matching, and Dense Retrieval for capturing conceptual meaning even without direct word overlap.

    Resolving this fragmentation means moving beyond short sliding-window chunking where possible. The architectural solution requires an initial retrieval mechanism capable of native long-context retention. Recent Transformer models incorporate advanced positional encoding, such as Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), alongside sparse or optimized attention matrices. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} demonstrated that these upgrades extend context windows up to 8,192 tokens without triggering memory failures. Deploying this class of architecture allows the system to read entire local ordinances as cohesive semantic units, preserving the law's internal structure and eliminating the Sliding Window fallacy.

    \subsection{Retrieval and Fusion Strategies}
    \label{sec:retrieval_paradigms_fusion}
    In computational legal informatics, isolating candidate enactments from extensive statutory collections requires understanding the theoretical foundations, failure modes, and complementary trade-offs of diverse retrieval strategies. Recent findings from the COLIEE benchmarks and legislative consistency literature identify five core methodological dimensions governing coarse statutory search.

    The foundational retrieval strategies in computational law encompass sparse probabilistic scoring and dense semantic embeddings. Each relevance scoring metric is to be computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Relevance Scoring Function:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q) = \sum_{q \in Q} \text{IDF}(q) \cdot \frac{f(q, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(q, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
        \label{eq:okapi_bm25}
        \eqcaption{Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Relevance Scoring Function}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $f(q, D)$ denotes term frequency of query token $q$ in document $D$, $|D|/\text{avgdl}$ normalizes for provision length, and parameters $k_1 = 1.5$ and $b = 0.75$ govern term saturation and length penalization within Robertson and Zaragoza's probabilistic relevance framework~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}. 
    
    Intuitively, the Okapi BM25 formula balances three core search mechanics:
    First, \textit{Inverse Document Frequency} ($\text{IDF}(q)$) gives higher weight to rare, informative words (such as specific statutory citations or administrative agency names) while downweighting common words that appear everywhere.
    Second, \textit{Term Frequency Saturation} (controlled by $k_1 = 1.5$) recognizes diminishing returns---mentioning a search term twenty times does not make a document twenty times more relevant than mentioning it four or five times, preventing keyword-stuffed texts from distorting search scores.
    Third, \textit{Document Length Normalization} (controlled by $b = 0.75$) penalizes exceptionally verbose provisions, ensuring that lengthy multi-page legislative codes do not dominate search rankings simply because their word volume increases the accidental probability of keyword hits.
    In statutory retrieval competitions such as COLIEE Task~3, BM25 serves as an indispensable baseline (as demonstrated by Team AIIR~\cite{wu2025aiir}) because legislative drafting routinely depends on exact statutory citations, formal administrative acronyms, and verbatim penal monetary amounts. However, exact lexical matching suffers from the vocabulary mismatch problem when local ordinances paraphrase statutory commands~\cite{blair1985an, manning2008introduction}.

    \noindent\textit{Dense Semantic Cosine Proximity Relevance Function:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{dense}}(D, Q) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_Q \cdot \mathbf{e}_D}{\|\mathbf{e}_Q\| \|\mathbf{e}_D\|}
        \label{eq:dense_cosine_sim}
        \eqcaption{Dense Semantic Cosine Proximity Relevance Function}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathbf{e}_Q$ and $\mathbf{e}_D$ represent pooled contextual embeddings generated by dense bi-encoders such as \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Rather than evaluating token overlap, dense retrievers score relevance via cosine proximity in latent semantic space, capturing conceptual legal intent and retrieving governing statutes even when local drafts employ disparate phrasing.

    In heterogeneous statutory databases spanning tens of thousands of enactments, researchers frequently investigate topic classification or domain partitioning to reduce search space complexity. However, empirical literature highlights the severe danger of hard categorical filtering in legal domains. Foundational statutory instruments, such as the Philippine Local Government Code of 1991 (RA 7160) or comprehensive penal codes, function as omnibus legislation that simultaneously govern local administration, taxation, public safety, and environmental sanitation. Hard filtering by a single predicted regulatory category discards the governing omnibus code whenever a query touches an adjacent regulatory area, causing catastrophic candidate recall collapse. In contrast, information retrieval theory establishes soft domain priors~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}. Instead of binary candidate exclusion, soft priors apply a calibrated multiplicative score boost (e.g., $+20\%$) to candidate documents matching the predicted topic or latent semantic cluster, preserving access to multi-domain statutory codes while gently elevating topically congruent provisions.

    Because lexical and semantic retrievers exhibit orthogonal failure modes, leading computational law frameworks combine both streams through hybrid fusion. In score-level linear combination, popularized by Team JNLP in the COLIEE competitions~\cite{goebel2026an}, weighted interpolation combines normalized sparse and dense scores according to $S_{\text{hybrid}}(D, Q) = \alpha \hat{S}_{\text{dense}}(D, Q) + (1 - \alpha) \hat{S}_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q)$, where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ balances semantic and lexical bias. However, because BM25 scores are unbounded while cosine similarities span $[-1, 1]$, linear combination requires min-max score normalization, introducing sensitivity across heterogeneous query lengths. To eliminate score normalization artifacts, Cormack et al.~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal} formulated Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), defined as $\text{RRF}(D) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} (k_{\text{rrf}} + \text{rank}_m(D))^{-1}$, where $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{BM25}, \text{Dense}\}$ and $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$. 
    
    The intuitive advantage of Reciprocal Rank Fusion lies in its scale-invariant rank combination: combining raw scores from BM25 and dense bi-encoders is mathematically comparable to adding kilograms to kilometers because their underlying distributions differ completely. Rather than attempting to rescale disparate numerical scores, RRF evaluates solely the ordinal rank position of each candidate (whether a statute ranked 1st, 2nd, or 10th in each individual search stream). The smoothing constant $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$ ensures that top-ranking documents receive high scores while preventing the gap between rank 1 and rank 2 from disproportionately overwhelming candidates that perform consistently well across both keyword matching and semantic vector search.

    Finally, statutory retrieval systems must respect constitutional hierarchies of authority. Anchored in the doctrine of legislative supremacy and the hierarchy of legal norms~\cite{kelsen1967pure, philgov1991ra7160}, statutory systems operate under strict formal ordering. In national digital archives, subordinate administrative issuances (such as executive orders, administrative circulars, and departmental memos) frequently constitute nearly half of all indexed records. Because administrative issuances share broad governmental vocabulary with local drafts, unconstrained retrieval often allows routine circulars to crowd out superior national statutes. Literature in legal informatics emphasizes structural safeguards that prioritize primary legislative acts (Republic Acts) over subordinate executive issuances. Implementing a hierarchical statutory priority safeguard shields governing statutes from administrative noise, preventing dilution of candidate shortlists and significantly improving Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).

    \subsection{Retrieval Benchmarks}
    Selecting effective retrieval architectures from the extensive landscape of open-weight models requires objective empirical benchmarking. In contemporary legal information retrieval, candidate models are evaluated against standardized empirical leaderboards that assess zero-shot generalization across dense statutory text. The Benchmarking Information Retrieval (BEIR) suite evaluates a dense retriever's capacity for out-of-domain transfer across diverse, specialized datasets~\cite{thakur2021beir}. Because Philippine local legislation lacks extensive annotated training corpora, candidate retrievers must possess strong zero-shot generalization. Dominant performance on the BEIR leaderboard serves as a primary indicator of a model's capacity to map novel, localized legal terminology into a structured latent space without prior fine-tuning~\cite{thakur2021beir}.

    Expanding on BEIR, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) establishes an international standard for ranking embedding systems across more than one hundred datasets and diverse linguistic tasks~\cite{muennighoff2023mteb}. In information retrieval literature, candidate architectures are frequently filtered using MTEB's retrieval and clustering leaderboards. Models excelling on these benchmarks demonstrate an empirical capacity to cluster conceptually related doctrines and retrieve relevant provisions based on continuous semantic proximity. Complementing general embedding benchmarks, the annual COLIEE Task~3 evaluation provides a specialized testbed for statute law retrieval~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}. Systems evaluated on Task~3 must extract precise governing articles from civil code collections in response to legal queries. Success in this competition requires balancing broad candidate recall with computational throughput, providing a proven testing foundation for leading statutory retrieval models~\cite{wu2025aiir, goebel2026an}.

\section{Natural Language Processing}
Natural Language Processing (NLP) provides the computational techniques needed to analyze and represent text at various linguistic levels, aiming for human-like comprehension \cite{liddy2001natural}. As Jurafsky and Martin \cite{jurafsky2024speech} explain, it acts as the bridge between human language and machine logic, allowing algorithms to parse and interpret unstructured text. In the legal field, NLP shifts document auditing from slow manual reviews to scalable, automated systems. However, applying standard NLP to law requires more than basic grammar checks; it demands systems that can navigate dense sentence structures, deontic operators (obligations, permissions, prohibitions), and the highly specific vocabulary found in statutory law.

Early Legal NLP relied heavily on Symbolic Artificial Intelligence (AI)~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. These systems used manually built ontologies and strict Boolean logic to extract legal information. However, because local legislative drafting language changes constantly over decades, these exact-match frameworks proved far too brittle. Contemporary NLP architectures solve this by moving to continuous vector representations. By mapping textual provisions into high-dimensional semantic spaces, modern Transformers bypass superficial vocabulary mismatches to mathematically quantify the deeper concepts connecting different legal terms. This allows the system to act as an active diagnostic engine that structurally maps legal conflicts, rather than just a passive search index.

This section breaks down how NLP operates within legislative analysis. It covers the systemic challenges of processing localized legal text, the structural need for Natural Language Inference (NLI), the benchmarks used for model selection, and the specific architectures built for these deductive tasks.

    \subsection{NLP Challenges}
    Deploying NLP frameworks against local archives quickly exposes the mechanical limits of standard Language Models. A primary hurdle is the pronounced Global North bias in pre-training data and legal benchmarking. As Ranathunga et al.~\cite{ranathunga2022some} point out, the vast majority of Legal NLP benchmarks, like LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} and CaseHOLD, are trained exclusively on pristine, digitally native Western legal text. These highly optimized models degrade when applied to Philippine Local Government Unit (LGU) archives, which are filled with scanned PDFs suffering from Optical Character Recognition (OCR) errors, archaic American colonial phrasing, untranslated Spanish legal terms, and local Taglish. Rust et al.~\cite{rust2021how} demonstrated that standard WordPiece tokenizers shatter unrecognized words into sub-tokens, destroying a model's ability to infer legal intent. Addressing localized morphological noise requires deploying models equipped with massive Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) vocabularies that absorb linguistic variants without triggering out-of-vocabulary errors, paired with Disentangled Attention architectures that separate word content from corrupted positions~\cite{he2023debertav3}.

    Beyond geographic and morphological bias, traditional NLP methods suffer from structural shortcomings in lexical similarity. Early NLP systems excel at identifying topical overlap by measuring surface-level token intersection using algorithms like TF-IDF or shallow word embeddings. However, Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli} explain that pure lexical similarity is fundamentally unsuited for detecting legal contradictions because lexical metrics are entirely blind to deontic logic and syntactic negation. For example, an ordinance stating that the city permits an activity and a statute stating that the state strictly prohibits it share near-perfect lexical similarity scores. A standard similarity model clusters them as highly related, completely missing the absolute policy collision occurring beneath the shared vocabulary. Consequently, detecting normative conflicts requires advancing from lexical proximity to directional logical inference.

    \subsection{From NLP to NLI}
    Historically, checking legislative consistency relied on Symbolic NLP (hand-written rules) and, later, Statistical NLP. In Statistical NLP, models learned probabilistic weights for specific inputs, like Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) or N-grams~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. These models depended heavily on human-engineered features to define grammar rules and categories. While they allowed for soft, probabilistic decisions, they consistently struggled with the deep lexical and semantic ambiguities inherent in legal drafting.
    
    The field largely transitioned to Neural NLP models in the mid-2010s~\cite{quevedo2024legal}. Unlike statistical systems that treat features as separate entities, neural networks operate end-to-end, mapping raw text directly to output predictions through hidden layers. These architectures capture deeper linguistic patterns than traditional statistical methods by learning complex relationships during optimization. However, while machine learning excels at finding patterns, it can overly mimic its training data, making domain-specific pre-training essential for high performance in rigorous legal tasks.

    Within modern neural approaches, Natural Language Inference (NLI)---originally formalized under the Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE) challenges initiated by Dagan et al.~\cite{dagan2006pascal}---has emerged as the definitive benchmark for evaluating whether an algorithm can perform genuine logical deduction. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024improving} define NLI as determining the directional logical relationship between two structured text sequences: a governing \textit{Premise} and a candidate \textit{Hypothesis}. In statutory preemption auditing, this formulation maps directly to constitutional hierarchy: the Premise represents the established positive legal standard (the superior national statute), while the Hypothesis represents the newly proposed local claim (the draft city ordinance provision). 
    
    The neural network must classify the relationship into one of three mutually exclusive logical states:
    (1) \textit{Entailment}, where the draft ordinance directly carries out, derives its authority from, or compliantly adheres to the national statute;
    (2) \textit{Contradiction}, where the draft ordinance directly collides with the national statute (for example, permitting what national law prohibits, or exceeding statutory penalty ceilings under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine); and
    (3) \textit{Neutral}, where the two provisions govern complementary or non-overlapping subject matter without normative conflict.
    This directional formulation exposes the fatal flaw of earlier statistical NLP, which mistakenly equated high word overlap with logical entailment~\cite{yang2024improving}. Modern neural approaches fix this by capturing deep compositional semantics. As Le Hong et al.~\cite{lehong2023a} point out, this allows modern models to resolve complex linguistic phenomena---like coreferences and modality---that rigid rule-based systems historically failed to grasp.

    \subsection{NLI Benchmarks}
    Identifying effective Cross-Encoder architectures for the inference stage requires isolating models with robust, pre-trained deductive logic. In natural language processing, research literature relies on established benchmarks to evaluate candidate reasoning models. Wang et al. introduced the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE)~\cite{wang2019glue} and its successor SuperGLUE~\cite{wang2020superglue} as foundational baselines for model reasoning. These frameworks evaluate a Transformer's ability to handle diverse linguistic challenges, such as Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE) and resolving complex coreferences. While high performance on SuperGLUE confirms baseline deductive capacity, it does not independently guarantee success in the legal domain due to the specialized vocabulary shifts present in statutory drafting.

    To evaluate specialized legal logic, legal NLP research relies on the Legal General Language Understanding Evaluation (LexGLUE) benchmark. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} designed LexGLUE specifically to test a model's ability to navigate legalese, where common words assume highly context-dependent meanings. High performance on LexGLUE entailment tasks indicates that an architecture possesses the foundational deductive capacity required to navigate complex statutory drafting and regulatory provisions.

    Complementing domain-specific suites, contemporary Hugging Face NLI leaderboards evaluate models on diverse, adversarial textual entailment tasks aggregating MultiNLI (MNLI), FEVER, Adversarial NLI (ANLI R1--R3)~\cite{nie2020anli}, and WANLI~\cite{liu2022wanli}. Benchmarking candidate models across these suites tests their resilience against spurious lexical heuristics, premise-hypothesis artifacts, and negation traps. High-performing architectures on these leaderboards, such as the pre-aligned DeBERTa-v3 variants curated by Laurer et al.~\cite{laurer2024less}, demonstrate strong zero-shot deductive capacity, serving as leading candidate foundations for statutory conflict detection. Finally, within legal informatics, the definitive international competition benchmark for statutory inference is Task~4 of the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE)~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}. In Task~4, systems evaluate whether a natural language query is legally entailed by or contradicts a governing civil code article. Evaluating architectures against COLIEE Task~4 methodologies provides direct empirical evidence of a model's capacity to navigate statutory conditions, exceptions, and deontic modalities.

    \subsection{Candidate NLI Models}
    \label{sec:nli_candidate_models}
    Evaluating deontic relationships across statutory provisions demands specialized inductive biases across candidate sequence encoders. Standard language models trained exclusively on general corpora frequently fail to capture the specialized semantics of legal drafting, where everyday terms carry specialized juridical meanings. To address this vocabulary divergence, researchers develop domain-adapted encoders initialized with custom legal vocabularies or continuously pretrained on expansive legal repositories. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2020legal} introduced \texttt{LEGAL-BERT}, pretrained on diverse English legislation, court judgments, and contracts, demonstrating superior term sensitivity across legal classification tasks. Henderson et al.~\cite{henderson2022pileoflaw} expanded this approach with \texttt{PoL-BERT} (Pile of Law), pretraining encoders on a 256~GB corpus encompassing administrative regulations, municipal codes, and statutory provisions. Complementary efforts by Paul et al.~\cite{paul2022inlegalbert} (\texttt{InLegalBERT}) and Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2021when} (\texttt{CaseLaw-BERT}) investigate the transferability of common-law statutory pretraining, while Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2021lawformer} evaluate long-document legal encoders. In literature, this family provides a critical testbed for assessing whether domain pretraining provides intrinsic protection against legal misinterpretation.

    A pervasive vulnerability of unadapted sequence encoders on deontic tasks is \textit{affirmative entailment bias}, where models exhibit an innate psychological tendency to default toward predicting Entailment or Neutral when reading complex clauses containing subtle prohibitions~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. In standard language corpora, true logical contradictions are exceedingly rare; text passages typically elaborate, confirm, or describe unrelated facts. Consequently, unadapted neural models learn a strong statistical prior that pairs generally agree. Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} established \texttt{bert-base-uncased} (110M parameters) with masked language modeling, serving as the canonical supervised baseline against which improvements are measured. To overcome heuristic reliance on lexical overlap and break affirmative bias, contemporary literature explores models pre-aligned on multi-task natural language inference datasets (such as MNLI, FEVER, ANLI, and WANLI). Laurer et al.~\cite{laurer2024less} demonstrate that initializing sequence classifiers from warm NLI checkpoints equips models with robust directional contradiction sensitivity before domain adaptation. Structurally, He et al.~\cite{he2023debertav3} developed \texttt{DeBERTa-v3}, introducing Disentangled Attention to represent word content and relative positions with separate vectors alongside adversarial replaced token generation. In parallel, Clark et al.~\cite{clark2020electra} developed \texttt{ELECTRA} with Replaced Token Detection, optimizing contextual representations across all input positions. These architectures excel at distinguishing subtle deontic shifts that standard unidirectional or masked encoders routinely miss.

    Statutory drafting is inherently verbose, characterized by complex preamble clauses, extensive definitions, and multi-sentence penal qualifications. Traditional transformer encoders are constrained by quadratic self-attention complexity, imposing a rigid 512-token ceiling that necessitates destructive document chunking or sliding window heuristics~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Early long-context architectures attempted to resolve this constraint using localized or sparse attention approximations, such as the sliding-window attention in \texttt{Longformer}~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} and the block-sparse random attention in \texttt{BigBird}~\cite{zaheer2020bigbird}. However, empirical legal informatics evaluations note that sparse attention patterns can dilute or sever semantic dependencies between distant provisos and governing commands. To overcome these limitations, modern architectures integrate architectural optimizations that support extended contexts natively. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} introduced \texttt{ModernBERT}, an 8,192-token encoder incorporating FlashAttention-2, Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), GeGLU activation functions, and sequence unpadding. Complementing this, Nussbaum et al.~\cite{nussbaum2024nomic} developed \texttt{Nomic-BERT} to extend context retention. These modern long-context designs enable joint encoding of complete multi-sentence statutory provisions without sparse approximation artifacts.

    Unlike bi-encoders that process queries and documents independently into pooled vector coordinates, cross-encoders execute full bidirectional self-attention across the concatenated pair simultaneously. In retrieval and inference benchmarks, joint cross-attention captures deep inter-token interactions that vector similarity cannot represent. In COLIEE statute retrieval benchmarks, researchers deploy multilingual cross-encoder rerankers, notably \texttt{BGE-Reranker-v2-m3}~\cite{xiao2024bge}, to score pairwise relevance across multi-thousand token contexts. Similarly, sequence classifiers adapted from MS-MARCO reranking frameworks employ deep cross-attentive scoring to evaluate pair compatibility. In literature, native cross-encoders represent the conceptual bridge between Stage~1 candidate filtering and Stage~2 logical validation.

    In post-colonial statutory jurisdictions like the Philippines, statutory archives exhibit unique sociolinguistic complexities. While statutory enactments are drafted in legal English, local ordinances and administrative records frequently feature localized terminology, regional administrative phrasing, Latin legal maxims, and English-Tagalog code-switching. Conneau et al.~\cite{conneau2020xlmr} introduced \texttt{XLM-RoBERTa}, trained across 100 languages, demonstrating strong cross-lingual transfer on semantic benchmarks. He et al.~\cite{he2023debertav3} extended disentangled attention to the multilingual domain with \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, providing resilience to mixed-language syntax. Furthermore, Cruz and Cheng~\cite{cruz2020tagalog} developed Tagalog-adapted transformers (\texttt{roberta-tagalog-base}) to capture Philippine syntactic structures. In statutory consistency checking, multilingual encoders evaluate whether localized linguistic variances can be processed without catastrophic comprehension loss.

    Deploying deep learning in public-sector local government environments requires strict adherence to available edge computing infrastructure. Because local government units typically operate without high-performance computing clusters or enterprise cloud APIs, running billion-parameter language models is computationally unfeasible. To establish realistic performance baselines under constrained hardware, the literature explores knowledge distillation and parameter sharing. Sanh et al.~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} introduced \texttt{DistilBERT}, which compresses transformer depth while retaining over 95\% of language comprehension. Lan et al.~\cite{lan2020albert} introduced \texttt{ALBERT}, utilizing cross-layer parameter sharing to reduce memory footprint down to tens of megabytes. In dense retrieval and inference, Reimers and Gurevych~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} established \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} as an ultra-compact 22-million parameter baseline. Evaluating distilled edge models allows researchers to quantify the exact accuracy penalty incurred when prioritizing sub-second CPU latency over multi-layer neural capacity.

\section{Ground Truth}
In supervised machine learning, a Ground Truth (or Gold Standard) dataset provides the correct, human-verified labels used to train and test models. A Ground Truth dataset functions essentially as an authoritative ``answer key'': during training, the neural network constantly compares its internal predictions against these verified answers, computing the mathematical discrepancy (the loss) and adjusting its internal weight parameters to avoid repeating those errors~\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. Without an accurate, expert-adjudicated answer key, a model suffers from the classic ``garbage in, garbage out'' dilemma, memorizing spurious statistical artifacts rather than genuine statutory logic.

Beyond just teaching the model, the ground truth is the only objective way to measure success. Researchers typically split this data into training, validation, and testing sets. The test set acts as the final exam, kept completely hidden from the model until the very end. Evaluating the model against this unseen ground truth allows researchers to calculate standard performance metrics like precision, recall, and F1-score. Without this baseline, it is mathematically impossible to prove that a model generalizes well to new data or works as intended in a real-world scenario~\cite{bowman2015large}.

    \subsection{Ground Truth in NLI}
    In Natural Language Inference (NLI), ground truth datasets define the logical relationship between two pieces of text. Annotators read a source text (the Premise) and a claim (the Hypothesis), then categorize their relationship into one of three distinct labels. If the premise guarantees the hypothesis is true, it is labeled as Entailment. If they contradict each other, it is labeled as Contradiction. If there is not enough information to prove either, the pair is labeled Neutral~\cite{yang2024improving}.

    Most traditional NLI datasets, such as the widely used SNLI (Stanford Natural Language Inference) corpus, are built using crowdsourced workers who write simple variations of everyday sentences~\cite{bowman2015large}. While this approach is cheap and easily generates hundreds of thousands of training pairs, the resulting datasets are incredibly basic and often embed unintended annotation artifacts~\cite{gururangan2018annotation}. They usually feature short, single-sentence premises with obvious logical connections. Training a model exclusively on these simple datasets leaves it entirely unprepared for the dense, multi-layered logic required in specialized fields.

    \subsection{Annotation and Reliability Metrics}
    Turning raw text into a useful NLI dataset requires strict guidelines to minimize human bias and prevent annotators from relying on subjective legal interpretations. In standard NLI, guidelines dictate simple factual agreement. However, legal conflict detection requires evaluating normative relationships based on deontic logic. Drawing from the frameworks established by Agotnes et al.~\cite{agotnes2007on} and Li et al.~\cite{li2012detecting}, the annotation guidelines strictly instruct experts to map the text into obligations (must do), permissions (may do), and prohibitions (cannot do). A ``Contradiction'' is only flagged if the local ordinance mandates an action the national statute prohibits, or prohibits an action the national statute explicitly permits. If a local ordinance merely adds a regulatory step (e.g., a local processing fee) to a national permit without banning the core activity, the guidelines strictly dictate a ``Neutral'' classification. This rigid deontic framework is designed to ensure that the dataset measures actual legal preemption rather than just topical friction.
    
    To evaluate the effectiveness of these strict guidelines, researchers must utilize Inter-Rater Reliability (IRR) metrics~\cite{mchugh2012interrater} rather than mathematically flawed percentage agreements. Raw percentage agreement fails to account for statistical probability: in a three-choice multiple-choice classification task, if three raters were to guess labels completely at random, they would still achieve an accidental agreement rate of approximately 33.3\% by pure chance. Reporting raw percentage agreement produces an illusion of consensus because it credits lucky coincidences as genuine human alignment.
    
    The two primary statistical formulas used to subtract this random chance are Cohen's Kappa~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} and Fleiss' Kappa~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}. Each inter-rater reliability metric is to be computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Cohen's Pairwise Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}
        \label{eq:cohens_kappa}
        \eqcaption{Cohen's Pairwise Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $p_o$ represents the relative observed agreement between the two individual raters, and $p_e$ represents the hypothetical probability of chance agreement. Cohen's Kappa evaluates agreement between exactly two raters.

    \noindent\textit{Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa = \frac{\bar{P} - \bar{P_e}}{1 - \bar{P_e}}
        \label{eq:fleiss_kappa}
        \eqcaption{Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\bar{P}$ represents the mean observed agreement across all pairs of raters, while $\bar{P_e}$ represents the expected chance agreement across the entire pool. Fleiss' Kappa generalizes Cohen's formula to accommodate three or more domain experts establishing a majority consensus.

    The resulting $\kappa$ score is interpreted using the universally accepted statistical matrix established by Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977measurement}:

        \centering
        \caption{Interpretation Ranges for Fleiss' Kappa Reliability Metric}
        \label{tab:kappa_interpretation}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}cc@{}}
            \toprule
            \textbf{Kappa ($\kappa$) Score} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
            \midrule
            $< 0.00$ & Poor agreement \\
            $0.01 - 0.20$ & Slight agreement \\
            $0.21 - 0.40$ & Fair agreement \\
            $0.41 - 0.60$ & Moderate agreement \\
            $0.61 - 0.80$ & Substantial agreement \\

        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}

    Table~\ref{tab:kappa_interpretation} outlines the standard categorical intervals for evaluating inter-rater agreement across multiple independent judges. It consists of two columns mapping numerical $\kappa$ ranges to their corresponding qualitative interpretations, providing an objective statistical benchmark for assessing annotation consensus. The table is included to ground the evaluation of the 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers within an authoritative psychometric standard. As the score progresses from negative values toward unity, it reflects a transition from chance-level alignment to robust consensus across evaluators. For this research, the matrix establishes the operational target of $\kappa \ge 0.61$ (``Substantial Agreement''), ensuring that the resulting ground truth benchmark represents dependable legal consistency rather than subjective individual interpretation.

    While objective tasks routinely achieve $\kappa$ scores above $0.90$, applying this expectation to the legal domain is mathematically unrealistic. Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015large}, in their foundational study for the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) corpus, established that evaluating semantic relationships is inherently subjective. Their benchmark study hit an approximate performance ceiling with an overall Fleiss' Kappa of $0.70$. They explicitly demonstrated that achieving near-perfect agreement ($> 0.80$) in textual inference usually indicates overly simplistic or fabricated test data.

        \subsubsection{Sub-Threshold Reliability}
        While achieving a ``Substantial'' Inter-Rater Reliability score ($\kappa \ge 0.61$) validates the clarity of the annotation guidelines, failing to meet this threshold does not inherently invalidate the dataset or halt the experimental pipeline. Klebanov and Beigman~\cite{klebanov2009from} establish that a sub-threshold $\kappa$ score typically indicates systematic ambiguity within the text itself---categorized as ``hard'' instances where seasoned legal professionals can reasonably disagree on statutory scope---rather than carelessness by the annotators. Their research demonstrates that it is still entirely possible to extract a reliable, low-noise subset from the instances where annotators do achieve consensus.

        When researchers encounter this sub-threshold reliability, the mitigation strategy depends entirely on the size of the dataset. For large-scale studies, including the disaster-tweet classification by Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing}, the standard procedure is data omission. Beigman et al.~\cite{klebanov2009from} explicitly advocate for filtering out difficult, borderline cases from the gold standard, arguing that evaluating algorithms on inherently ambiguous text introduces statistical noise akin to random coin-flips. In these massive datasets, items that fail to reach majority consensus are simply discarded because the volume of remaining ``easy'' data easily absorbs the loss.
        
        However, applying this standard omission strategy to a specialized, few-shot legal dataset mathematically destroys the test set's Minimum Detectable Effect. Furthermore, in local legislation, these ``hard'' cases often represent the exact jurisdictional friction the logic engine needs to learn. To preserve dataset volume without introducing benchmarking noise, researchers in specialized domains employ Expert Adjudication rather than omission. Gao et al.~\cite{gao2022hierarchical} establish that in these scenarios, a hierarchical adjudication procedure is used where a senior domain expert reviews the conflicting annotations. The senior expert resolves the ambiguity based on governing jurisprudential principles and manually establishes the Gold Standard label, treating the initial low $\kappa$ metric as a measure of text difficulty rather than a rigid experimental blocker.

    \subsection{Ground Truth in Legal NLP}
    When applying NLI to the legal domain, the standard approach of comparing short, isolated sentences quickly breaks down. Statutory phrasing is notoriously complex, filled with conditional clauses, exceptions, and domain-specific vocabulary. Consequently, a hypothesis in a legal context rarely aligns perfectly with a single sentence in the premise. Instead, proving an entailment or contradiction often requires the model to connect evidence scattered across multiple paragraphs or pages.

    To address this, researchers have developed specialized legal datasets that reflect real-world document structures. For instance, the Competition on Legal Information Extraction/Entailment (COLIEE) dataset constructs its ground truth by pairing civil code statutes with legal bar exam queries, requiring models to identify specific legal provisions before determining entailment~\cite{rabelo2022coliee}. Similarly, the ContractNLI dataset by Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} engineers its gold standard around non-disclosure agreements. Rather than giving the model pre-cut sentence pairs, ContractNLI forces the system to evaluate hypotheses against entire legal documents, requiring annotators to both classify the logical relationship and extract the specific supporting text span.

    Furthermore, unlike general-domain datasets crowdsourced through platforms like Amazon Mechanical Turk, legal ground truth relies heavily on domain experts. Constructing datasets like CaseHOLD~\cite{zheng2021when}, which evaluates legal holding statements, requires law students and legal professionals to ensure the labels accurately reflect actual jurisprudence. The push towards comprehensive legal benchmarks, such as LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}, demonstrates that evaluating legal NLP models demands rigorously structured, expert-annotated data rather than automated or amateur labeling.

    This difficulty is magnified when dealing with local legislation. While national or federal laws generally follow standardized drafting conventions, local ordinance texts are highly heterogeneous, exhibiting significant variations in terminology, structure, and overall organization across different jurisdictions~\cite{campos2026nlp}. Because of this localized variance, local legal documents have historically been neglected in NLP research, hampered primarily by a severe lack of annotated ground truth datasets~\cite{campos2026citilink}. Standard legal corpora fail to capture the specific legislative templates, localized governance terminology, or idiosyncratic cross-referencing styles used in local legislation, such as Philippine city ordinances. Without existing resources that reflect these structural and linguistic complexities, evaluating models on local legislation requires building highly specialized, expert-annotated datasets from the ground up.

    A critical architectural decision in dataset construction is determining the exact granularity of the extracted legal premise. While the hypothesis (the local draft) is evaluated as a multi-sentence span to capture context, the premise (the national statute) must be strictly isolated at the sentence level. In Philippine statutory drafting, a single paragraph frequently contains a general rule, multiple conditional modifiers, and distinct exceptions. Holzenberger et al.~\cite{holzenberger2020a} demonstrate that feeding an entire statutory paragraph into an NLI model as a premise dilutes the model's attention mechanism, causing it to lose track of specific variable constraints. By forcing human annotators to isolate the national rule down to its exact sentence boundary, the resulting Ground Truth prevents the candidate logic engines from getting distracted by surrounding, non-governing text, ensuring the classification mathematically targets the exact point of semantic collision.

    The difficulty of dataset construction is magnified by the scarcity of legal domain experts available for annotation. As Zhong et al.~\cite{zhong2020how} explicitly note, the complex terminology required for legal texts makes manual labeling highly time-consuming and difficult to scale, as it strictly requires domain experts rather than crowdsourced workers. Because constructing massive datasets for localized city law is practically unfeasible, researchers must rely on constrained, highly curated Gold Standards. To compensate for this limited data volume, the current NLP standard mandates treating legal conflict detection as a few-shot learning scenario. Gao et al.~\cite{gao2021making} validate this approach, proving that transformer models can be successfully fine-tuned on specialized datasets containing merely hundreds of examples, provided the evaluation framework rigorously controls for overfitting.

    \subsection{Multi-Sentence and Cross-Document Limits}
    Traditional Natural Language Inference (NLI) evaluates text using a strict 1-to-1 pairing, comparing a single premise sentence directly against a single hypothesis sentence. However, this highly granular approach fundamentally breaks down in the legal domain due to two specific edge cases. First, intra-document context: a primary rule in one section of an ordinance is frequently modified or negated by an exception buried several paragraphs later (the ``1v2'' or multi-sentence problem). Second, cross-document dependencies: a contradiction may only emerge when synthesizing provisions from two entirely separate statutes simultaneously.

    To address the first edge case, modern legal frameworks utilize Asymmetric Span-Level Pairing. Yin et al.~\cite{yin2021docnli} critique traditional sentence-level datasets, proving that capturing contextual exceptions requires evaluating multi-sentence blocks rather than isolated lines. As demonstrated by Koreeda and Manning in the ContractNLI dataset~\cite{koreeda2021contract}, the premise remains a single, focused legal rule, while the corresponding hypothesis is extracted as a multi-sentence chunk. This asymmetric structure allows the model's attention mechanism to accurately evaluate a primary rule against a distant exception contained within the same local provision.

    While asymmetric spans successfully capture intra-document context, resolving the second edge case---cross-document multi-hop reasoning---remains computationally prohibitive. Identifying semantic preemption that spans multiple independent laws currently represents the bleeding edge of NLP research and is not yet fully explored for local governance. Recent frameworks have attempted to solve this, but they introduce severe architectural overhead. For instance, Lu et al.~\cite{lu2023multi} address cross-document retrieval by programmatically mapping Entity Graphs to mine evidence paths between laws, a process that is highly resource-intensive and unscalable for unstructured LGU archives. Alternatively, Shen et al.~\cite{shen2025hopweaver} proposed the HopWeaver framework, which relies on massive generative Large Language Models (LLMs) to synthesize complex reasoning paths across documents.

    Both of these state-of-the-art solutions immediately violate the strict hardware constraints of consumer-grade LGU workstations. Consequently, practical, edge-deployable systems must intentionally bound their theoretical scope to single-premise, asymmetric span evaluations, deliberately avoiding the quadratic memory scaling required for multi-document fusion.

    \subsection{Dataset Partitions}
    When evaluating NLI models on constrained datasets, researchers often default to $k$-fold cross-validation to maximize testing coverage. However, Vabalas et al.~\cite{vabalas2019machine} mathematically demonstrated that applying cross-validation to datasets under 1,000 items introduces severe optimistic bias, causing models to perform significantly worse in real-world deployment than their test scores suggest. To rigorously control for overfitting in few-shot legal scenarios, contemporary evaluation frameworks must abandon $k$-fold approaches. Instead, they require strict, static Train/Validation/Test partitions (e.g., 70/15/15) to ensure the evaluation metrics remain entirely untainted by training exposure.

    Beyond merely preventing optimistic bias, utilizing a static partition allows for rigorous validation monitoring during the fine-tuning phase. In datasets with limited volume, neural networks are highly susceptible to memorizing the training data rather than learning underlying logical patterns. Goodfellow et al.~\cite{goodfellow2016deep} establish that permanently isolating a validation set allows architectures to employ early stopping criteria, halting the training process the moment the model's accuracy on unseen data begins to degrade. Furthermore, as Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2021revisiting} note regarding few-sample fine-tuning, this static separation ensures strict document-level boundaries, preventing clauses from a specific draft ordinance from accidentally bleeding across the training and testing sets to artificially inflate performance metrics.

    \subsection{Minimum Detectable Effect}
    A standard critique of static splits on small datasets is that the resulting test set lacks the statistical power to reliably compare candidate architectures. However, Card et al.~\cite{card2020with} established that a test set's validity is fundamentally determined by its Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) rather than arbitrary size thresholds. 
    
    Intuitively, statistical power in model evaluation can be understood through the analogy of an optical telescope: if two astronomical bodies (or two competing algorithm accuracy rates) are separated by only a microscopic fraction of a degree, an enormous telescope (a massive evaluation benchmark spanning tens of thousands of test instances) is required to detect the gap. Conversely, when comparing an unadapted zero-shot baseline that performs barely above random guessing with a fine-tuned model that achieves a dramatic leap in domain comprehension, the performance gap is so wide that even a compact, carefully curated test set of approximately 50 instances possesses the statistical power needed to confirm that the difference is genuine and not a statistical fluke.

    The practical application of MDE fundamentally shifts how model performance is evaluated in specialized domains. In general NLP research, massive test sets are required because researchers are often evaluating models against benchmarks like GLUE to prove marginal, incremental gains of one percent to two percent over previous state-of-the-art architectures~\cite{wang2019glue}. However, applying a base foundation model---which possesses no prior knowledge of Philippine local governance for example---to a highly specialized task generally results in baseline performance barely above random chance~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2021when} demonstrate that in the legal domain, fine-tuning foundational architectures on expertly curated pairs triggers a massive, double-digit jump in classification accuracy compared to zero-shot baselines. Because this expected performance delta is substantial, legal NLP frameworks do not require thousands of annotated examples to demonstrate statistically valid improvements, proving that an evaluation set of approximately 50 curated pairs possesses sufficient statistical power to mathematically capture this distinct leap in model reasoning capacity.

\section{Explainable AI}
In legal informatics, a ``black box'' prediction is fundamentally unacceptable, regardless of nominal classification accuracy. Automated systems that flag normative conflicts between statutory enactments must provide verifiable reasoning (\textit{ratio decidendi}) behind their classifications to genuinely assist legislative analysis~\cite{doshi2017towards}. Without transparent justification, legal practitioners and legislative staff cannot trust, audit, or formally defend algorithmic determinations. Consequently, contemporary legal NLP literature emphasizes Explainable AI (XAI) not as an optional secondary layer, but as an intrinsic structural requirement of high-stakes legal decision support, prioritizing faithful mathematical traces over ungrounded generative summaries.

    \subsection{Intrinsic Explainability}
    Within the explainability literature, researchers distinguish between Post-Hoc Surrogate Models and Intrinsic Explainability Mechanisms. Surrogate methods, such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)~\cite{ribeiro2016why} and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)~\cite{lundberg2017unified}, attempt to approximate the behavior of complex deep networks by fitting a simplified, interpretable linear model around individual predictions. Intuitively, intrinsic explainability is comparable to reading the native dashboard gauges of an automobile (such as the speedometer and tachometer, which directly reflect the engine's mechanical operations during driving). In contrast, post-hoc surrogate methods are like attempting to deduce how the engine works by repeatedly kicking the tires or perturbing individual components thousands of times from the outside, which introduces significant approximation errors and freezes modest office desktop computers~\cite{rudin2019stop}. In text classification benchmarks, these external explainers are computationally demanding and inherently prone to approximation errors, as they explain an external proxy rather than the underlying network itself~\cite{rudin2019stop}. In contrast, intrinsic approaches extract internal model representations---such as self-attention weights and contextualized hidden states---directly from the transformer layers during the forward pass. Because native attention extraction reflects the mathematical weights computed during inference without fitting secondary approximations, it offers an intrinsically faithful and computationally lightweight mechanism for structural transparency.
    
    While utilizing attention weights as a proxy for feature importance has sparked significant debate, empirical findings demonstrate its utility for targeted textual inspection. Jain and Wallace~\cite{jain2019attention} argued that raw attention distributions do not always correlate consistently with gradient-based feature importance. However, Wiegreffe and Pinter~\cite{wiegreffe2019attention} provided critical counter-evidence, demonstrating that attention mechanisms provide faithful representations of internal prioritization under rigorous diagnostic testing. In statutory conflict detection and deontic reasoning, extracting token-level attention spans allows reviewers to identify the specific conflicting provisions (for example, isolating an ``absolute prohibition'' in a superior statute against an ``unauthorized permit'' in a local draft), establishing a direct visual link between textual evidence and classification decisions without incurring the computational overhead of auxiliary surrogate models.
    
    Complementing structural attention heatmaps in modern explainability frameworks is Confidence Calibration. Deep learning models, particularly multi-layer transformers, frequently exhibit severe overconfidence in erroneous predictions when evaluated on out-of-domain or specialized text. In simple terms, modern neural networks often resemble overconfident test-takers: they frequently declare 99\% confidence in an answer even when their underlying classification is completely mistaken. As Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017on} demonstrated, modern neural networks are fundamentally miscalibrated, such that raw softmax probabilities rarely reflect the true empirical likelihood of correctness. In high-stakes regulatory auditing, relying on uncalibrated probabilities produces misleading risk assessments. To restore probabilistic reliability, calibration literature advocates post-hoc temperature scaling applied to pre-softmax logits. Post-hoc temperature scaling functions like a calibrating lens: it softens the raw logits by a learned parameter $\tau$ before softmax normalization, ensuring that when the system predicts an 80\% confidence score on a potential preemption conflict, the model is empirically correct approximately 80\% of the time. This enables decision support systems to distinguish between definitive contradictions and borderline ambiguities with sound statistical justification.

    \subsection{Edge Deployment Feasibility}
    In edge and on-premise computing environments, operational feasibility requires strict adherence to memory and latency boundaries. Post-hoc explainability methods operate by repeatedly perturbing input tokens, requiring hundreds or thousands of forward passes per document to approximate Shapley values or linear boundary slopes~\cite{ribeiro2016why, lundberg2017unified}. Empirical evaluations by Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2020problems} confirm that permutation-based explainers inflate per-document inference latency by several orders of magnitude, rendering them computationally prohibitive for lengthy legislative texts. Furthermore, Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020edge} demonstrate that running complex post-hoc explainers on edge workstations severely degrades throughput, making interactive review impractical. In resource-constrained deployment settings, intrinsic extraction of native attention weights and calibrated logits provides a computationally viable pathway to transparency without requiring specialized high-performance computing clusters.

    \subsection{Explanation Quality Metrics}
    Evaluating explanation quality in natural language processing requires distinguishing between two fundamental evaluation criteria: Plausibility and Faithfulness. In everyday human communication, a persuasive speaker can deliver an explanation that sounds completely reasonable and elegant (high plausibility) even if it completely misrepresents what actually occurred. In artificial intelligence, \textit{plausibility} measures how convincing, fluent, and intuitive an explanation appears to a human reader, whereas \textit{faithfulness} measures the degree to which an explanation accurately reflects the internal reasoning operations employed by the model to generate its prediction~\cite{jacovi2020towards, doshi2017towards}. Jacovi and Goldberg~\cite{jacovi2020towards} demonstrate that optimizing explanations purely for plausibility frequently produces deceptive explanations that mask internal reasoning failures and biases. In legal domains, legal scholarship emphasizes that faithfulness must take precedence, as an explanation that sounds plausible but misrepresents statutory text can lead to catastrophic compliance failures.
    
    This trade-off is particularly evident in generative explainability approaches that deploy auto-regressive large language models to draft plain-language explanations of legal predictions. While generative summaries achieve high human plausibility scores, empirical legal informatics studies demonstrate that generative models frequently suffer from legal hallucinations, fabricating non-existent statutory citations or altering legal interpretations during text generation~\cite{linna2026challenges, katz2024gpt4}. Because generated natural language explanations are decoupled from the classifier's internal mathematical representations, they remain inherently unfaithful~\cite{jacovi2020towards}. Consequently, legal informatics literature increasingly favors extractive and attention-grounded visualizations that display the exact mathematical spans influencing classification, avoiding generative distortions.

    \subsection{Explainability in Multi-Stage Pipelines}
    In multi-stage NLP pipelines, literature on Explainable AI emphasizes situating interpretability mechanisms at the fine-grained inference stage rather than the coarse retrieval phase. In retrieve-then-entail systems, the initial retrieval phase functions as a high-throughput candidate selector across extensive document repositories. As Anand et al.~\cite{anand2020explainable} observe, computing dense feature attributions during coarse document filtering provides negligible analytical utility while incurring prohibitive computational latency. In contrast, the subsequent cross-encoder inference stage conducts fine-grained semantic entailment across a constrained candidate set, making it the mathematically appropriate locus for interpretability analysis.

    At this second stage, legal NLP frameworks utilize token-level saliency and attention visualizations to bridge model internals with human inspection~\cite{vig2019bertviz}. By mapping cross-attention weights and calibrated class probabilities back to corresponding spans in the premise and hypothesis texts, systems provide structural verification of detected contradictions without introducing generative paraphrasing risks. This dual mechanism of intrinsic attention mapping and post-hoc confidence calibration allows legislative researchers to trace classification outputs directly back to source statutory phrases.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
As established in legal informatics literature, the Retrieve-then-Entail architecture represents the leading theoretical framework for statutory conflict detection and legislative consistency checking. Originating in the international benchmarks of the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE 2025 and 2026)~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}, this framework integrates two complementary theoretical pillars: a scalable Information Retrieval (IR) architecture to isolate relevant statutory context from expansive corpora, followed by a fine-grained Natural Language Inference (NLI) architecture for rapid deontic conflict detection. Specifically, this theoretical framework synthesizes the dense statutory retrieval methodology of Team AIIR Lab~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}, the multi-stage ensemble retrieval principles of Team JNLP~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, and the encoder-based textual entailment framework of Team KIS~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}.

    \subsection{Statutory Retrieval (Team AIIR)}
    In statute law retrieval, dense bi-encoder architectures offer an effective theoretical foundation for bridging the semantic gap between newly drafted provisions and expansive statutory corpora without relying on computationally demanding generative text decoding or manual taxonomy engineering~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}. Drawing from the empirical methodology developed by the AIIR Lab in recent COLIEE benchmarks, this retrieval pipeline operates across four theoretical dimensions: domain-adapted data augmentation, semantic vectorization, corpus pre-computation, and nearest-neighbor distance evaluation. As depicted, the incoming query provision is projected into a shared semantic coordinate space, allowing its vector representation to be compared directly against pre-computed statutory embeddings via cosine distance. This architectural separation ensures that high-volume statutory collections spanning tens of thousands of provisions can be searched in sub-second latency on consumer-grade hardware, providing the high-recall candidate shortlist necessary to feed the downstream entailment engine.

    Targeted data augmentation and domain adaptation expose dense retrieval models to synthetically augmented legal variants. This training teaches the neural network to recognize identical statutory concepts regardless of whether they appear in archaic terminology or modern drafting syntax, substantially enhancing robustness against lexical divergence. Second, semantic vectorization utilizes a bi-encoder architecture (such as \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2}) to project statutory provisions into a dense, continuous vector space. Unlike cross-encoders that must process query-document pairs jointly, bi-encoders encode queries and corpus documents independently, optimizing embedding coordinates such that logically relevant statutory pairs exhibit high geometric proximity under cosine distance. Third, the pipeline relies on offline corpus pre-computation and vector indexing to satisfy operational efficiency constraints across tens of thousands of statutory enactments. Prior to live query execution, the fine-tuned bi-encoder vectorizes the entire statutory collection into static embeddings within an indexed vector database. Because corpus vectorization is completely decoupled from runtime query execution, dense retrieval scales across extensive archives without requiring real-time GPU re-encoding. Finally, live query vectorization and proximity scoring project incoming draft provisions into the shared embedding space, executing approximate nearest neighbor search to calculate continuous proximity scores in sub-second latency and deliver high candidate recall to prime downstream natural language inference.

    \subsection{Multi-Stage Retrieval (Team JNLP)}
    Complementing pure bi-encoder retrieval, literature in computational law emphasizes multi-stage retrieval pipelines to balance candidate recall against ranking precision. Exemplified by Team JNLP's high-performing architecture in COLIEE benchmarks~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, this multi-stage pipeline progressively filters statutory search spaces across three structured phases: Pre-Retrieval Candidate Pruning, Model Fine-Tuning, and Result Ensembling. The resulting predictions are synthesized through rank fusion, ensuring that the candidate set benefits from both keyword specificity and conceptual semantic coverage.

        \subsubsection{Pre-Retrieval}
        The primary objective of the pre-retrieval phase is to drastically reduce the expansive statutory search space while maintaining high recall across governing provisions. Team JNLP achieves this through a multi-stage candidate pruning procedure combining distance-based heuristic filtering with neural re-ranking~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}. Dense embeddings for statutory queries and corpus provisions are extracted using high-capacity multilingual representations. Rather than relying solely on raw cosine similarity, the methodology calculates multi-dimensional distance metrics (such as L1 distance bins), training a gradient-boosted decision tree to predict relevance and counter severe sample imbalance. Following initial filtering, a specialized passage re-ranker evaluates candidate texts and eliminates non-relevant provisions, narrowing the candidate pool to top-ranked candidates while preserving recall.

        \subsubsection{Model Fine-Tuning}
        Once the candidate space is constrained, the framework applies targeted natural language models to perform fine-grained relevance scoring. Because fine-tuning large models across full parameter sets is computationally intensive, legal NLP frameworks employ Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques, such as LoRA and QLoRA. These techniques enable foundational language models to adapt to specialized statutory semantics with minimal memory overhead. Furthermore, because statutory candidate sets exhibit severe class imbalance where only a small minority of retrieved provisions contain true preemption conflicts, sampling strategies and asymmetric loss functions are applied during training to preserve sensitivity to positive conflict signals.

        \subsubsection{Result Ensemble}
        To maximize retrieval robustness and mitigate individual model variance, multi-stage retrieval pipelines aggregate relevance scores across distinct retrieval architectures. Relevance predictions are aggregated through weighted score aggregation, combining calibrated probabilities from complementary lexical and neural models. The weighting coefficients assigned to individual scoring components are optimized via systematic hyperparameter search algorithms such as Optuna. This optimization ensures balanced contributions between lexical keyword precision and dense semantic recall, yielding a stable candidate shortlist for downstream reasoning.

    In the second phase of legal retrieve-then-entail architectures, the theoretical objective transitions from broad candidate discovery to fine-grained deontic reasoning. To ensure high computational throughput and deterministic classification, modern legal NLP frameworks deploy non-generative cross-encoder architectures, exemplified by the ModernBERT-based rationale extraction methodology developed by Team KIS~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}. \subsection{Theoretical Synthesis}
This synthesis integrates the foundational theoretical models reviewed throughout this chapter into a unified framework for ex-ante legislative conflict detection. While each individual methodology addresses an isolated phase of legal document processing, their conceptual integration establishes a principled retrieve-then-entail architecture. The theoretical foundation for statutory candidate discovery draws from the dense retrieval methodology established by the AIIR Lab~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}. Their approach demonstrates that targeted domain augmentation mitigates OCR noise in archival legal text, while continuous semantic vectorization enables high-throughput candidate identification across large statutory collections without relying on rigid keyword indices.

To balance candidate recall against precision, the framework incorporates the multi-stage ranking principles demonstrated by Team JNLP~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}. This methodology utilizes lexical filtering (BM25) to prune extraneous documents, followed by neural scoring and rank fusion to isolate authoritative candidate enactments on standard computing hardware. For granular deontic conflict evaluation, the framework adopts the Team KIS framework for textual entailment~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}. Their methodology formats premise-hypothesis pairs into a continuous sequence evaluated via lightweight cross-encoders, utilizing token-level separation boundaries to classify logical relationships without generative hallucination risks.

Synthesizing these foundational frameworks establishes an end-to-end theoretical foundation for automated preemption auditing. The retrieval stage narrows extensive statutory search spaces down to high-probability candidate provisions, while the textual entailment stage conducts deterministic normative conflict checks. The latest findings from the COLIEE 2026 proceedings~\cite{coliee2026proceedings, ngo2026nowj} reinforce this architectural consensus, validating that pipelined retrieval and textual entailment provide the most reliable foundation for automated legal consistency analysis.
