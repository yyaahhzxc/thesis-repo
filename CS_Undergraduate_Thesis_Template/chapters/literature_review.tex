\chapter{Literature Review}
    
\section{Semantic Conflict Detection}
In jurisprudence and legal philosophy, the fundamental requirement for a functional legal order is internal consistency, where positive enactments provide unambiguous guidance without subjecting citizens to contradictory commands~\cite{fuller1964the}. In legal theory and formal deontic logic, this phenomenon is strictly defined as a \textit{normative conflict}---a structural clash between legal norms wherein an action is simultaneously commanded, prohibited, or permitted across distinct enactments, which violates the principle of non-contradiction~\cite{li2012detecting, agotnes2007on}. Specifically, normative conflict occurs when two or more valid laws impose incompatible legal duties on the same subject under the same conditions, as when one law mandates an act that another law forbids~\cite{agotnes2007on, italiani2026clash}. When statutory systems generate conflicting obligations, citizens cannot conform their conduct to the law without incurring liability under an opposing mandate; this contradiction destabilizes legal predictability and public trust. Consequently, establishing systematic mechanisms to identify and resolve normative collisions represents a vital safeguard for the integrity of any rule-of-law framework.

However, in real-world legislative governance and local government archives, statutory enactments do not exist as pre-formalized formulas in symbolic logic. Rather, statutes are drafted and promulgated as unstructured, multi-generational natural language texts characterized by high syntactic variance and implicit legal exceptions~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. From a computational perspective, recent literature formulates this challenge as \textit{Semantic Conflict Detection}. By translating abstract deontic clashes into a natural language processing problem, semantic conflict detection uses continuous vector representations and Natural Language Inference (NLI) to identify directional contradictions between local drafts and governing statutory provisions without relying on manually maintained legal ontologies~\cite{shao2021bert, italiani2026clash}. As Ruhl and Katz~\cite{ruhl2015measuring} established, the volume of positive law expands far faster than human auditing capacity, making automated semantic auditing an operational necessity for modern legislative bodies.

   \subsection{Unstructured Legal Corpora}
   Managing unstructured legal text, defined as statutory material not stored in structured relational databases, represents a fundamental maintenance challenge for legislative systems. Practical automated auditing must address rapid textual accumulation alongside the syntactic complexity of legal drafting. Unstructured enactments vary widely across historical eras in formatting, vocabulary, and citation style. These structural realities motivate the shift in legal informatics toward neural retrieval and inference models for statutory verification.
   
        \subsubsection{Legal Inflation}
        The term \textit{legal inflation} describes the continuous expansion in the volume and interconnected complexity of statutory enactments over time. Ruhl and Katz~\cite{ruhl2015measuring} observe that as modern societies grow more complex, legislative bodies enact new laws at a pace that vastly outpaces the capacity of human legal staff to manually review them for consistency. Within legal informatics and administrative science, this rapid accumulation generates \textit{regulatory debt}, formally defined as the compounding institutional burden of uncoordinated, obsolete, or overlapping legal enactments that accumulate when new measures are promulgated without harmonizing them against pre-existing statutory codes~\cite{ruhl2015measuring}. In local government administration, this phenomenon is particularly acute because local ordinances are rarely compiled into centralized, cross-referenced statutory codes~\cite{conchas2025digital}. Conchas and Salapa~\cite{conchas2025digital} emphasize that transforming historical local enactments into machine-readable data presents severe engineering hurdles because records remain scattered across disparate administrative formats across both codified ordinances and transitory executive orders.

        Consequently, the statutory search space for identifying normative conflicts constitutes a fragmented, multi-generational archive rather than a structured relational database. Zhong et al.~\cite{zhong2020how} characterize this challenge as a dynamic consistency problem, wherein governing facts and regulatory obligations continually shift across successive council terms. Because local statutory data lacks centralized indexing, local government agencies are forced into a reactive posture, discovering statutory preemption defects only after an invalid measure is challenged in court. This systemic backlog motivates the transition in legal informatics from rigid, rule-based systems toward neural language models capable of learning semantic representations directly from unstructured text~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Such automated systems are required to process extensive legislative corpora that exceed the working memory and analytical throughput of human researchers.

        \subsubsection{Limits of Manual Review}
        In contemporary legal informatics, digitizing physical documents into electronic archives no longer represents the primary operational constraint; rather, the central hurdle is auditing whether newly introduced provisions logically align with governing positive law. As legislative throughput accelerates, manual review workflows become overwhelmed by the cognitive burden of cross-referencing multi-volume statutory digests. A primary challenge for human reviewers is distinguishing between beneficial regulatory overlap (where multiple enactments address adjacent dimensions of a public policy issue) and destructive statutory conflict (where an enactment inadvertently contradicts a superior mandate). Recent surveys confirm that conventional keyword-based search platforms consistently fail to capture these subtle legal distinctions~\cite{ariai2025natural}. Consequently, latent statutory contradictions frequently remain undetected throughout the drafting lifecycle, shifting the burden of conflict discovery from legislative staff during pre-enactment review to judicial authorities during adversarial litigation~\cite{ariai2025natural, scp1994magtajasvpryce}.

        \subsubsection{Semantic Ambiguity}
        Traditional search systems rely on exact character matching, operating on the premise that legal concepts can be retrieved by searching for specific vocabulary strings. However, natural legal language exhibits pervasive \textit{semantic ambiguity} driven by two primary linguistic phenomena: \textit{synonymy} (where different words express the same legal concept, such as \textit{prohibited} versus \textit{unlawful}) and \textit{polysemy} (where the same word assumes drastically different meanings depending on context, such as a \textit{party} referring to a litigant rather than a social gathering)~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue, Greco2023Bringing}. In statutory drafting, normative directives are governed by deontic modal verbs (such as \textit{shall}, \textit{must}, or \textit{may}) whose binding legal force cannot be determined by simple string matching. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} demonstrate that this syntactic ambiguity causes exact-match techniques to fail when processing complex legal tasks. For example, a local ordinance regulating ``motor vehicles'' and a national statute governing ``two-wheeled conveyances'' might address the same civic activity, yet a Boolean query for ``automobile'' will fail to retrieve either provision due to vocabulary divergence.

        Furthermore, legal consistency frequently depends on implied normative boundaries rather than explicit textual declarations. Katz et al.~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp} establish that identifying these subtle contradictions requires computational systems that evaluate logical relationships and contextual semantics rather than surface character overlap. Transformer-based language models address this limitation by projecting text sequences into high-dimensional vector spaces that capture deep contextual semantics~\cite{zheng2021when}. However, deploying neural architectures introduces an explainability trade-off. As Sovrano et al.~\cite{Sovrano2021Making} argue, while deep neural models achieve high classification accuracy, their uninspected internal activations function as opaque black boxes that fail to articulate the underlying legal reasoning behind a flagged conflict. In administrative and legislative settings, articulating the verifiable \textit{ratio decidendi} behind an alert is just as vital as the detection itself~\cite{savelka2021discovering}.

    \subsection{Local Conflict Detection}
    While international legal informatics benchmarks such as LexGLUE advance automated conflict detection across Western legal corpora, corresponding research within post-colonial developing jurisdictions like the Philippines remains in its nascent stages~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Since the passage of the Local Government Code of 1991, Philippine local government units have enacted thousands of local ordinances that remain largely unindexed in machine-readable formats~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. These decentralized enactments govern everyday commerce, public health, and zoning, yet lack standardized cross-referencing to national statutes. Although theoretical algorithms for conflict detection are established in mainstream artificial intelligence literature, their empirical application to localized Philippine statutory text remains largely unaddressed.

        \subsubsection{Philippine Legal NLP}
        Within domestic computer science, research applying Natural Language Processing to Philippine law has historically focused on administrative classification and text summarization rather than deductive logical reasoning. Early investigations used basic supervised classifiers to forecast appellate outcomes in Supreme Court decisions~\cite{virtucio2018predicting} or applied extractive algorithms to condense extensive jurisprudence~\cite{sagum2023philippine}. Recent studies in domestic legal informatics have explored transformer-based dense embedding architectures, which map text sequences into continuous mathematical vectors where geometric distance corresponds to topical relatedness~\cite{roque2024legal}. While dense embeddings significantly outperform traditional keyword searches in retrieving domain-specific statutes (such as tax regulations), they remain fundamentally correlational: they identify that two provisions address the same subject, but lack the deductive reasoning layers required to determine whether the two provisions contradict each other.

        Analyzing these research patterns reveals a pronounced structural gap in local literature. The Philippine NLP community has automated the categorization and retrieval of national statutory text, while also showing that inference models can resolve semantic contradictions in local languages for general-domain tasks such as online fact-checking~\cite{virtucio2018predicting, sagum2023philippine, roque2024legal, nunez2026pomi}. However, national statutory retrieval benchmarks do not account for the decentralized drafting practices and archival noise found in subnational legislation. Extending these inference models to detect normative conflicts within the multi-generational ordinances of a Highly Urbanized City like Davao therefore remains unexplored.

        \subsubsection{Logic and Conflict Research}
        Within Philippine computational linguistics, Natural Language Inference (NLI) has been applied in adjacent domains such as detecting misinformation and rumors in low-resource text. Nuñez et al.~\cite{nunez2026pomi} demonstrated that transformer architectures effectively resolve directional logical contradictions across Philippine English, Tagalog, and code-switched Taglish claims. In local government record management, however, document indexing still relies predominantly on Boolean keyword matching and rigid rule-based heuristics. Such keyword-based retrieval fails when enactments use synonymous terms (such as ``firecrackers'' versus ``pyrotechnic devices'') or when historical scans contain optical character recognition (OCR) noise~\cite{lopresti2008optical}; in Philippine text processing, Naguio and Roxas~\cite{naguio2024hybrid} similarly demonstrate that purely rule-based approaches struggle with high lexical variation unless paired with neural representations. Relying on static rules or manual keyword queries to isolate statutory contradictions across thousands of local ordinances is therefore impractical for human researchers.

    
        \subsubsection{Research Gaps}
        While the Philippine AI community has automated the search and summarization of national laws, a significant gap remains in applying deep inference models to audit local ordinances. Existing studies indicate that this gap persists because subnational legislation is substantially more challenging for automated systems to process than national statutory compilations. In particular, four interrelated barriers hinder automated local ordinance auditing: the absence of curated local datasets, limited workstation hardware inside local government offices, multilingual local phrasing, and institutional distrust of opaque neural predictions. Addressing these four barriers defines the design space for practical ex-ante legislative screening.

        The primary structural barrier to local legislative auditing is the critical dataset gap, characterized by the complete absence of curated ground-truth datasets for Philippine local enactments. Unlike national statutes that benefit from centralized repositories, local ordinances represent low-resource textual environments~\cite{ranathunga2022some}. Standard legal NLP models are trained on massive Western corpora like LexGLUE, but there are no equivalent standardized benchmarks providing annotated pairs of conflicting local ordinances~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis, nrcp2024review}. Without a standardized benchmark to measure progress, local legal informatics research has historically struggled to move beyond superficial keyword search to deductive logical reasoning~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}.

        Compounding the lack of training data is a pronounced computational resource gap within Local Government Units (LGUs). Deductive reasoning models, particularly deep cross-encoders, demand substantial compute and memory resources that local government offices typically do not possess. Studies on Philippine public administration indicate that local digital adoption remains constrained by limited funding, scarce machine learning personnel, and legacy office desktop hardware~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}. In a local legislative council readiness study, only 20\% of council members had received formal ICT training and 40\% owned devices suitable for digital work, with offices continuing to rely on paper-based workflows and basic text editors rather than structured databases~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}. This lack of digital infrastructure makes it difficult to justify complex models that would be too computationally demanding to execute on actual LGU workstations~\cite{huangetal2025integrated}.

        This computational disparity is directly reflected in the hardware profiles of local administrative offices. Under Commission on Audit guidelines (COA Circular No.~2022-004), government agencies classify tangible ICT items costing below \textpeso 50,000.00 as semi-expendable property~\cite{coa2022circular004}, and public-sector ICT procurement bidding documents for standard office workstations specify Intel Core i3 or Core i5 processors with 8~GB of DDR4 system memory and no discrete graphics accelerators~\cite{dict2020specs}. Combined with local ICT infrastructure and training deficits~\cite{gabuya2025towardsdigitallegis}, local legislative offices typically operate modest, CPU-bound office workstations. Because standard local government computers lack dedicated tensor-processing hardware, deploying multi-billion-parameter autoregressive large language models locally is computationally and financially unviable, while cloud-hosted commercial APIs introduce recurring subscription costs that exceed local operating budgets. Consequently, practical ex-ante conflict detection for local governance requires lightweight, offline-capable architectures that execute within consumer-grade 8~GB CPU-only workstation limits.

        At the textual level, local legislative conflict detection faces a complex sociolinguistic gap. Local ordinances exhibit higher linguistic heterogeneity than national statutes because local drafting operates within a multilingual environment where formal legal English coexists with Cebuano and Filipino expressions~\cite{dreisbach2021language}. Cebuano vocabulary itself incorporates Spanish-derived loanwords that have undergone semantic shifts over time~\cite{maxilom2008semantic}, creating lexical variation that monolingual English tokenizers may misinterpret in localized records. While recent studies confirm that neural inference models can parse regional expressions in informal text, extending this capacity to dense, multi-generational local enactments requires domain adaptation that remains largely unexplored in local literature~\cite{citygov2025lissp, Greco2023Bringing}.

        Finally, the slow adoption of computational consistency checking is driven by an institutional trust gap across the legal profession. As emphasized by Supreme Court Senior Associate Justice Marvic Leonen, the legal community remains cautious regarding artificial intelligence because conventional deep learning models function as opaque black boxes that cannot articulate their underlying reasoning~\cite{leonen2024keynote}. Legal researchers and legislative counsel express concern that automated tools could introduce subtle statutory errors and lead to the enactment of void legislation~\cite{conchas2025digital}. Consequently, academic research has historically favored low-risk document indexing and retrieval systems; as a result, deep semantic conflict detection remains largely unaddressed in local governance~\cite{ashley2017artificial}.

\section{Philippine Legislative Domain}
The Philippine legal system operates under a unitary constitutional framework, wherein Local Government Units (LGUs) possess purely delegated legislative powers conferred by Congress~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. This jurisdictional structure establishes a strict hierarchical subordination that bounds local lawmaking beneath national legislative authority. Any computational system engineered to audit local ordinances must operationalize these jurisdictional constraints. This section analyzes the statutory and structural conditions governing Philippine local law. First, it details the constitutional and statutory hierarchy subordinating local ordinances beneath national laws. Second, it traces the historical and stylistic roots of Philippine legal syntax. Third, it examines the parliamentary mechanics of the Three Readings legislative procedure during plenary sessions and the challenge of bilingual translation. Fourth, it contrasts ex-ante legislative auditing with ex-post judicial review, and finally reviews existing consistency checking methods to demonstrate the necessity of automated semantic auditing.

    \subsection{Hierarchical Constraints}
    The Philippine legal system operates under a unitary constitutional framework in which national laws strictly control subordinate local enactments~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. At the apex stands the 1987 Constitution, followed by national statutes enacted by Congress, international treaties, presidential executive issuances, and local ordinances. Under this constitutional structure, no Local Government Unit can enact measures that contradict an Act of Congress. The doctrine of national preemption establishes an absolute boundary: whenever the national legislature enacts comprehensive statutory standards governing a given regulatory domain, local governments are legally precluded from enacting conflicting or diluting local rules~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. Consequently, any automated conflict detection system for local drafting must reflect these structural rules subordinating local ordinances beneath national statutes.

    The primary organic charter governing local lawmaking is the Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No.~7160). Section~16 of the Code, known as the General Welfare Clause, delegates police powers to local legislative councils to enact ordinances promoting the health, safety, and general prosperity of their constituents, provided such enactments remain strictly within statutory boundaries~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. Because Davao City is classified as a Highly Urbanized City (HUC), it operates autonomously from provincial administrative supervision; its ordinances are therefore not reviewed by a provincial board. This administrative independence places the entire burden of ex-ante consistency auditing directly upon the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod before measures proceed to final enactment~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    The definitive judicial test governing the validity of local legislation is established by the Supreme Court in the landmark ruling \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corporation, Inc.}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}. To withstand judicial scrutiny, an ordinance must satisfy six foundational substantive requirements: it must not contravene the Constitution or any national statute, it must not be unfair or oppressive, it must not be partial or discriminatory, it must not prohibit but may regulate trade, it must be general and consistent with public policy, and it must not be unreasonable. Because local legislative bodies possess purely delegated authority, local councils cannot prohibit activities that national legislation has expressly authorized or regulated~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. This principle of statutory subordination forms the primary legal benchmark against which automated conflict detection systems must evaluate candidate local drafts.

    When a local ordinance impermissibly enters an area governed by national legislation, Philippine courts consistently apply the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine to declare the local measure invalid. In \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties Corporation, Inc.}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}, the City Council of Cagayan de Oro enacted ordinances prohibiting casino operations to protect public morals. The Supreme Court struck down the local enactments as \textit{ultra vires}, ruling that a local council cannot prohibit commercial gambling activities that a national statute expressly authorized through the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR) charter. Similar preemption disputes have directly affected Davao City legislation. In \textit{Mosqueda v. Pilipino Banana Growers \& Exporters Association, Inc.}~\cite{scp2016mosqueda}, the Supreme Court invalidated Davao City Ordinance No.~0309-07, which imposed a local ban on aerial pesticide spraying across agricultural plantations, holding that the ordinance exceeded local police powers and collided with the regulatory authority granted to the national Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority under Presidential Decree No.~1144. In \textit{City of Manila v. Laguio, Jr.}~\cite{scp2005manilavlaguio}, the Court likewise struck down an ordinance prohibiting motels and massage parlors, holding that local police powers cannot arbitrarily prohibit lawful commercial activities under the guise of zoning regulation.

    In contrast to vertical statutory preemption, local lawmaking also involves horizontal consistency, where newly proposed ordinances must align with existing, fellow local enactments. When drafting local legislation, the Sangguniang Panlungsod uses the Three-Reading legislative process, particularly the committee debates and line-by-line amendments during the Second Reading, to harmonize new proposals with older local enactments~\cite{spdc2022ra00122}. Unlike vertical conflicts that represent fatal legal defects, inconsistencies between a new draft and an older local ordinance frequently reflect intentional legislative updates, such as revising local tax rates or adjusting traffic routes. To manage these horizontal modifications systematically, legislative drafters rely on standardized legislative saving clauses established under Philippine local legislative practice~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}. Every formal ordinance incorporates an express or general repealing clause, declaring that all prior local enactments inconsistent with the newly approved measure are amended or repealed accordingly. Furthermore, ordinances feature a separability clause, which is a structural legal safeguard so that if any single section is declared invalid by judicial authorities, the unoffending sections remain in full legal force~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. However, because local legal researchers must review hundreds of active local enactments under strict legislative session schedules, subtle inconsistencies often go unnoticed during the Second Reading and proceed to final enactment.

    A fundamental design decision in legal informatics is delineating which positive legal instruments establish binding preemption standards. Under Article~VII, Section~17 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution and Book~III, Title~I, Chapter~2, Section~2 of the Administrative Code of 1987 (Executive Order No.~292)~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, Executive Orders (EOs) are acts of the President providing rules of a general or permanent character in implementation or execution of constitutional or statutory mandates. Unlike Supreme Court decisions (which function as \textit{ex-post} judicial dispute resolutions settling adversarial controversies under the doctrine of \textit{stare decisis} in Article~8 of the Civil Code), Executive Orders operate as \textit{ex-ante}, forward-looking statutory instruments possessing the full force and effect of law. Furthermore, during both the Martial Law period (1972--1986) and the Revolutionary Government under the Freedom Constitution (Proclamation No.~3, 1986--1987), the President exercised direct legislative powers through Executive Orders and Presidential Decrees to establish substantive national enactments that remain operative law today. Modern Executive Orders frequently establish binding nationwide regulatory standards, such as Executive Order No.~26 (s.~2017) establishing nationwide public smoking prohibitions or Executive Order No.~168 (s.~2014) creating the Inter-Agency Task Force for the Management of Emerging Infectious Diseases (IATF), that legally preempt conflicting local legislation. Excluding Executive Orders would therefore omit critical nationwide regulatory mandates that local ordinances are legally bound to respect.

    \subsection{Historical and Stylistic Heritage}
    The structural complexity of Philippine legislative text stems directly from the nation's historical experience as a mixed legal jurisdiction~\cite{agpalo2009statutory, cruz2007political}. Philippine jurisprudence combines continental civil law traditions with Anglo-American statutory common law conventions. Understanding why Philippine statutes and local ordinances are drafted in their characteristic syntax requires examining how these colonial and post-colonial traditions fused over more than a century of codification. Tracing that syntactic evolution clarifies why automated statutory parsers encounter long nested clauses and mixed terminology in local ordinances.

    During more than three centuries of Spanish rule, the Philippine legal system was rooted firmly in the Roman-Germanic civil law tradition~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. This continental tradition emphasized systematic codification, deductive legal reasoning, and broad normative principles organized into structured books, titles, and chapters. Foundational enactments, such as the Spanish Civil Code of 1889 and the Penal Code of 1870, relied on generalized legal mandates rather than granular factual definitions. Furthermore, this civilian heritage embedded a rich vocabulary of Latin doctrinal maxims into Philippine jurisprudence, including \textit{in pari materia} (construing related laws together), \textit{generalia specialibus non derogant} (a general law does not override a specific one), and \textit{ultra vires} (acts beyond legal authority)~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. Modern Philippine codes retain this civilian structure, expecting adjudicators to derive specific legal duties from comprehensive normative commands.

    Following the Treaty of Paris and the establishment of the American Insular Government (1900--1935), American legal administrators introduced common law statutory drafting habits without fully displacing the civilian codification base~\cite{cruz2007political}. Enacted by the Philippine Commission and later the Philippine Legislature, early statutes introduced formal drafting conventions borrowed directly from United States federal and state legislation. These conventions included explicit statutory title preambles, exhaustive definitions sections, severability clauses, and general repealing provisions~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. Significantly, American administrators introduced the practice of drafting extensive conditional provisos (introduced by the traditional formula ``\textit{Provided, that...}'' and ``\textit{Provided, further, that...}''), designed to restrict, qualify, or create exceptions to preceding operative clauses within the same section. The Revised Administrative Code of 1917 (Act No.~2711) and the contemporary Administrative Code of 1987 (Executive Order No.~292) solidified this mixed tradition; these codes established English as the controlling statutory language while maintaining civil law institutional categories~\cite{philgov1987eo292}.

    This dual heritage produces a legislative drafting style marked by high syntactic density and multi-clause complexity. Unlike standard conversational English, a single Philippine statutory provision often places multiple dependent clauses, qualifying prepositional phrases, and recursive proviso exceptions into a solitary grammatical sentence~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}. At the local level, legislative staff adopt this formal national drafting template while adding procedural boilerplate; as a result, verbose local ordinances often separate the operative regulatory command from its governing subject across long spans of intervening text~\cite{spdc2022ra00122}. From an analytical and document processing perspective, this hybrid syntax presents substantial structural hurdles: archaic legal terms resist straightforward vocabulary normalization, long-distance dependencies obscure the core legal directive, and qualifying provisos invert the practical meaning of preceding clauses unless the text is examined in its full structural context.

    \subsection{Plenary Legislative Procedure}
    Besides the hierarchy of laws, the actual parliamentary mechanism through which laws are drafted, debated, and approved determines how statutory text evolves over time. In the Philippine legal architecture, positive law enactment is governed by a mandatory three-reading procedure conducted during formal plenary sessions of the legislative body. At the national level, Article~VI, Section~26, Paragraph~2 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution explicitly dictates that no bill passed by either the House of Representatives or the Senate shall become law unless it has passed three readings on separate days, with printed copies in final form distributed to members at least three days prior to passage~\cite{philconst1987, philhouse2022rules}. At the local government level, Sections~53 and 54 of the Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No.~7160)~\cite{philgov1991ra7160} and the Internal Rules of Procedure of the 20th Sangguniang Panlungsod of Davao City (Resolution No.~001-22)~\cite{spdc2022ra00122} replicate this parliamentary doctrine. Under these statutory rules, every proposed municipal or city ordinance must undergo three distinct readings before the full assembly in plenary session. This parliamentary mechanism is designed to guarantee institutional transparency and deliberate democratic accountability before any regulatory constraint attains the force of law.

    The legislative progression begins on the plenary floor with the First Reading. In both national congressional chambers and the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod, the First Reading is strictly ministerial and introductory: the presiding officer directs the Secretary to read solely the assigned measure number, title, and the name of the author or proponent before the assembled plenary body~\cite{philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. Under parliamentary rules, no substantive debate, interpellation, or floor amendment is permitted during First Reading. Instead, the presiding officer refers the proposed measure immediately to the appropriate standing committee (such as the Committee on Rules, Laws, and Ordinances), initiating the inter-session committee review phase. In this committee stage, technical working groups, legal counsel, and committee researchers examine the draft, hold public hearings, and prepare the formal committee report. Because local ordinance drafting is decentralized, with individual council members authoring distinct proposals rather than relying on a centralized drafting bureau~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}, drafting conventions and legal terminology vary widely. Legal researchers bear the heavy burden of manually cross-referencing proposed clauses against thousands of national statutes and prior city enactments under strict legislative session schedules. When staff lack automated semantic search tools, potential preemption defects and horizontal contradictions frequently survive committee scrutiny undetected.

    Following committee review, the proposed ordinance returns to the plenary floor for the Second Reading, which represents the primary substantive and deliberative phase of the legislative lifecycle. The Second Reading commences with a formal sponsorship speech delivered by the committee chair or measure proponent to summarize the policy objectives and legal basis of the enactment. Immediately following sponsorship, the plenary assembly enters the Period of Interpellation and General Debate. During this period, colleagues interrogate the sponsor, probe statutory ambiguities, question fiscal or regulatory feasibility, and raise substantive legal objections regarding whether the measure contravenes superior national legislation. Once general debate is formally terminated, the body transitions into the Period of Amendments, where committee amendments and individual floor amendments are presented, debated, and voted upon section by section~\cite{philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. This period of amendments is the decisive procedural window where proposed clauses are actively reshaped, modified, or deleted to align with governing law.

    The Period of Interpellation and Amendments on Second Reading constitutes the exact procedural nexus where an automated semantic conflict detection system delivers its highest institutional utility. Because the Second Reading is the sole phase on the plenary floor where substantive textual alterations, statutory reconciliations, and corrective rewrites can be introduced, legal researchers and sponsors require granular, evidence-backed conflict diagnostics before and during floor deliberations. If a draft ordinance clause encroaches upon a national statutory domain (such as violating the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine by attempting to prohibit what national law permits, or exceeding statutory penalty caps under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160), an automated screening tool can pinpoint the conflicting statutory premise, flag contradictory deontic terms, and provide an explainable audit trail. Equipping Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers with these findings allows them to draft precise, legally sound floor amendments during the Period of Amendments, rectifying latent preemption flaws before the measure advances past the amendable phase.

    After the assembly adopts all floor amendments and finalizes the draft on Second Reading, the measure is engrossed, printed in its final form, and scheduled for Third Reading in plenary session. In stark contrast to the dynamic debates of Second Reading, the Third Reading is procedurally rigid and unmodifiable. Under Article~VI, Section~26, Paragraph~2 of the 1987 Constitution and corresponding local Sanggunian rules, no amendment whatsoever is permitted upon the Third Reading of a bill or ordinance~\cite{philconst1987, philhouse2022rules, spdc2022ra00122}. Plenary action is confined strictly to a nominal roll-call vote, where each member registers an affirmative, negative, or abstaining vote, accompanied by a brief oral explanation. This procedural finality creates severe institutional risk: if a statutory collision is not discovered and rectified prior to or during the Second Reading, the flawed provision is permanently locked into positive law upon Third Reading approval. The enacted ordinance becomes vulnerable to immediate judicial nullification under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine, administrative disputes before national executive departments, or citizen litigation. This structural irreversibility demonstrates why ex-ante computational conflict detection is essential during committee review and Second Reading preparation.

    Compounding the procedural complexities of the Three Readings is the statutory mandate requiring local legislation to be accessible across languages. Under Section~469 of the Local Government Code, the Secretary to the Sanggunian is mandated to translate approved ordinances into local dialects, such as Cebuano for Davao City~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122, dreisbach2021language}. However, this creates legal friction because the Administrative Code of 1987 establishes that the English statutory text strictly controls in cases of conflict~\cite{philgov1987eo292}. If an error occurs in regional translation, a citizen relying on the Cebuano text may inadvertently violate the authoritative English version; this disparity conflicts with the public notice doctrine in \textit{Tañada v. Tuvera}~\cite{sc1985tanadavtuvera}. Despite policy initiatives such as the proposed \textit{Batas sa Sariling Wika} legislative measure (House Bill No.~3863)~\cite{reganit2025diokno}, subtle dialect differences often produce semantic drift where the exact legal obligation in English is distorted in regional vernacular text~\cite{maxilom2008semantic}. Consequently, consistency checking systems must operate primarily on the authoritative English statutory provisions while accounting for regional linguistic artifacts generated during local committee debates and drafting iterations.

    \subsection{Ex-Ante Legislative Review}
    The term ``ex-ante'' derives from Latin meaning ``before the event,'' and in legislative informatics and administrative jurisprudence it denotes the evaluation of a proposed law or local regulation prior to official enactment~\cite{fried2015exanteexpost}. This prospective auditing process aims to identify and remediate potential statutory collisions, constitutional infirmities, and unintended regulatory burdens while the draft remains amenable to revision. In the Philippines, ex-ante regulatory review gained heightened institutional prominence under Republic Act No.~11032, the Ease of Doing Business and Efficient Government Service Delivery Act of 2018~\cite{philippines2018ra11032}. Under this statutory framework, the Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA) alongside the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG) mandates that Local Government Units conduct a formal Regulatory Impact Assessment (RIA) for proposed regulations impacting commerce and public welfare~\cite{philippines2018ra11032}. The objective is to verify that proposed ordinances possess valid statutory authority and do not impose duplicative or contradictory requirements on citizens and businesses.

    The fundamental distinction between ex-ante and ex-post evaluation lies in timing, methodology, and legal impact across the hierarchy of legal instruments. Ex-ante evaluation focuses strictly on prospective, forward-looking legislative texts (such as draft local ordinances, pending congressional bills, and proposed executive issuances) prior to their passage into positive law. In contrast, ex-post evaluation operates retrospectively through judicial dispute resolution, encompassing adversarial litigation, appellate court rulings, and Supreme Court jurisprudence governed by \textit{stare decisis}. While ex-post judicial adjudication rectifies constitutional injuries after harm has occurred, it incurs substantial societal and litigation expenses, as repealing an active ordinance requires protracted court proceedings~\cite{gesteletal2011exanteevaluation}. Ex-ante auditing is therefore substantially more efficient, protecting local governments from administrative deadlock and judicial invalidation.

    Deploying automated computational tools for ex-ante conflict detection empowers local legislative researchers to identify normative collisions during the drafting stage. Because legislative bodies generate extensive statutory volumes, manual review frequently fails to catch subtle inconsistencies between new drafts and existing laws~\cite{ruhl2015measuring}. By operating as an automated conflict-screening tool, computational systems flag potential preemption conflicts while the measure remains in committee deliberation. This early screening facilitates alignment with superior national statutes and reduces the risk of enacting local measures that are legally void \textit{ab initio}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}.

    \subsection{Legislative Consistency Checking}
    Because local governments hold subordinate legislative authority within a decentralized drafting environment, systematic consistency checking represents an essential institutional safeguard. Verifying that an ordinance does not contradict superior statutes requires far more than title matching; it demands attention to legal semantics and jurisdictional boundaries. Current consistency checking methods remain constrained by syntactic ambiguity and reliance on basic document tracking platforms. Addressing these limitations requires tools capable of inspecting substantive provisions across both local and national repositories.

    Discourse analysis of Philippine statutory language reveals pervasive ambiguity in how normative keywords and modalities are deployed~\cite{scp1970villacortavbernardo}. In particular, the modal auxiliary ``shall'' introduces frequent interpretive friction, as it may denote an absolute command, an administrative directive, or a directory permission depending on statutory context. Empirical studies confirm that conjunctions such as ``and/or'' and unclear modifier placement create additional syntactic ambiguity even when enactments adhere to formal drafting guidelines~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}. These linguistic hazards make surface-level keyword checks unreliable for verifying regulatory compliance.

    Philippine jurisprudence recognizes that statutory conflicts rarely present themselves through explicit textual declarations~\cite{cruz2007political}. A local ordinance virtually never declares an overt intent to contradict an Act of Congress; rather, conflicts typically arise as implied preemption. This occurs when a national statute occupies a regulatory field, and a local council introduces unauthorized preconditions or local fees that restrict activities already licensed by the state~\cite{scp1970villacortavbernardo}. In such cases, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the local measure impermissibly modifies national law under the guise of police power regulation. For manual reviewers and keyword-based search tools, detecting implied preemption is exceptionally difficult because the conflicting enactments employ disparate vocabularies; uncovering the contradictory regulatory effect demands deep semantic analysis.

    In the local context, the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod processes extensive legislative volume, passing nearly 400 ordinances and over 2,000 resolutions within a single three-year council term~\cite{patumbon2025landmark}. To manage this throughput, the city deploys administrative platforms: the Resolution Ordinance Tracking System (ROTS) manages document routing and metadata, while the Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP) enables council members to access digitized enactments~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. However, while these digital systems streamline document logistics, substantive consistency auditing across provisions remains entirely manual. Furthermore, archival scans frequently contain optical character recognition (OCR) noise, which causes keyword search queries to miss critical governing provisions~\cite{lopresti2008optical}.

\section{Digital Legislative Archives}
Building a functional Semantic Conflict Detection system requires access to comprehensive and machine-readable legal corpora. In the Philippines, the digital landscape of legislative data is highly fragmented, split between national statutory repositories and localized city archives. This structural division presents significant technical hurdles for statutory data ingestion. Before deep logical inference can occur, automated auditing pipelines must first secure reliable data streams from these disparate legal environments. Consequently, understanding the specific characteristics of these sources informs the design of statutory retrieval and preprocessing methodologies.

   \subsection{National Sources}
   National legal repositories represent the authoritative apex of the Philippine legislative hierarchy and house laws that universally preempt local ordinances. These databases are maintained by centralized government agencies and contain highly formalized texts such as Republic Acts, Commonwealth Acts, and Batas Pambansa. They form the primary knowledge base for external legal context and supply the authoritative baseline rules against which local drafts are evaluated. Because national legislation undergoes a standardized formal drafting process, the vocabulary within these repositories is generally consistent and adheres to strict legal drafting standards. Accessing these national databases provides computational systems with the necessary macroscopic view of state-level mandates and prohibitions.

   The Official Gazette is the constitutionally mandated repository for Philippine statutes and the primary journal of record for national legislative text~\cite{officialgazette}. It publishes all newly enacted national laws, presidential executive orders, and administrative issuances, as required by Republic Act No.~453~\cite{act453_1902} and Commonwealth Act No.~638~\cite{ca638_1941}. Because it contains the definitive, legally binding version of a statute, any computational system evaluating local statutory preemption must anchor its baseline rules to this repository. This archive forms the authoritative historical ledger for state-level mandates; it preserves the exact phrasing approved by the legislative and executive branches. Consequently, it remains the most critical target for establishing an authoritative statutory knowledge base.

   Complementing the official journal, developers use the Open Congress API provided by the House of Representatives Legislative Information System (LEGIS) to access structured, machine-readable metadata regarding legislative routing and statutory amendments~\cite{hrep2023legis}. This programmatic interface tracks a statute's formal lifecycle, charting its progression from initial committee filing to final executive approval. By providing standardized output formats, the API allows computational pipelines to establish relational dependencies between different legislative acts without manually parsing unstructured text. Combining structured legislative metadata with full-text statutory collections helps automated systems trace active rules from superseded enactments.

   Finally, the Lawphil Project functions as an extensive, privately maintained digital repository of statutory records and jurisprudential decisions~\cite{lawphil2000}. Unlike official government channels that frequently change web templates, this repository provides a comprehensive, pre-digitized plaintext corpus of historical and contemporary Philippine law. This accessibility makes it well suited for the unsupervised pre-training and vectorization of language models tailored to the local legal domain. By ingesting this large corpus of clean text, NLP architectures learn the specialized syntactic structures and deontic operators inherent in Philippine jurisprudence and acquire the domain vocabulary required to process statutory drafting~\cite{zheng2021when}.

   \subsection{Local Sources}
   In contrast to centralized national databases, local legal repositories are maintained independently by individual Local Government Units (LGUs). These archives house local ordinances, resolutions, and executive orders that govern specific geographic jurisdictions. The legislative output at this tier is entirely decentralized; as a result, archiving practices vary widely depending on each city council's technological capacity. These local databases form the immediate target corpus for local conflict detection and contain the actual regulatory constraints imposed on city residents. Consequently, they represent a high-volume, dynamic data environment that continuously expands as the local council holds regular drafting sessions.

   The local corpus is anchored upon the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod portal, the official digital repository for local legislative outputs~\cite{davaocity_sp_dept}. This portal preserves the primary historical record for city regulations, resolutions, and zoning policies enacted within the city's jurisdiction. It represents a dynamic and continuously expanding text environment that directly reflects the decentralized nature of local lawmaking. The texts housed here encapsulate the specific regulatory constraints and policy directions of the city government and supply the ordinance text needed to evaluate new drafts for logical consistency against the existing legislative framework.

   To manage the extensive volume of local legislation, the city introduced the Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP) as its primary administrative backbone~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. This platform is a digital tracking system designed to maintain metadata regarding ordinance routing, committee assignments, and plenary approval statuses. The system provides transparency for monitoring ordinances and resolutions in real time~\cite{patumbon2025lissp}. By digitizing the legislative workflow, LISSP operates as a centralized database of ordinances, providing the structured tracking and contextual data necessary for evaluating the lifecycle of local laws, while addressing the interoperability challenges identified in recent regional e-governance assessments~\cite{conchas2025digital}.

    \subsection{Data Extraction Challenges}
    While these repositories contain the necessary legal knowledge, extracting statutory text for computational analysis presents severe technical hurdles. Acquiring and standardizing records across national and local platforms exposes downstream pipelines to noise and structural inconsistencies along two main dimensions. First, many archived documents exist only as scanned page images that suffer from optical character recognition (OCR) degradation. Second, incomplete public listings and authenticated internal portals isolate full-text local records from automated collection.

        \subsubsection{Archival Scanning and OCR Degradation}
        A primary barrier to automated statutory auditing is the lack of native, machine-readable text across archival collections. Both the Official Gazette and the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod public archive predominantly host scanned, image-based PDFs rather than selectable digital documents. Archived scans in these collections frequently exhibit visual noise such as slanted pages, faded ink, low contrast, and compression artifacts. As Lopresti~\cite{lopresti2008optical} documents, such noisy visual data results in severe Optical Character Recognition (OCR) degradation, where standard extraction tools produce broken alphanumeric strings and nonsensical tokens. These irregularities suggest that a direct ingestion pipeline suffers from semantic fragmentation, requiring an additional high-precision extraction layer and extensive text-cleaning heuristics to ensure data viability.

        The resulting character and word-level corruptions severely hinder automated semantic analysis. In statutory conflict detection, a single corrupted character can alter the legal interpretation of a provision, such as transforming a negative operator into an affirmative assertion or scrambling a penalty amount. Standard tokenizers struggle to process these degraded sequences, fragmenting broken words into out-of-vocabulary sub-tokens that dilute contextual representations~\cite{rust2021how}. Furthermore, scanning artifacts frequently obscure section demarcations and legislative numbering; this prevents standard parsers from isolating self-contained statutory units. Consequently, automated preemption pipelines must incorporate noise-tolerant document normalization to restore structural integrity before downstream retrieval.

        \subsubsection{Repository Scarcity and Digital Isolation}
        Another critical challenge is archival fragmentation, wherein legislative repositories suffer from systemic gaps in their digital records. This poses a significant problem for high-fidelity auditing because the absence of even a single related ordinance can lead to false negatives in conflict detection, where a system declares a draft valid simply because it cannot access the contradicting record. A prominent example is the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod public archive, which features intermittently missing ordinance files and unorganized chronological listings that hinder systematic data collection~\cite{spdavao2026}. Even relatively complete national databases like Lawphil can lack specific Republic Acts or historical amendments. As Westermann et al.~\cite{Westermann2019computer} observe, fragmented legal databases force developers to move beyond simple scraping toward fault-tolerant data engineering to ensure an exhaustive corpus.

        A further obstacle in statutory data acquisition is the discrepancy between the theoretical and actual accessibility of local legislative databases. Although administrative systems like Davao City's LISSP are described in public reports as transparency portals~\cite{citygov2025lissp, patumbon2025lissp}, public access to full-text enactments remains gated behind authenticated administrative interfaces. This computational isolation restricts automated extraction by public web scrapers and requires administrative clearance and manual provisioning for full-text access. As Conchas and Salapa~\cite{conchas2025digital} observe, isolated administrative data often exists as digitally orphaned artifacts, where metadata about an enactment is disconnected from its full-text searchable content, requiring specialized ingestion pipelines to reconcile sparse public metadata with offline full-text documents.


\section{Exact and Symbolic Methods}
Before the adoption of neural networks, computational legal analysis relied heavily on deterministic systems to process text. Deterministic systems operate on predefined rule matrices, wherein a given input produces reproducible outputs without probabilistic inference or statistical estimation. Traditional methods for searching and evaluating legal information assumed that legislative ideas could be cleanly mapped either to specific vocabulary strings or to rigid, hand-coded logic formulas. While highly transparent and easy to debug, these foundational approaches frequently fail when forced to detect hidden conflicts in complex, unstructured legal texts. The core issue is that human language is inherently messy, and rigid systems break down when faced with the ambiguous phrasing typical of local ordinances.

    \subsection{Boolean Information Retrieval}
    Evaluating why legacy legal repositories struggle with semantic conflict detection requires examining how deterministic retrieval architectures process text at the machine level. Boolean Information Retrieval (BIR) represents the foundational search architecture underlying traditional legal databases. BIR operates on the bag-of-words assumption, abstracting statutory enactments into unordered multisets of terms while discarding grammatical syntax, clause order, and relational context. At its core, the architecture relies on an \textit{inverted index}, a mapping that pairs every distinct vocabulary term with a postings list recording document identifiers and term frequencies. Because an inverted index registers solely the discrete presence or absence of literal character strings, the retrieval engine cannot infer latent semantic relationships, grammatical qualifications, or underlying regulatory intent.
    
    In operational search engines, the retrieval mechanism in BIR is strictly based on set theory and formal Boolean logic. A user constructs a query using logical operators like \texttt{AND} ($\land$), \texttt{OR} ($\lor$), and \texttt{NOT} ($\neg$) to create intersecting sets of documents, as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:boolean_query}:

    \noindent\textit{Boolean Query Set Intersection Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
        Q = (\text{fireworks} \lor \text{pyrotechnics}) \land \neg \text{exempt}
        \label{eq:boolean_query}
        \eqcaption{Boolean Query Set Intersection Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where the compound query $Q$ combines disjunctive keyword sets under conjunction with a negated exemption condition. The system retrieves documents by calculating the exact mathematical intersection of these sets to pull only the files that satisfy every condition simultaneously. If a document contains the specific string of characters requested, it is retrieved and scored as a 1; if it misses even a single required term, it is ignored and scored as a 0. As a result, BIR evaluates the raw presence of strings while ignoring conceptual meaning and legal intent~\cite{blair1985an}.

        The primary limitation of BIR in legal auditing is its absolute reliance on exact string matching. This rigidity creates a severe ``vocabulary mismatch'' problem when processing natural language. Human language features synonymy, where entirely different words express the same meaning, and polysemy, where one word takes on different meanings depending on context. In statutory drafting, one author might write ``prohibited'' while another writes ``unlawful'' to express the same legal rule. Because BIR operates on character-level matching rather than conceptual mapping, it cannot link synonymous expressions and leaves large blind spots in search results.
        
        A landmark empirical evaluation of these systems demonstrated this vocabulary gap in a practical litigation setting. Legal researchers conducting queries using standard BIR systems confidently estimated they were finding over 75 percent of the relevant documents for a given case. They experienced an illusion of effectiveness because the retrieved documents looked highly accurate, which masked the large volume of missed relevant files. In reality, the system retrieved less than 20 percent of the actual relevant files contained within the database~\cite{blair1985an}. The researchers missed the remaining 80 percent simply because the authors of those hidden documents used slightly different, valid phrasing that did not align with the strict search query~\cite{opijnen2017on}.

        In a local government context like Davao City, this margin of error is administratively unacceptable. Missing even one existing ordinance during an ex-ante review due to a vocabulary mismatch could result in the passage of an invalid, conflicting law. Furthermore, legislative vocabulary evolves over time as drafting conventions change. An ordinance passed in the 1990s and a modern draft written in 2026 might regulate the same local activity while sharing no common keywords. Recent studies emphasize that resolving this gap requires neural transformer models that replace exact string matching with continuous vector spaces capable of linking distinct expressions to the same legal concept~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    \subsection{Symbolic AI}
    Because BIR fails to capture underlying meaning, early artificial intelligence researchers attempted to automate legal reasoning by directly modeling the rules of law themselves. This approach, widely known as Symbolic AI or ``Good Old-Fashioned AI'' (GOFAI), operates on the premise that human cognition can be replicated by manipulating formal symbols~\cite{vellino1986artificial}. Symbolic AI formalizes legal reasoning by modeling statutory constraints into explicit, hand-coded production rules. Rather than searching unstructured text strings, Symbolic AI translates legislative commands into formal deontic and first-order logic representations so that computational engines can execute deductive reasoning over regulatory relationships.

    To execute this logic, symbolic systems use propositional or first-order logic combined with deontic operators. Deontic logic is a branch of mathematical philosophy that evaluates normative modalities governing conduct: obligations ($\mathcal{O}$, mandatory actions that must be performed), permissions ($\mathcal{P}$, discretionary actions that may be undertaken), and prohibitions ($\mathcal{F}$, unlawful actions that must not be performed). For example, a local zoning prohibition can be expressed mathematically as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:first_order_deontic}:

    \noindent\textit{First-Order Deontic Prohibition Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \forall x (\text{Business}(x) \land \text{ResidentialZone}(x) \rightarrow \text{Prohibited}(x))
        \label{eq:first_order_deontic}
        \eqcaption{First-Order Deontic Prohibition Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where the universal quantifier ($\forall$) asserts that for any entity $x$, if it is classified as a business ($\land$) and located in a residential zone, it is prohibited ($\rightarrow$). By converting ordinances into these rigid formulas, a deductive engine can test whether two rules are logically incompatible. If Rule~A dictates $\mathcal{O}(X)$ (an obligation to perform $X$) and Rule~B dictates $\mathcal{F}(X)$ (a prohibition against performing $X$), the engine flags a formal logical contradiction ($\bot$)~\cite{athan2013legalruleml}. This formalization gives symbolic conflict checking exact deductive guarantees whenever statutory rules are fully and accurately encoded.

        While Symbolic AI provides transparent and mathematically sound reasoning, its reliance on manual programming makes it impractical to scale for real-world governance. The primary architectural barrier is the classical ``knowledge acquisition barrier'' in expert systems: human domain experts and software engineers must manually read, interpret, and translate thousands of unstructured city ordinances into rigid computer rules. This manual translation process is slow, expensive, and prone to human error during rule encoding~\cite{lucos2025neurosymbolic}. Moreover, whenever the city council amends an existing law, every affected symbolic formula must be manually rewritten; this manual requirement creates an unsustainable maintenance burden over time.

        Beyond the logistical challenge of manual coding, the rigid binary architecture of Symbolic AI clashes with what legal philosopher H.L.A.~Hart termed the \textit{open texture of law}~\cite{hart1961concept}. As Hart demonstrated in \textit{The Concept of Law}, statutory language cannot anticipate every future factual scenario; consequently, lawmakers deliberately employ open-ended, discretionary standards (such as mandating ``reasonable care,'' ``due diligence,'' or ``good moral conduct'') to allow adjudicators flexibility across changing societal contexts. These subjective legal standards cannot be reduced to binary IF-THEN rules because their meaning shifts depending on circumstance and judicial interpretation~\cite{ashley2017artificial}. While symbolic systems achieve deterministic precision in tightly constrained formal micro-environments, they fail when exposed to the linguistic variance, unformalized exceptions, and semantic ambiguities characteristic of local legislative archives~\cite{zhong2020how}.


    
\section{Graph-Based Architectures}
Because exact word matching can be unreliable, computational legal studies frequently explore graph-based architectures to model relationships between statutory provisions. Instead of treating legal texts as isolated character sequences, graph-based frameworks represent statutes as interconnected nodes within a structural network. By analyzing how enactments cite, amend, and conceptually reference one another, these architectures capture structural legal context that bag-of-words models invariably miss. Existing literature develops these graph representations at two main granularities: document- and provision-level citation networks on the one hand, and sentence-level semantic predicate graphs on the other.

    \subsection{Knowledge Graphs and GNNs}
    Legal Knowledge Graphs (LKGs) model statutory ecosystems by representing legal entities, provisions, and administrative bodies as structured vertices linked by directional, typed edges. In formal graph theory, a Legal Knowledge Graph models a statutory corpus as a directed, multi-relational graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$, wherein vertices $v \in \mathcal{V}$ represent discrete statutory enactments, administrative agencies, or substantive regulatory provisions, while edges $(u, r, v) \in \mathcal{E}$ designate formal juridical relationships $r \in \mathcal{R}$ such as statutory delegation, cross-reference citation, explicit amendment, or regulatory enforcement. Unlike flat text indices, these relational graphs enable Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to learn node embeddings by aggregating feature representations across local structural neighborhoods~\cite{ma2025gnn, yang2021legalgnn}. Researchers have developed automated frameworks like GNN-ERE to extract legal entities and relations directly from unstructured legal text and construct structured taxonomies without manual relational coding~\cite{ma2025gnn}. Furthermore, empirical evaluations demonstrate that graph-based representations improve legal document retrieval by mapping semantically related court judgments even in the absence of verbatim keyword overlap~\cite{harde2025lecnet}.

        \subsubsection{Graph Modeling and Multi-Hop Reasoning}
        The foundational strength of Graph Neural Networks lies in their ability to propagate structural context across multi-hop relational paths. In statutory interpretation, normative conflicts rarely occur through isolated direct collisions; rather, they frequently manifest indirectly across chains of administrative definitions and delegated authorities~\cite{yang2021legalgnn}. Formally, Graph Neural Networks execute iterative neighborhood aggregation (message passing), wherein the representation $\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}$ of node $v$ at layer $l$ is recursively updated by aggregating the latent feature vectors of its adjacent 1-hop topological neighbors. This inductive propagation allows the network to incorporate multi-hop structural context, evaluating whether an ordinance provision conflicts with a superior statutory mandate through intermediate administrative definitions or delegated authority chains. This topological awareness enables neural graph architectures to separate distinct structural roles and reasoning targets across heterogeneous legal entities~\cite{ma2025gnn}. Consequently, graph neural architectures provide a principled mechanism for structural reasoning across complex, multi-tiered statutory hierarchies.

        Furthermore, graph representations capture institutional relationships that remain hidden during sequential text reading. In decentralized governance frameworks like the Philippines, local legislative acts operate within a matrix of national agencies, provincial guidelines, and local zoning boundaries. Graph neural models learn relational node embeddings that reflect institutional relationships, so that algorithms can evaluate legal connections within a broader relational network~\cite{ma2025gnn}. By mapping shared regulatory targets across different enactments, the graph structure guides inference models toward relevant statutory clusters that standard search engines fail to connect. This relational capacity makes graph modeling an appealing conceptual paradigm for analyzing legislative coherence across decentralized jurisdictions.

        \subsubsection{Structural Vulnerabilities in Unstructured Archives}
        While Graph Neural Networks offer substantial theoretical utility, their practical deployment depends strictly on the completeness and fidelity of the underlying graph representation. A primary vulnerability is their reliance on explicit statutory cross-references to establish graph edges~\cite{humphreys2020populating}. In Philippine local archives, local ordinances frequently omit formal citations to the governing Republic Acts they inadvertently contradict; drafts rely instead on localized colloquial descriptions. When explicit relational links are missing from the initial graph construction, the GNN remains fundamentally blind to the underlying normative collision, unable to propagate message vectors across disconnected nodes.

        This limitation is exacerbated by systemic disconnection and physical degradation across local government records. City archives like Davao City's LISSP store enactments as isolated scanned documents rather than interconnected, machine-readable data nodes~\cite{conchas2025digital}. When automated relation extraction pipelines encounter noisy optical character recognition outputs, they generate hallucinated edges and misattributed entity linkages~\cite{lopresti2008optical}. These erroneous connections propagate through successive graph convolution layers, contaminating node representations and introducing false conflict signals throughout the network. Consequently, without expensive, manual human curation to verify every edge, graph-based systems prove too brittle for noisy, real-world local archives.

    \subsection{Abstract Meaning Representation}
    While Graph Neural Networks capture macro-level relationships between distinct legal documents, Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) evaluates the semantic and logical structure inside individual sentences. AMR represents the meaning of a sentence as a rooted, directed acyclic graph where nodes correspond to semantic concepts and edges represent semantic roles~\cite{banarescu2013abstract}. In legal informatics, AMR provides a formal representation of predicate-argument semantics, abstracting away superficial syntactic variations such as active versus passive voice~\cite{banarescu2013abstract, trong2018an}. For example, whether a statute declares that ``The City Mayor shall enforce the penalty'' or that ``The penalty shall be enforced by the City Mayor,'' AMR generates the identical semantic rooted graph, identifying the City Mayor as the agent (\texttt{:ARG0}) and the penalty as the patient (\texttt{:ARG1}). This semantic grounding allows computational systems to evaluate legal statements based on their core logical assertions rather than surface grammar.

        \subsubsection{Semantic Graph Representations and Role Labeling}
        A principal advantage of Abstract Meaning Representation in statutory analysis is its ability to isolate predicate-argument structures and modal distinctions within complex legislative commands. Statutory rules depend on deontic modalities (such as obligations, permissions, and prohibitions) to govern citizen behavior and administrative authority~\cite{agotnes2007on, humphreys2020populating}, while semantic parsing surveys note how predicate-argument representations capture modality alongside core event roles~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. Standard grammar parsers often conflate these modal verbs with general descriptive predicates; this conflation obscures the primary regulatory command. In contrast, AMR maps core predicates and modal distinctions explicitly into graph nodes and relations to identify who bears a mandatory obligation or restriction~\cite{trong2018an}. This structural clarity allows systems to identify subtle policy collisions, such as when a local ordinance authorizes a commercial practice that a national statute expressly forbids.

        \subsubsection{Sentence Boundary Constraints and Parser Degradation}
        Despite its structural precision, Abstract Meaning Representation faces severe operational limitations when applied to Philippine local legislation. The primary architectural constraint is AMR's restriction to single-sentence parsing~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}. In statutory drafting, preemption conflicts rarely manifest within a single isolated sentence; rather, normative contradictions typically emerge across distinct sections, such as a general mandate in one clause negated by an unprovided exception several paragraphs later. Because standard AMR parsers operate at the single-sentence level without cross-sentence coreference integration~\cite{tohidi2024abstract}, they struggle to connect distant provisos; this boundary limitation creates significant logical blindspots in statutory conflict detection.

        Compounding this sentence-level restriction is AMR's high sensitivity to complex statutory syntax and optical character recognition noise. Philippine statutory provisions, such as sections within Republic Act No.~7160~\cite{philgov1991ra7160} and related Republic Acts~\cite{bernardo2023disambiguating}, routinely pack deeply nested conditional clauses and multiple provisos within a single period. In an empirical evaluation on translated civil code provisions, Trong and Le~\cite{trong2018an} show that standard AMR parsers struggle with long, syntactically dense legal sentences and scanning or preprocessing artifacts, consistent with sequence-to-sequence AMR sensitivity analyses reported by van Noord and Bos~\cite{vannoord2017the}. Without extensive domain-specific parser retraining tailored to Philippine legal drafting, AMR cannot provide a dependable foundation for ex-ante local statutory auditing.

    \subsection{Statutory Citation Networks and Legislative Lineages}
    Beyond abstract sentence semantics, computational legal informatics models positive law through statutory citation networks and legislative lineage graphs. In democratic legal systems, statutory enactments do not exist in isolation; rather, they form an evolving web of cross-references and amendatory sequences governed by delegating authorities. Frameworks such as Akoma Ntoso~\cite{vitali2007towards} and LegalRuleML~\cite{palmirani2021legalruleml, athan2013legalruleml} formalize these relational structures and establish standardized metadata schemas for legislative drafting and normative rule representation. Mapping statutory relationships into explicit topological networks lets researchers trace how delegated authority flows from national parent codes down to local administrative bodies and how legislative modifications propagate across successive assemblies. Understanding both the empirical deployments and extraction trade-offs of these networks clarifies where graph modeling succeeds and where noisy local archives limit its coverage.

        \subsubsection{Statutory Network Deployments and Operational Granularity}
        Empirical applications of graph architectures in legal informatics span diverse statutory ecosystems and public sector repositories. In the European Union, the European Legislation Identifier (ELI) within the EUR-Lex database establishes a multi-relational metadata network connecting thousands of Directives and Regulations through explicit legal relations, such as legislative amendments and domestic transpositions. In United States statutory modeling, large-scale legal corpus initiatives incorporate tens of thousands of United States Code sections alongside regulatory filings for domain-specific tokenization and structural corpus analysis~\cite{bommarito2025kl3m, katz_2023_legal_nlp}. Similarly, judicial and statutory benchmark datasets such as LeCNet~\cite{harde2025lecnet} and Chinese Legal Knowledge Graphs~\cite{li2024construction} organize legal provisions and precedent citations into relational graphs to support structured legal reasoning and retrieval.

        A central architectural consideration in constructing legal knowledge graphs is selecting the operational unit of legal granularity. In legal text modeling, researchers have evaluated coarse document-level representations against fine-grained token and provision-level alternatives. Modeling statutory collections exclusively at the document level obscures the operative commands of positive law, aggregating complex enactments spanning hundreds of diverse articles into single undifferentiated nodes. A document-level graph can indicate that two enactments reference each other, but remains fundamentally blind to which specific administrative fee or licensing standard was modified. Conversely, attempting to construct graphs at the individual token or word level generates tens of millions of sparse, polysemous edges that lack substantive legal meaning, creating intractable computational graphs without isolating regulatory obligations.

        To balance computational tractability with normative precision, legal knowledge engineering commonly extracts normative elements at fine-grained provision level via semantic role labeling~\cite{humphreys2020populating}. This provision-level focus complements judgment-level citation networks such as LeCNet~\cite{harde2025lecnet}. In positive law, an individual numbered section represents the fundamental, self-contained normative command of a legislative act. A section connects the regulated actor and governing deontic operator with the substantive regulatory command, while preserving accompanying administrative exceptions. Organizing statutory representations at provision-level granularity isolates operative legal rules so that downstream graph or retrieval algorithms can evaluate legislative compliance at the rule level.

        \subsubsection{Relational Edge Typologies and Extraction Workflows}
        The construction of a statutory knowledge graph from unstructured legislative corpora requires a systematic extraction and entity resolution workflow. Because legislative drafting manuals enforce standardized statutory formulas, automated pipelines connect legal provisions through a multi-stage procedural sequence. In the initial stage, entity normalization algorithms scan legal texts to map surface variations (such as ``Republic Act No. 7160,'' ``R.A. 7160,'' and ``The Local Government Code of 1991'') to a single canonical entity identifier via standardized dictionary aliasing. Following normalization, high-precision regular expression patterns and structural parsers scan individual sections to extract explicit cross-references, instantiating directed vertical authority edges linking local provisions to governing national statutes.

        Following citation resolution, the pipeline parses amendatory directives to establish horizontal legislative lineages under the doctrine of \textit{lex posterior derogat legi priori}. Philippine legislative drafting manuals require statutory amendments to adopt standardized linguistic formulas, such as declaring that a specific prior section ``is hereby amended to read as follows'' or that an older enactment ``is hereby repealed in its entirety.'' Automated relation extraction algorithms identify these explicit amendatory formulas, generating directed modification edges linking amendatory enactments back to their historical parent sections. Assigning enactment dates to network vertices enables the graph engine to track point-in-time statutory validity, determining whether a referenced provision represents active positive law or an obsolete historical fragment. When scanning noise produces truncated numerical citations, the pipeline resolves candidates through dictionary matching against the national statutory gazette or isolates ambiguous fragments in diagnostic quarantine pools.

        Unlike homogeneous graphs that contain a single edge type, statutory networks function as heterogeneous multi-relational graphs $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$. Within this multi-relational topology, edge relations $r \in \mathcal{R}$ represent distinct juridical relationships, including structural containment connecting acts to sections, alongside authority delegations and amendatory sequences that modify positive law. Formalizing these diverse legal connections into typed edges enables graph traversal algorithms to evaluate legislative authority paths without conflating structural references with substantive amendments. Separating citation links from repeal or amendment links also prevents automated crawlers from treating superseded provisions as active statutory constraints.

        \subsubsection{Neuro-Symbolic and Graph-Augmented Transformers}
        While topological networks capture formal statutory relationships, pure graph representations exhibit known limitations when detecting preemption conflicts across unlinked enactments. In local legislative practice, draft ordinances frequently collide with superior national statutes without explicitly citing the governing legislation; drafts rely instead on localized terminology or administrative phrasing. Under these conditions, deterministic graph traversal fails because no explicit citation edge connects the conflicting provisions. This absence leaves the network topology blind to the underlying normative contradiction.

        To overcome this disconnection, contemporary legal NLP literature investigates neuro-symbolic and graph-augmented architectures that combine topological knowledge graphs with dense transformer models~\cite{li2024construction, ma2025gnn}. In these hybrid frameworks, the statutory knowledge graph provides structural priors, such as mapping governing legislative hierarchies and pruning obsolete repealed enactments. Concurrently, deep transformer encoders evaluate continuous cross-attention across candidate text spans, resolving semantic equivalence and deontic collisions even in the complete absence of formal citation edges. This synthesis establishes a complementary division of labor: symbolic graph topology enforces structural authority and legislative lineage, while continuous neural inference resolves unstructured textual contradictions.

        Within this neuro-symbolic interface, legal informatics literature explores two primary paradigms for integrating knowledge graph structure with transformer cross-encoders: \textit{Prompt-Conditioned Metadata Conditioning} and \textit{Graph-Guided Decision Thresholding}. Under prompt-conditioned metadata injection, structured graph node properties (such as jurisdictional level, governing macro-domain, and formal statutory titles) are serialized into standardized textual prefixes prepended directly to the input sequence (e.g., \texttt{[JURISDICTION: NATIONAL | DOMAIN: ...]} and \texttt{[JURISDICTION: LOCAL | DOMAIN: ...]}). Studies in legal document classification and natural language inference demonstrate that prepending structured metadata provides explicit contextual framing and allows transformer self-attention layers to condition token representations on institutional hierarchies without custom model architectures~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue, li2024construction}. At the same time, work on legal prompt variants~\cite{alshehri2026cross} observes that prompt structure and prefix tokens influence how attention and classification heads behave. When cross-encoders encounter prefix schemas that differ from their primary training data distribution, attention heads can become sensitive to contrasting lexical tokens in the prompt. This sensitivity reflects a recognized methodological trade-off between contextual guidance and input representation consistency.

        In parallel, an alternative design paradigm decouples graph-based legal representation~\cite{ma2025gnn} from post-hoc decision calibration based on Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) threshold analysis~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. Building on those two building blocks, this study formulates and evaluates a topological calibration scheme in which the original verbatim statutory sequence is preserved for neural inference while shortest-path geodesic distance $d_G(u, v)$ and authority hierarchy $H(u, v)$ modulate the post-softmax decision boundary $\tau^*(u, v)$. In statutory knowledge graphs containing typed authority and domain links, topological proximity between two provisions captures institutional closeness: enactments connected by direct citation links ($d_G = 1$) or shared macro-domains ($d_G = 2$) operate within a common regulatory framework. Separating linguistic cross-attention from relational graph traversal lets the cross-encoder evaluate pure textual semantics while graph topology adapts decision sensitivity according to structural distance. Together, these two paradigms test whether knowledge graph structure is captured more effectively within the model's linguistic input representation or along its operational decision boundary.

\section{Deep Learning and Transformers}
The application of Natural Language Processing (NLP) in the legal domain has shifted from rigid, rule-based systems and basic word-matching toward Deep Learning~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. Legacy algorithms treat legal texts as simple character strings, which frequently fail to capture the dense and interconnected nature of statutory drafting. Deep Learning, conversely, uses multi-layer artificial neural networks to map out the semantic meaning of legal language. This allows systems to process complex syntax and perform the deductive reasoning required to spot conflicts. This section examines the Transformer architecture, covering its underlying mechanics and computational constraints in auditing unstructured local archives.

    \subsection{Transformers and Self-Attention}
    A major breakthrough was the Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} in 2017. Prior neural sequence models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, processed text sequentially from left to right, one token at a time. This sequential dependency created severe computational delays during training and suffered from vanishing gradient dynamics over long sequences. As sequence length expanded, contextual information from earlier tokens attenuated, which hindered the model's capacity to resolve dependencies across multi-clause statutory provisions. 
    
    Transformers eliminate this sequential constraint through the \textit{Self-Attention} mechanism. Rather than reading words one by one, self-attention evaluates all tokens in an input sequence simultaneously in parallel. For every word in a provision, the mechanism computes a set of attention weights measuring how much every other word in the text contributes to its contextual meaning, regardless of physical distance in the sentence. For example, in a provision stating that ``Any licensee who operates without a valid health clearance shall be penalized,'' self-attention directly connects ``licensee'' to ``penalized'' across the intervening relative clause in a single computational step. Because it processes data in parallel, the Transformer architecture trains substantially faster on modern hardware and enabled foundational language models. Modern iterations introduce refinements like Disentangled Attention to represent token content and relative positional offsets separately, alongside Windowed Attention to process long-context legal documents. However, while generative Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at text summarization, they are prone to ``hallucinations'' (fabricating plausible but non-existent statutory citations or misinterpreting cross-references)~\cite{deroy2023how}. To maintain legal reliability, consistency auditing requires a discriminative approach that classifies whether two statutory provisions stand in a relationship of Entailment, Neutrality, or Contradiction, rather than probabilistically generating unverified text.

    \subsection{Transformer Challenges}
    Despite their architectural versatility, deploying transformer models within the computational constraints of Philippine Local Government Units (LGUs) introduces substantial operational hurdles. The foremost engineering constraint arises from quadratic memory scaling and context length limits. The standard self-attention mechanism scales quadratically ($O(n^2)$) with input sequence length $n$. Because computing pairwise attention scores requires constructing an explicit $n \times n$ attention matrix across all query-key token pairs, memory consumption scales quadratically with sequence length, such that doubling sequence length quadruples the memory footprint required for attention computation. Consequently, standard foundational architectures like BERT are constrained to a rigid 512-token context window~\cite{devlin2019bert}, whereas Philippine ordinances frequently span multiple pages. While practitioners employ sliding-window techniques to segment documents into overlapping spans~\cite{anand2020explainable, seo2025sliding}, this segmentation severs the semantic dependency between general regulatory mandates and distant provisos~\cite{savelka2021discovering}. Sequence truncation therefore creates structural blind spots that can lead to false conflict classifications.

    A secondary challenge involves morphological noise and domain adaptation limits. Pretrained transformers require large corpora of clean text, and while Western legal models like Legal-BERT perform well on foreign statutory benchmarks~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}, no dedicated foundation model exists for Philippine local legislation. Applying general tokenizers to local archives (which contain scanning artifacts, archaic statutory phrases, and code-switched expressions) triggers severe token fragmentation. Domain-specific terms are broken into meaningless sub-tokens that degrade contextual comprehension.

    Finally, edge deployment is restricted by local government hardware realities. Executing deep logical inference across thousands of document pairs demands substantial GPU compute and memory bandwidth~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Most local government units operate standard desktop workstations with modest hardware specifications, so deploying unoptimized neural models leads to system latency spikes or memory exhaustion. These resource limits motivate decoupled, multi-stage architectures suited for local inference.

    \subsection{Suitability of Transformers}
    Notwithstanding these hardware limitations, Transformer models remain well suited for Semantic Conflict Detection in local ordinances. General-domain language models typically struggle with legal text because common words frequently take on entirely different legal definitions. For instance, ``party'' refers to a litigant rather than a celebration, and ``suit'' signifies a legal action rather than clothing. Greco et al.~\cite{Greco2023Bringing} emphasize that this high degree of polysemy is why domain-specific models are required. While older static systems assign a single, fixed meaning to a word~\cite{Greco2023Bringing}, a Transformer dynamically adjusts its reading of a word based on the surrounding sentence context~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2019large} demonstrated that this contextual awareness allows a model to distinguish between amending a law and violating it; keyword searches miss this distinction.

    The transition to Deep Learning involves moving away from legacy methodologies. Boolean keyword searches fail when legislative drafting vocabulary evolves over the decades. Conversely, manually mapping thousands of unlinked Republic Acts and local ordinances into deterministic graph structures creates an intractable administrative burden. As Li et al.~\cite{li2024construction} observed, hand-crafting legal knowledge graphs does not scale for volatile, unstructured local records. Transformers offer a practical alternative because they map latent semantic relationships in unstructured legal text without requiring manual graph entry.

    

\section{Single-Stage and Multi-Stage Architectures}
In mainstream Natural Language Processing (NLP), end-to-end, single-stage neural architectures are the conventional baseline for document classification and question answering. These systems funnel raw input sequences directly into a single neural network that outputs the final classification without intermediate filtering. However, in legal informatics, detecting statutory conflicts requires a level of deductive precision across vast corpora that a monolithic model cannot easily provide. Forcing a single architecture to simultaneously scan tens of thousands of statutory records and evaluate fine-grained deontic logic creates severe mathematical and operational trade-offs.

    \subsection{Single-Stage Monolithic Pipelines}
    The appeal of a single-stage neural architecture is its conceptual simplicity: a single model handles the entire input-output mapping without requiring intermediate data handoffs. In standard NLP tasks operating over small document collections, this approach minimizes engineering complexity and eliminates pipeline handoff delays. However, deploying a monolithic model to audit draft local legislation against expansive national repositories introduces severe operational barriers. Such designs inevitably sacrifice either candidate recall or deductive precision.

    One common monolithic approach involves prompting generative Large Language Models to directly declare legal preemption. Autoregressive language models operate as probabilistic token predictors rather than deterministic logical discriminators, which leaves them vulnerable to legal hallucinations where non-existent statutory citations are fabricated~\cite{linna2026challenges}. Furthermore, their large parameter counts make offline deployment on standard local government hardware infeasible without heavy quantization. Aggressive quantization degrades deductive reasoning fidelity in specialized statutory domains~\cite{dettmers2022llmint8}. 
    
    Conversely, relying exclusively on single-stage bi-encoders introduces severe semantic compression limits. In a bi-encoder (or dual-tower) architecture, the search query and the candidate statute are processed through separate neural networks into independent vector coordinates. Because the two texts never interact directly during encoding, the model can pre-compute and store embeddings for tens of thousands of statutes in advance, which enables fast nearest-neighbor search across large archives~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert, chen2024bgem3}. However, compressing an entire multi-clause provision into a single fixed-dimensional vector discards granular syntactic nuance~\cite{karpukhin2020dense, thakur2021beir}. This architecture optimizes for broad topical similarity rather than logical consistency, causing bi-encoders to conflate permission and prohibition provisions that share identical topical vocabulary~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    Furthermore, executing a single-stage cross-encoder across an entire statutory repository is computationally prohibitive. In contrast to bi-encoders, a cross-encoder concatenates the draft query and the candidate statute into a single unified sequence so every token in the draft interacts directly with every token in the statute through full bidirectional cross-attention. While this joint comparison achieves high logical sensitivity, full cross-attention scales quadratically ($O(n^2)$) with input length. Comparing a newly proposed local draft against tens of thousands of national statutes simultaneously would require hundreds of thousands of complex forward passes, exhausting workstation memory and causing severe system latency~\cite{beltagy2020longformer}. Forcing the model to process fragmented sliding windows across thousands of enactments severs inter-clause dependencies. This computational ceiling confirms that isolating a compact candidate shortlist via coarse retrieval is necessary prior to executing fine-grained sequence classification.

    \subsection{Two-Stage Retrieve-then-Entail Architectures}
    To resolve the mathematical and computational limitations of monolithic architectures, contemporary legal informatics adopts decoupled, two-stage systems. In the retrieve-then-entail paradigm, the workflow is split into two specialized stages: an initial coarse Information Retrieval (IR) phase that scans the entire statutory corpus to extract a top-$k$ candidate shortlist, followed by a fine Natural Language Inference (NLI) phase that performs deep cross-attention on the candidate pairs~\cite{koreeda2021contract, goebel2026an}. Prior investigations in legal contract verification and statutory entailment show that this decoupled two-tier design substantially contracts the combinatorial search space of pairwise comparisons~\cite{koreeda2021contract}. Restricting cross-attention to shortlisted pairs preserves inferential accuracy while keeping runtime manageable.

    The primary engineering advantage of this decoupled architecture is its alignment with edge computing realities. By offloading broad corpus search to pre-computed vector indices that require minimal runtime memory, the computationally intensive $O(n^2)$ matrix operations of the cross-encoder are restricted exclusively to the top-$k$ candidate pairs. Khattab and Zaharia~\cite{khattab2020colbert} established that this decoupled structure enables neural language processing to execute locally on consumer-grade hardware without distributed GPU clusters. Furthermore, decoupling retrieval from inference provides a pragmatic resolution to the vector compression trade-off. The initial retrieval stage accepts the loss of granular syntactic nuance to maximize search throughput across extensive statutory archives. In contrast, the subsequent inference stage bypasses vector pooling entirely, performing joint token-level cross-attention over uncompressed raw text spans to achieve high-precision deontic reasoning~\cite{humeau2019poly}.

    \subsection{Comparative Architectural Trade-offs}
    Comparing single-stage and dual-stage architectures highlights a fundamental division of labor in statutory preemption analysis. A single-stage retriever maximizes search speed but remains blind to subtle modal negators, whereas a single-stage cross-encoder possesses high logical sensitivity but cannot scale beyond a few dozen provisions without memory failure. Chaining both mechanisms in a coarse-to-fine hierarchy combines the complementary strengths of each model family. Stage One absorbs corpus volume and context length, while Stage Two resolves logical nuance and morphological noise~\cite{louis2022a}.

    The empirical superiority of this decoupled design is reinforced by over a decade of findings from the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE). As Goebel et al.~\cite{goebel2026an} document, COLIEE divides legal reasoning into statutory information retrieval (Task 3) and statutory textual entailment (Task 4). Year after year, top-performing teams reject single-stage monoliths in favor of pipelined approaches~\cite{althammer2021dossiercoliee}. While early benchmarks in COLIEE 2021 established this separation, modern iterations through COLIEE 2025~\cite{coliee2025proceedings} and COLIEE 2026~\cite{coliee2026proceedings} confirm that decoupled retrieval and inference remain the dominant paradigm. For statutory retrieval, hybrid dense-sparse systems achieve near-perfect candidate recall~\cite{ngo2026nowj}, while cross-encoder entailment systems reach over 95\% accuracy by isolating granular reasoning from broad corpus retrieval~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. This sustained global validation confirms that separating Information Retrieval from Natural Language Inference is an established operational blueprint for resolving semantic conflict across statutory corpora.

    
\section{Information Retrieval}
Information Retrieval (IR) is the process of extracting relevant documents from large unstructured datasets based on a specific query. In legal informatics, IR is the foundational filtering mechanism. It forms the ``coarse'' stage of a dual-pipeline system, isolating candidate statutes from thousands of irrelevant records before intensive logical analysis takes place. Candidate filtering reduces the pairwise comparison load for downstream inference models.

    \subsection{Information Retrieval Challenges in Statutory Corpora}
    Applying information retrieval techniques to statutory collections introduces domain-specific hurdles that general-domain search engines are not engineered to handle. A primary challenge is severe lexical divergence and vocabulary mismatch across legislative eras~\cite{blair1985an}. In statutory law, statutes governing identical civic activities may be enacted decades apart, using drastically different terminology, archaic phrasing, or specialized administrative acronyms. When newly drafted local ordinances employ modern regulatory expressions, exact keyword matching fails to retrieve governing historical statutes; this omission creates dangerous blind spots in preemption review~\cite{opijnen2017on}.

    Furthermore, statutory corpora exhibit extreme length asymmetry and structural complexity that disrupt standard retrieval scoring. Statutory enactments frequently contain verbose, multi-clause provisions exceeding one thousand tokens, where general commands are qualified by distant provisos and statutory exceptions~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Standard passage retrievers struggle to balance term saturation across these extended provisions, frequently penalizing lengthy, comprehensive enactments. Compounding this structural challenge is administrative hierarchy noise. In national legal archives, subordinate issuances (such as executive orders and departmental circulars) outnumber primary legislative acts, frequently diluting candidate shortlists unless protected by statutory priority safeguards~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}.

    \subsection{Relevance in Retrieval}
    To understand how computational systems extract documents, it is useful to examine the human cognitive process that defines relevance in the physical world. In cognitive science, relevance is not a static property but a dynamic relationship between information and a specific task. According to Relevance Theory, humans naturally filter information by seeking the greatest cognitive benefit for the least mental effort~\cite{sperber1996relevance}. When a legal researcher checks for conflicts, they do not simply count matching words; they evaluate the ``intent'' and ``applicability'' of a rule based on their background knowledge of the legal system. This human-centric view suggests that a document is relevant only if it provides new information that changes the researcher's understanding of the legal landscape~\cite{saracevic2007relevance}.

    Beyond the cognitive baseline, the theoretical definition of relevance in legal information retrieval is anchored in the philosophical requirements of a functional legal system. According to the jurisprudence established by Lon Fuller in \textit{The Morality of Law}, a primary requirement for the internal morality of law is consistency; a system that issues contradictory rules fails to be a legal system at all~\cite{fuller1964the}. Within this philosophical framework, relevance is not simply about finding documents that talk about the same subject matter; it is about identifying provisions that share a ``normative overlap.'' This means that a retrieved document is relevant if it occupies the same jurisdictional or behavioral space as a draft local ordinance, potentially creating a situation where a citizen cannot obey one law without violating another. Grounding the retrieval stage in this quest for consistency treats relevance as the systematic identification of potential normative collisions and threats to the rule of law~\cite{fuller1964the}.

    However, traditional computational methods struggle to replicate this human judgment because they rely on a rigid mathematical definition of relevance. In standard deterministic systems, relevance is treated as a binary mathematical property based entirely on set intersection~\cite{blair1985an}. If a candidate document is denoted as $D$ and a user query as $Q$, this lexical relevance is formally evaluated as a binary function $R(D,Q) \in \{0,1\}$. Under this strict lexical definition, a document is deemed fully relevant (i.e., $1$) if and only if it contains the exact sequence of characters requested by the user~\cite{opijnen2017on}. Consequently, this mechanism establishes a purely topical baseline, effectively grouping documents together simply because they share a surface-level vocabulary~\cite{blair1985an}. While this mathematical simplicity allows for rapid indexing across large databases, it strips away the legal context required for complex legislative analysis.

    This stark discrepancy between superficial lexical matching and actual legal consequence represents a major stumbling block for legacy search architectures. In statutory law, two documents discussing the same topic might be lexically relevant to one another, but they are only \textit{legally} relevant if one provision actively modifies, permits, restricts, or preempts the other~\cite{opijnen2017on}. For instance, a retrieved document might use identical vocabulary but apply to a completely different jurisdiction or regulatory scope; this jurisdictional divergence renders it unhelpful for an ex-ante conflict check. Conversely, a document regulating the same civic behavior using different phrasing (such as substituting ``motor vehicle'' for ``automobile'') receives a relevance score of zero in a strict Boolean system~\cite{blair1985an}. Therefore, to audit local ordinances against superior law, statutory retrieval systems must move beyond binary, keyword-driven matching in favor of dense semantic representations capable of capturing jurisprudential relationships~\cite{kim2024legal}.

    To resolve these limitations, modern computational pipelines redefine relevance spatially rather than lexically. Instead of scanning a query as a raw string of text, dense retrieval architectures use Bi-Encoder models to compress entire legal provisions into high-dimensional mathematical coordinates, commonly referred to as dense vectors~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Under this semantic framework, relevance is determined not by counting shared characters, but by calculating the geometric distance (such as cosine similarity) between coordinate points~\cite{kim2024legal}. If a newly drafted local ordinance and an existing national statute govern the same underlying civic behavior, the neural network learns to map their respective vectors closely together in the mathematical space and bypasses the vocabulary mismatch problem~\cite{wu2025aiir}. The retrieval mechanism evaluates relevance as a continuous proximity score rather than a rigid binary output; this spatial formulation surfaces conceptually related laws that basic keyword searches miss.

    \subsection{Long-Context Retrieval} 
    Relying on standard BERT-based architectures introduces an artificial constraint on legal analysis: the 512-token limit. Because standard Self-Attention mechanisms scale quadratically with sequence length, processing long texts requires severe truncation. To work around this limit, researchers like Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} use Sliding Window techniques to segment documents into overlapping chunks. However, applying this to Philippine ordinances (which are historically wordy and often bury exceptions deep within 1,000-token provisions) disrupts statutory interpretation. It severs the substantive semantic link between a primary rule and its distant exception.

    Consequently, the model operates with a logical blind spot. Beltagy et al.~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} note that this causes logical errors, where a rule may be flagged as an absolute prohibition simply because its exempting clause was pushed into a separate, unconnected processing window. Furthermore, retrieval systems must balance Sparse Retrieval (such as BM25) for exact keyword matching against Dense Retrieval for capturing conceptual meaning without direct word overlap. Combining both paradigms helps cover exact statutory citations alongside paraphrased local provisions.

    Resolving this fragmentation means moving beyond short sliding-window chunking where possible. The architectural solution requires an initial retrieval mechanism capable of native long-context retention. Recent Transformer models incorporate advanced positional encoding, such as Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), alongside sparse or optimized attention matrices. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} demonstrated that these upgrades extend context windows up to 8,192 tokens without triggering memory failures. Deploying this class of architecture allows the system to read entire local ordinances as cohesive semantic units, preserving the law's internal structure and preventing sliding-window truncation errors.

    \subsection{Retrieval and Fusion Strategies}
    \label{sec:retrieval_paradigms_fusion}
    In computational legal informatics, isolating candidate enactments from extensive statutory collections requires understanding the theoretical foundations and complementary trade-offs of diverse retrieval strategies. Findings from the COLIEE benchmarks and legislative consistency literature identify five methodological dimensions governing coarse statutory search. The foundational retrieval strategies encompass sparse probabilistic scoring and dense semantic embeddings, formalized in Equation~\eqref{eq:okapi_bm25} and Equation~\eqref{eq:dense_cosine_sim}.

    \noindent\textit{Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Relevance Scoring Function:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q) = \sum_{q \in Q} \text{IDF}(q) \cdot \frac{f(q, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(q, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
        \label{eq:okapi_bm25}
        \eqcaption{Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Relevance Scoring Function}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $f(q, D)$ denotes term frequency of query token $q$ in document $D$, $|D|/\text{avgdl}$ normalizes for provision length, and standard default parameters $k_1 = 1.5$ and $b = 0.75$ lie within the empirical ranges ($1.2 < k_1 < 2$, $0.5 < b < 0.8$) reported in Robertson and Zaragoza's probabilistic relevance framework~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}. 
    
    The Okapi BM25 formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:okapi_bm25} balances three core retrieval properties within a unified probabilistic relevance framework. First, Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{IDF}(q)$) assigns higher numerical weight to rare, discriminative terms (such as specific statutory numbers, formal administrative acronyms, or penal sanctions) while suppressing ubiquitous statutory stopwords that appear across all enactments. Second, Term Frequency Saturation ($k_1 = 1.5$) enforces sub-linear term weighting to capture diminishing marginal relevance, so repeating a keyword multiple times does not disproportionately inflate relevance over concise provisions. Third, Document Length Normalization ($b = 0.75$) penalizes exceptionally verbose provisions so lengthy multi-page legislative digests do not dominate search rankings simply through word volume. In statutory retrieval competitions such as COLIEE Task~3, BM25 is an essential baseline (as demonstrated by Team AIIR~\cite{wu2025aiir}) because legislative drafting relies on exact statutory citations and verbatim penal amounts, even though exact lexical matching still suffers from vocabulary mismatch when local ordinances paraphrase statutory commands~\cite{blair1985an, manning2008introduction}.

    \noindent\textit{Dense Semantic Cosine Proximity Relevance Function:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{dense}}(D, Q) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_Q \cdot \mathbf{e}_D}{\|\mathbf{e}_Q\| \|\mathbf{e}_D\|}
        \label{eq:dense_cosine_sim}
        \eqcaption{Dense Semantic Cosine Proximity Relevance Function}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathbf{e}_Q$ and $\mathbf{e}_D$ represent pooled contextual embeddings generated by dense bi-encoders such as all-MiniLM-L6-v2~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Rather than evaluating token overlap, Equation~\eqref{eq:dense_cosine_sim} scores relevance via cosine proximity in latent semantic space, capturing conceptual legal intent and retrieving governing statutes even when local drafts employ disparate phrasing.

    In heterogeneous statutory databases spanning tens of thousands of enactments, researchers frequently investigate topic classification or domain partitioning to reduce search space complexity. However, empirical literature highlights the severe danger of hard categorical filtering in legal domains. Foundational statutory instruments, such as the Philippine Local Government Code of 1991 (RA 7160) or comprehensive penal codes, function as omnibus legislation that simultaneously govern local administration, taxation, public safety, and environmental sanitation. Hard filtering by a single predicted regulatory category discards the governing omnibus code whenever a query touches an adjacent regulatory area; this exclusion causes severe drops in candidate recall. In contrast, information retrieval theory establishes soft domain priors~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}, which apply a multiplicative score boost to candidate documents matching the predicted topic or latent semantic cluster while preserving access to multi-domain statutory codes.

    Because lexical and semantic retrievers exhibit orthogonal failure modes, leading computational law frameworks combine both streams through hybrid fusion. In score-level linear combination, popularized by Team JNLP in the COLIEE competitions~\cite{goebel2026an}, weighted interpolation combines normalized sparse and dense scores according to $S_{\text{hybrid}}(D, Q) = \alpha \hat{S}_{\text{dense}}(D, Q) + (1 - \alpha) \hat{S}_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q)$, where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ balances semantic and lexical bias. Because BM25 produces unbounded positive relevance scores while dense bi-encoders output bounded cosine similarities within $[-1, 1]$, score-level linear combination requires sensitive calibration and min-max score normalization across heterogeneous query lengths. To eliminate score normalization artifacts, Cormack et al.~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal} formulated Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), defined as $\text{RRF}(D) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} (k_{\text{rrf}} + \text{rank}_m(D))^{-1}$, where $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{BM25}, \text{Dense}\}$ and $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$. RRF abstracts away disparate score distributions by operating strictly on ordinal rank positions, where the smoothing constant $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$ stabilizes reciprocal scoring and rewards candidates with high concordance across both lexical matching and dense embeddings.

    Finally, statutory retrieval systems must respect constitutional hierarchies of authority. Anchored in the doctrine of legislative supremacy and the hierarchy of legal norms~\cite{kelsen1967pure, philgov1991ra7160}, statutory systems operate under strict formal ordering. In national digital archives, subordinate administrative issuances (such as executive orders and administrative circulars) frequently constitute a large share of indexed records. Because administrative issuances share broad governmental vocabulary with local drafts, unconstrained retrieval often allows routine circulars to crowd out superior national statutes. Literature in legal informatics therefore emphasizes structural safeguards that prioritize primary legislative acts (Republic Acts) over subordinate executive issuances. Implementing a hierarchical statutory priority safeguard shields governing statutes from administrative noise; this prevents dilution of candidate shortlists and improves Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).

    \subsection{Retrieval Benchmarks}
    Selecting effective retrieval architectures from the extensive landscape of open-weight models requires objective empirical benchmarking. In contemporary legal information retrieval, candidate models are evaluated against standardized empirical leaderboards that assess zero-shot generalization across dense statutory text. The Benchmarking Information Retrieval (BEIR) suite evaluates a dense retriever's capacity for out-of-domain transfer across diverse, specialized datasets~\cite{thakur2021beir}. Because Philippine local legislation lacks extensive annotated training corpora, candidate retrievers must possess strong zero-shot generalization. Dominant performance on the BEIR leaderboard is a primary indicator of a model's capacity to map novel, localized legal terminology into a structured latent space without prior fine-tuning~\cite{thakur2021beir}.

    Expanding on BEIR, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) establishes an international standard for ranking embedding systems across more than one hundred datasets and diverse linguistic tasks~\cite{muennighoff2023mteb}. In information retrieval literature, candidate architectures are frequently filtered using MTEB's retrieval and clustering leaderboards. Models excelling on these benchmarks demonstrate an empirical capacity to cluster conceptually related doctrines and retrieve relevant provisions based on continuous semantic proximity. Complementing general embedding benchmarks, the annual COLIEE Task~3 evaluation provides a specialized testbed for statute law retrieval~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}. Systems evaluated on Task~3 must extract precise governing articles from civil code collections in response to legal queries; success requires balancing broad candidate recall with computational throughput~\cite{wu2025aiir, goebel2026an}.

    \subsection{Candidate Retrieval Models}
    \label{sec:ir_candidate_models}
    Surveying leading open-weight architectures documented across recent retrieval literature and standardized benchmarks reveals eight prominent bi-encoder models relevant to statutory candidate discovery. Among distilled edge baselines, Reimers and Gurevych~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} established the Sentence-BERT framework, mapping text into fixed-dimensional dense vector spaces optimized for cosine proximity. Building on this framework, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 compresses a 6-layer BERT architecture down to 22.7~million parameters through knowledge distillation to produce 384-dimensional dense vectors. Despite its compact footprint, it retains strong semantic clustering capabilities with low-latency inference on consumer CPUs. Complementing this baseline, Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2024bge} introduced the BGE family (including the compact 384-dimensional small variant, released as bge-small-en-v1.5 at 33.4M parameters); this architecture optimizes contrastive representation learning for high rank sensitivity on MTEB. In public-sector edge deployment, these distilled bi-encoders provide high-throughput candidate filtering without requiring dedicated GPU accelerators.

    Scaling bi-encoder capacity to standard 110M-parameter base dimensions enables more expressive semantic representations across general retrieval benchmarks. Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2024bge} developed the 768-dimensional base BGE encoder (released as bge-base-en-v1.5 at 109M parameters), using retrofitted contrastive pretraining and instruction-tuned query prefixes to excel across multi-domain retrieval leaderboards. Similarly, Wang et al.~\cite{wang2022text} introduced the E5 embedding family (including e5-base-v2 at 109M parameters), trained on approximately 1.3~billion weakly-supervised text pairs via contrastive objectives; E5 demonstrated strong generalization on unseen technical and legal search tasks. Furthermore, Song et al.~\cite{song2020mpnet} developed MPNet (110M parameters in its base configuration), combining masked language modeling with permuted language pretraining to eliminate positional dependence, which is the foundational architecture for all-mpnet-base-v2 (109M parameters).

    Beyond general-domain models, legal-domain adapted encoders capture specialized statutory taxonomies. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2020legal} demonstrated that pretraining bidirectional encoders on specialized legal repositories enables dense representations to capture statutory taxonomy and regulatory jargon that general-domain embedders conflate. Mean-pooled sentence embeddings derived from legal-bert-base-uncased (110M parameters) map statutory provisions into vector spaces that reflect legislative hierarchy and administrative codification rather than colloquial word associations. Unlike general web text encoders, legal-domain pretraining exposes the tokenizer and transformer weights to statutory citations and formal procedural clauses. This domain-specific pretraining provides a structured baseline to evaluate whether legal pre-training improves candidate provision ranking over general embeddings.

    To overcome the quadratic attention complexity of traditional 512-token encoders, modern architectures incorporate native long-context and multilingual designs. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} developed ModernBERT-base (149M parameters), which integrates FlashAttention-2, Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), GeGLU activations, and sequence unpadding to natively process up to 8,192 tokens. In statutory retrieval, this allows direct encoding of verbose multi-paragraph enactments without destructive sliding-window chunking. Expanding to the multilingual domain, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024bgem3} introduced bge-m3 (568M parameters, 1,024 dimensions), supporting 8,192-token contexts and supporting dense and sparse alongside multi-vector representations. In statutory consistency checking, these multi-functionality representations provide resilience across code-switched local records and bilingual administrative issuances.

\section{Natural Language Processing}
Natural Language Processing (NLP) provides computational techniques to analyze and represent text across linguistic levels toward human-like comprehension~\cite{liddy2001natural}. As Jurafsky and Martin~\cite{jurafsky2024speech} explain, it bridges human language and machine logic and enables algorithms to parse and interpret unstructured text. In the legal field, NLP shifts document auditing from slow manual reviews to scalable, automated systems. However, applying standard NLP to law requires more than basic grammar checks; it demands systems that can navigate dense sentence structures and specialized statutory vocabulary, including deontic operators such as obligations, permissions, and prohibitions.

Early Legal NLP relied on Symbolic Artificial Intelligence (AI)~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. These systems used manually built ontologies and strict Boolean logic to extract legal information. Because local legislative drafting language changes constantly over decades, these exact-match frameworks proved far too brittle. Contemporary NLP architectures address this by moving to continuous vector representations that map textual provisions into high-dimensional semantic spaces, bypassing superficial vocabulary mismatches to quantify conceptual links between legal terms. This shift allows automated tools to operate as active diagnostic engines rather than passive keyword indices, motivating the analysis below of domain challenges, Natural Language Inference (NLI), deductive benchmarks, and candidate encoder architectures.

    \subsection{NLP Challenges}
    Deploying NLP frameworks against local archives quickly exposes the mechanical limits of standard Language Models. A primary hurdle is the pronounced Global North bias in pre-training data and legal benchmarking. As Ranathunga et al.~\cite{ranathunga2022some} point out, the vast majority of Legal NLP benchmarks, like LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} and CaseHOLD, are trained exclusively on pristine, digitally native Western legal text. These highly optimized models degrade when applied to Philippine Local Government Unit (LGU) archives, which contain scanned PDFs with Optical Character Recognition (OCR) errors, archaic American colonial phrasing, untranslated Spanish legal terms, and local code-switched expressions. Rust et al.~\cite{rust2021how} demonstrated that standard WordPiece tokenizers shatter unrecognized words into sub-tokens; this fragmentation destroys a model's ability to infer legal intent. Addressing localized morphological noise requires models equipped with large Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) vocabularies that absorb linguistic variants without out-of-vocabulary errors, paired with Disentangled Attention architectures that separate word content from corrupted positions~\cite{he2023debertav3}.

    Beyond geographic and morphological bias, traditional NLP methods suffer from structural shortcomings in lexical similarity. Early NLP systems excel at identifying topical overlap by measuring surface-level token intersection using algorithms like TF-IDF or shallow word embeddings. However, Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli} explain that pure lexical similarity is unsuited for detecting legal contradictions because lexical metrics are blind to deontic logic and syntactic negation. For example, an ordinance stating that the city permits an activity and a statute stating that the state prohibits it share near-perfect lexical similarity scores. A standard similarity model clusters them as highly related, missing the policy collision beneath the shared vocabulary. Consequently, detecting normative conflicts requires advancing from lexical proximity to directional logical inference.

    \subsection{From NLP to NLI}
    Historically, checking legislative consistency relied on Symbolic NLP (hand-written rules) and, later, Statistical NLP. In Statistical NLP, models learned probabilistic weights for specific inputs, like Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) or N-grams~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. These models depended heavily on human-engineered features to define grammar rules and categories. While they allowed for soft, probabilistic decisions, they consistently struggled with the deep lexical and semantic ambiguities inherent in legal drafting.
    
    The field largely transitioned to Neural NLP models in the mid-2010s~\cite{quevedo2024legal}. Unlike statistical systems that treat features as separate entities, neural networks operate end-to-end; they map raw text directly to output predictions through hidden layers. These architectures capture deeper linguistic patterns than traditional statistical methods by learning complex relationships during optimization. However, while machine learning excels at finding patterns, it can overly mimic its training data, making domain-specific pre-training essential for high performance in rigorous legal tasks.

    Within modern neural approaches, Natural Language Inference (NLI), originally formalized under the Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE) challenges initiated by Dagan et al.~\cite{dagan2006pascal}, has emerged as a standard benchmark for evaluating algorithmic deduction. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024improving} define NLI as determining the directional logical relationship between two structured text sequences: a governing \textit{Premise} and a candidate \textit{Hypothesis}. In statutory preemption auditing, this formulation maps directly to constitutional hierarchy: the Premise represents the established positive legal standard (the superior national statute), while the Hypothesis represents the newly proposed local claim (the draft city ordinance provision). Because statutory compliance is directional rather than symmetric, reversing the Premise and Hypothesis would alter the governing legal obligation.
    
    Under this formulation, the neural network classifies each pair into one of three mutually exclusive logical states. In an Entailment relationship, the draft ordinance directly carries out, derives its authority from, or compliantly adheres to the governing national statute. In a Contradiction relationship, the draft ordinance collides with the national statute (for example, permitting what national law prohibits, or exceeding statutory penalty ceilings under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine). In a Neutral relationship, the two provisions govern complementary or non-overlapping subject matter without normative conflict. This directional formulation exposes the flaw of earlier statistical NLP, which equated high word overlap with logical entailment~\cite{yang2024improving}. As Le Hong et al.~\cite{lehong2023a} point out, modern neural architectures resolve complex linguistic phenomena, such as coreferences and modality, that rigid rule-based systems historically failed to grasp.

    \subsection{NLI Benchmarks}
    Identifying effective Cross-Encoder architectures for the inference stage requires isolating models with strong pre-trained deductive logic. In natural language processing, research literature relies on established benchmarks to evaluate candidate reasoning models. Wang et al. introduced the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE)~\cite{wang2019glue} and its successor SuperGLUE~\cite{wang2020superglue} as foundational baselines for model reasoning. These frameworks evaluate a Transformer's ability to handle diverse linguistic challenges, such as Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE) and resolving complex coreferences. While high performance on SuperGLUE confirms baseline deductive capacity, it does not independently guarantee success in the legal domain due to the specialized vocabulary shifts present in statutory drafting.

    To evaluate specialized legal logic, legal NLP research relies on the Legal General Language Understanding Evaluation (LexGLUE) benchmark~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} designed LexGLUE specifically to test a model's ability to navigate legalese, where common words assume highly context-dependent meanings. High performance on LexGLUE tasks indicates that an architecture possesses foundational capacity to handle statutory drafting and regulatory provisions. Evaluating candidate architectures on domain-specific benchmarks therefore distinguishes general language fluency from statutory precision.

    Complementing domain-specific suites, contemporary Hugging Face NLI leaderboards evaluate models on diverse, adversarial textual entailment tasks aggregating MultiNLI (MNLI), FEVER, Adversarial NLI (ANLI R1--R3)~\cite{nie2020anli}, and WANLI~\cite{liu2022wanli}. Benchmarking candidate models across these suites tests their resilience against spurious lexical heuristics, premise-hypothesis artifacts, and negation traps. High-performing architectures on these leaderboards, such as the pre-aligned DeBERTa-v3 variants curated by Laurer et al.~\cite{laurer2024less}, demonstrate strong zero-shot deductive capacity and establish leading candidate foundations for statutory conflict detection. Finally, within legal informatics, the definitive international competition benchmark for statutory inference is Task~4 of the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE)~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}. In Task~4, systems evaluate whether a natural language query is legally entailed by or contradicts a governing civil code article. Evaluating architectures on Task~4 provides direct empirical evidence of a model's capacity to navigate statutory conditions and deontic exceptions.

    \subsection{Candidate NLI Models}
    \label{sec:nli_candidate_models}
    Evaluating deontic relationships across statutory provisions demands specialized inductive biases across candidate sequence encoders. Standard language models trained exclusively on general corpora frequently fail to capture the specialized semantics of legal drafting, where everyday terms carry specialized juridical meanings. To address this vocabulary divergence, researchers develop domain-adapted encoders initialized with custom legal vocabularies or continuously pretrained on expansive legal repositories. Chalkidis et al.~\cite{chalkidis2020legal} introduced LEGAL-BERT, pretrained on English legislation and court judgments; this domain pretraining demonstrated stronger term sensitivity across legal classification tasks. Henderson et al.~\cite{henderson2022pileoflaw} expanded this approach with PoL-BERT (Pile of Law), pretraining encoders on a 256~GB corpus encompassing administrative regulations and statutory provisions. Complementary efforts by Paul et al.~\cite{paul2022inlegalbert} (InLegalBERT) and Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2021when} (custom Legal-BERT, later released as CaseLaw-BERT) investigate the transferability of common-law statutory pretraining, while Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2021lawformer} evaluate long-document legal encoders. In literature, this family provides a critical testbed for assessing whether domain pretraining provides intrinsic protection against legal misinterpretation.

    A pervasive vulnerability of unadapted sequence encoders on deontic tasks is \textit{affirmative entailment bias}, where models exhibit a statistical tendency to default toward predicting Entailment or Neutral when reading complex clauses containing subtle prohibitions~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. In standard language corpora, true logical contradictions are rare; text passages typically elaborate, confirm, or describe unrelated facts. Consequently, unadapted neural models learn a strong prior that pairs generally agree. Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} established bert-base-uncased (110M parameters) with masked language modeling as a canonical supervised baseline against which improvements are measured. To overcome heuristic reliance on lexical overlap and break affirmative bias, contemporary literature explores models pre-aligned on multi-task natural language inference datasets (such as MNLI, FEVER, ANLI, and WANLI). Laurer et al.~\cite{laurer2024less} demonstrate that initializing sequence classifiers from warm NLI checkpoints equips models with strong directional contradiction sensitivity before domain adaptation. Structurally, He et al.~\cite{he2023debertav3} developed DeBERTa-v3, introducing Disentangled Attention to represent word content and relative positions with separate vectors alongside replaced token detection. In parallel, Clark et al.~\cite{clark2020electra} developed ELECTRA with Replaced Token Detection to optimize contextual representations across all input positions. These architectures excel at distinguishing subtle deontic shifts that standard masked encoders frequently miss.

    Statutory drafting is inherently verbose, characterized by complex preamble clauses and multi-sentence penal qualifications. Traditional transformer encoders are constrained by quadratic self-attention complexity, imposing a rigid 512-token ceiling that necessitates document chunking or sliding-window heuristics~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}. Early long-context architectures attempted to resolve this constraint using localized or sparse attention approximations, such as the sliding-window attention in Longformer~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} and the block-sparse random attention in BigBird~\cite{zaheer2020bigbird}. However, empirical legal informatics evaluations note that sparse attention patterns can dilute or sever semantic dependencies between distant provisos and governing commands. To overcome these limitations, modern architectures integrate architectural optimizations that support extended contexts natively. Warner et al.~\cite{warner2024modernbert} introduced ModernBERT, an 8,192-token encoder incorporating FlashAttention-2, Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), GeGLU activation functions, and sequence unpadding. Complementing this, Nussbaum et al.~\cite{nussbaum2024nomic} developed Nomic-BERT to extend context retention. These modern long-context designs enable joint encoding of complete multi-sentence statutory provisions without sparse approximation artifacts.

    Unlike bi-encoders that process queries and documents independently into pooled vector coordinates, cross-encoders execute full bidirectional self-attention across the concatenated pair simultaneously. In retrieval and inference benchmarks, joint cross-attention captures deep inter-token interactions that vector similarity cannot represent. In COLIEE statute retrieval benchmarks, researchers deploy multilingual cross-encoder rerankers, notably BGE-Reranker-v2-m3~\cite{xiao2024bge}, to score pairwise relevance across multi-thousand token contexts. Similarly, sequence classifiers adapted from MS-MARCO reranking frameworks employ deep cross-attentive scoring to evaluate pair compatibility. In multi-stage neural retrieval paradigms, Nogueira and Cho~\cite{nogueira2020passage} demonstrated that candidate passages retrieved by coarse search can be evaluated in parallel batches via cross-attentive models (such as MonoBERT), aggregating candidate pairs into structured mini-batches to use hardware vectorization. In literature, native cross-encoders represent the conceptual bridge between Stage~1 candidate filtering and Stage~2 logical validation.

    Evaluating candidate sequence pairs through multi-layer cross-encoders introduces computational throughput trade-offs during live document review. In standard transformer implementations, mini-batch tensors are padded with dummy zero tokens to match a static global sequence limit, such as 256 or 512 tokens~\cite{devlin2019bert}. Because self-attention complexity scales quadratically ($O(L^2)$) with input sequence length $L$, allocating static global padding causes significant floating-point operations to be expended on empty padding positions when input clauses are short. In neural language processing literature, dynamic sequence padding addresses this inefficiency by clamping batch tensor dimensions strictly to the length of the longest clause within each mini-batch; this adjustment reduces redundant self-attention calculations while producing mathematically identical hidden states. Furthermore, in multi-document and multi-clause document auditing, batch collation literature establishes that flattening candidate pairs across multiple queries into a single continuous tensor pass amortizes runtime call overhead and uses multi-threaded central processing unit vectorization.

    In post-colonial statutory jurisdictions like the Philippines, statutory archives exhibit unique sociolinguistic complexities. While statutory enactments are drafted in legal English, local ordinances and administrative records frequently feature localized terminology, regional administrative phrasing, Latin legal maxims, and English-Tagalog code-switching. Conneau et al.~\cite{conneau2020xlmr} introduced XLM-RoBERTa, trained across 100 languages; this model established strong cross-lingual transfer on semantic benchmarks. He et al.~\cite{he2023debertav3} extended disentangled attention to the multilingual domain with mDeBERTa-v3; this design provides resilience to mixed-language syntax. Furthermore, Cruz and Cheng~\cite{cruz2020tagalog} developed Tagalog-adapted transformers (roberta-tagalog-base) to capture Philippine syntactic structures. In statutory consistency checking, multilingual encoders evaluate whether localized linguistic variances can be processed without severe drops in comprehension.

    Deploying deep learning in public-sector local government environments requires strict adherence to available edge computing infrastructure. Because local government units typically operate without high-performance computing clusters or enterprise cloud APIs, deploying billion-parameter language models is computationally unfeasible. To establish realistic performance baselines under constrained hardware, the literature explores knowledge distillation and parameter sharing. Sanh et al.~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} introduced DistilBERT, compressing transformer depth to reduce model size by 40\% while retaining 97\% of BERT's language comprehension capabilities and running 60\% faster. Lan et al.~\cite{lan2020albert} introduced ALBERT, using factorized embedding parameterization and cross-layer parameter sharing to reduce parameter size down to 12~million in its base configuration (an 89\% reduction compared to BERT-base). In dense semantic representation, Reimers and Gurevych~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} established the Sentence-BERT (SBERT) siamese embedding framework. This framework supports compact distilled bi-encoders such as all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (22.7M parameters) for low-latency inference on commodity CPU hardware. Evaluating distilled edge models allows researchers to quantify the exact accuracy penalty incurred when prioritizing low CPU latency over multi-layer neural capacity.

\section{Ground Truth}
In supervised machine learning, a Ground Truth (or Gold Standard) dataset provides the verified target labels used to optimize objective functions and evaluate model parameters. Formally, the ground truth establishes the empirical reference standard against which supervised learning models optimize loss functions~\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. During training, the optimization algorithm computes empirical risk between model predictions and verified target labels; it derives gradients via backpropagation to iteratively adjust model weights. In domain-specific legal NLP, expert-adjudicated ground truth is essential to prevent models from learning superficial statistical artifacts or spurious annotation heuristics that fail to generalize to actual statutory logic.

In model evaluation methodology, the ground truth provides the objective benchmark for measuring generalization capacity across held-out partitions. Datasets are partitioned into independent training, validation, and test subsets. The test partition remains strictly unobserved during hyperparameter tuning and model checkpoint selection and forms a clean out-of-sample evaluation set. Evaluating the model on this held-out data enables objective computation of classification metrics (including precision, recall, Macro $F_1$, and class-specific $F_2$-scores) and tests whether the architecture generalizes to unseen legislative drafts~\cite{bowman2015snli}.

    \subsection{Ground Truth in NLI}
    In Natural Language Inference (NLI), ground truth datasets define the logical relationship between two pieces of text. Annotators read a source text (the Premise) and a claim (the Hypothesis), then categorize their relationship into one of three distinct labels. If the premise guarantees the hypothesis is true, it is labeled as Entailment. If they contradict each other, it is labeled as Contradiction. If there is not enough information to prove either, the pair is labeled Neutral~\cite{yang2024improving}.

    Most traditional NLI datasets, such as the widely used SNLI (Stanford Natural Language Inference) corpus, are built using crowdsourced workers who write simple variations of everyday sentences~\cite{bowman2015snli}. While this approach is cheap and easily generates hundreds of thousands of training pairs, the resulting datasets are basic and often embed unintended annotation artifacts~\cite{gururangan2018annotation}. They usually feature short, single-sentence premises with obvious logical connections. Training a model exclusively on these simple datasets leaves it unprepared for the dense, multi-layered logic required in specialized fields.

    \subsection{Annotation and Reliability Metrics}
    Turning raw text into a useful NLI dataset requires strict guidelines to minimize human bias and prevent annotators from relying on subjective legal interpretations. In standard NLI, guidelines dictate simple factual agreement. However, legal conflict detection requires evaluating normative relationships based on deontic logic. Drawing from the frameworks established by Agotnes et al.~\cite{agotnes2007on} and Li et al.~\cite{li2012detecting}, deontic annotation guidelines in statutory conflict detection instruct experts to map text into obligations (must do), permissions (may do), and prohibitions (cannot do). A ``Contradiction'' is flagged if a local ordinance mandates an action the national statute prohibits, or prohibits an action the national statute explicitly permits. If a local ordinance imposes an administrative fee on an activity within devolved local regulatory competence, the guidelines dictate a ``Neutral'' classification; however, if the local measure attempts to condition, restrict, or penalize an activity already authorized or licensed under superior national legislation (such as imposing an unauthorized local permit or additional fee on a nationally licensed activity), the provision triggers a ``Contradiction'' under statutory preemption. This deontic framework ensures that annotation captures legal preemption rather than superficial topical friction.

        \subsubsection{Inter-Rater Reliability Metrics}
        To evaluate the consistency of legal annotations, researchers use Inter-Rater Reliability (IRR) metrics~\cite{mchugh2012interrater} rather than raw percentage agreement. Percentage agreement is inadequate for evaluating multi-annotator consistency because it fails to correct for expected chance concordance ($p_e$). Under a three-class discrete annotation schema ($\mathcal{C} = 3$), independent stochastic labeling yields a baseline expected agreement of approximately $33.3\%$ purely through chance alignment. Reporting unadjusted percentage concordance confounds random agreement with genuine evaluative consensus and inflates reliability estimates. The two primary statistical formulas used to subtract random chance are Cohen's Kappa~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} and Fleiss' Kappa~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}, defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:cohens_kappa} and Equation~\eqref{eq:fleiss_kappa}.

    \noindent\textit{Cohen's Pairwise Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}
        \label{eq:cohens_kappa}
        \eqcaption{Cohen's Pairwise Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $p_o$ represents the relative observed agreement between the two individual raters, and $p_e$ represents the hypothetical probability of chance agreement. Equation~\eqref{eq:cohens_kappa} evaluates agreement between exactly two raters.

    \noindent\textit{Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa = \frac{\bar{P} - \bar{P_e}}{1 - \bar{P_e}}
        \label{eq:fleiss_kappa}
        \eqcaption{Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa Reliability Metric ($\kappa$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\bar{P}$ represents the mean observed agreement across all pairs of raters, while $\bar{P_e}$ represents the expected chance agreement across the entire pool. Equation~\eqref{eq:fleiss_kappa} generalizes Cohen's formula to accommodate three or more domain experts establishing a majority consensus.

    The resulting $\kappa$ score from Equation~\eqref{eq:fleiss_kappa} is interpreted using the standard statistical intervals established by Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977measurement}. Table~\ref{tab:kappa_interpretation} summarizes these six categorical ranges for evaluating inter-annotator consensus.
    
    \begin{table}[htbp]
        \centering
        \caption[Interpretation Ranges for Fleiss' Kappa Reliability Metric]{Interpretation Ranges for Fleiss' Kappa Reliability Metric}
        \label{tab:kappa_interpretation}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}cc@{}}
            \toprule
            \textbf{Kappa ($\kappa$) Score} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
            \midrule
            $< 0.00$ & Poor agreement \\
            $0.00 - 0.20$ & Slight agreement \\
            $0.21 - 0.40$ & Fair agreement \\
            $0.41 - 0.60$ & Moderate agreement \\

            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}

    Table~\ref{tab:kappa_interpretation} presents the categorical intervals used to interpret multi-rater agreement across independent judges. Its two columns map numerical $\kappa$ ranges to qualitative labels across six bands (Poor, Slight, Fair, Moderate, Substantial, and Almost perfect agreement). As $\kappa$ increases from negative values toward unity, the intervals distinguish chance-level alignment from consistent consensus across evaluators. In legal annotation studies, reaching $\kappa \ge 0.61$ (``Substantial agreement'') provides an operational benchmark indicating that labeled pairs reflect shared legal judgment rather than individual subjectivity.

    While objective tasks routinely achieve $\kappa$ scores above $0.90$, such agreement is rare in textual inference tasks. Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli}, in their foundational study for the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) corpus, reported an overall Fleiss' Kappa of $0.70$ across validated pairs ($0.77$ for contradiction, $0.72$ for entailment, and $0.60$ for neutral). Even with multiple trained raters, semantic inference across natural language statements involves borderline interpretations where reasonable annotators disagree. Legal NLI inherits this inherent ambiguity and adds statutory complexity; $\kappa$ scores in the substantial range therefore represent a realistic target.

        \subsubsection{Sub-Threshold Reliability}
        Achieving a ``Substantial'' Inter-Rater Reliability score ($\kappa \ge 0.61$) validates the clarity of the annotation guidelines. However, failing to meet this threshold does not inherently invalidate a dataset. Klebanov and Beigman~\cite{klebanov2009from} establish that a sub-threshold $\kappa$ score often indicates systematic ambiguity within the text itself, specifically ``hard'' instances where experienced raters can reasonably disagree, rather than annotator carelessness. Their noise-modeling framework demonstrates that it remains possible to extract a reliable, low-noise subset from items where annotators reach consensus.

        When researchers encounter sub-threshold reliability, the mitigation strategy depends on dataset size. For large-scale studies, including the disaster-tweet classification by Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing}, the standard procedure is data omission. Klebanov and Beigman~\cite{klebanov2009from} formalize filtering out difficult, borderline cases from the gold standard because evaluating algorithms on inherently ambiguous text introduces label noise that degrades model evaluation. In large general-domain corpora, items that fail to achieve consensus are omitted because the remaining volume preserves statistical power.
        
        However, applying omission to a specialized, few-shot legal dataset sharply reduces statistical power and Minimum Detectable Effect. Furthermore, in local legislation, these ``hard'' cases often represent the exact jurisdictional friction an inference engine must detect. Rather than discarding ambiguous items, Gao et al.~\cite{gao2022hierarchical} demonstrate a hierarchical adjudication workflow in specialized clinical annotation, where a senior adjudicator reviews conflicting labels to reach consensus. Adapting expert adjudication to legal datasets resolves ambiguity under governing jurisprudential principles and treats initial disagreement as a measure of statutory difficulty rather than a barrier.

    \subsection{Ground Truth in Legal NLP}
    When applying NLI to the legal domain, comparing short, isolated sentences quickly breaks down. Statutory phrasing is complex, filled with conditional clauses and domain-specific vocabulary. Consequently, a hypothesis in a legal context rarely aligns perfectly with a single sentence in the premise. Instead, proving an entailment or contradiction often requires connecting evidence scattered across multiple paragraphs or pages.

    To address this, researchers have developed specialized legal datasets that reflect real-world document structures. For instance, the Competition on Legal Information Extraction/Entailment (COLIEE) dataset constructs its ground truth by pairing civil code statutes with legal bar exam queries, requiring models to identify specific legal provisions before determining entailment~\cite{rabelo2022coliee}. Similarly, the ContractNLI dataset by Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} engineers its gold standard around non-disclosure agreements. Rather than giving the model pre-cut sentence pairs, ContractNLI requires the system to evaluate hypotheses against entire legal documents and extract supporting text spans.

    Furthermore, unlike general-domain datasets crowdsourced through platforms like Amazon Mechanical Turk, legal ground truth relies on domain experts. Constructing datasets like CaseHOLD~\cite{zheng2021when}, which evaluates legal holding statements, requires law students and legal professionals to verify that labels reflect actual jurisprudence. Benchmarks such as LexGLUE~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} show that evaluating legal NLP models demands expert-annotated data rather than automated or amateur labeling. Relying on trained legal annotators prevents factual misreadings of statutory rules from corrupting benchmark labels.

    This difficulty is magnified when dealing with local legislation. While national or federal laws generally follow standardized drafting conventions, local ordinance texts are highly heterogeneous, exhibiting significant variations in terminology and structural organization across jurisdictions~\cite{campos2026nlp}. Because of this localized variance, local legal documents have historically been neglected in NLP research, hampered primarily by a severe lack of annotated ground truth datasets~\cite{campos2026citilink}. Standard legal corpora fail to capture the specific legislative templates, localized governance terminology, or idiosyncratic cross-referencing styles used in local legislation, such as Philippine city ordinances. Without existing resources that reflect these structural and linguistic complexities, evaluating models on local legislation requires building specialized, expert-annotated datasets from the ground up.

    A critical architectural decision in dataset construction is determining the granularity of the extracted legal premise. While the hypothesis (the local draft) is evaluated as a multi-sentence span to capture context, isolating the premise (the national statute) to its exact governing rule limits extraneous noise. In Philippine statutory drafting, a single paragraph frequently contains a general rule alongside conditional modifiers and exceptions. Holzenberger et al.~\cite{holzenberger2020a} observe that statutory reasoning involves matching concise governing rules and variable constraints against fact scenarios, whereas raw long-text segments often exceed standard encoder token limits. Isolating the governing statutory provision to its exact sentence boundary prevents candidate inference models from being distracted by unrelated surrounding clauses so classification remains focused on the point of semantic collision.

    Dataset construction is further constrained by the scarcity of legal domain experts available for annotation. As Zhong et al.~\cite{zhong2020how} note, specialized legal terminology makes manual labeling time-consuming and difficult to scale, since it requires domain experts rather than crowdsourced workers. Because constructing massive datasets for localized city law is impractical, researchers rely on smaller, carefully curated Gold Standards. Gao et al.~\cite{gao2021making} demonstrate that pre-trained language models can be effectively fine-tuned in few-shot settings using only a small number of annotated examples per class (such as $K = 16$ or $32$ examples), provided the evaluation framework controls for overfitting.

    \subsection{Multi-Sentence and Cross-Document Limits}
    Traditional Natural Language Inference (NLI) evaluates text using a strict 1-to-1 pairing, comparing a single premise sentence directly against a single hypothesis sentence. However, this granular approach breaks down in the legal domain due to two specific edge cases. First, intra-document context: a primary rule in one section of an ordinance is frequently modified or negated by an exception buried several paragraphs later (the ``1v2'' or multi-sentence problem). Second, cross-document dependencies: a contradiction may only emerge when synthesizing provisions from two entirely separate statutes simultaneously.

    To address the first edge case, modern legal frameworks use Asymmetric Span-Level Pairing. Yin et al.~\cite{yin2021docnli} critique traditional sentence-level datasets and show that capturing contextual exceptions requires evaluating multi-sentence blocks rather than isolated lines. As demonstrated by Koreeda and Manning in the ContractNLI dataset~\cite{koreeda2021contract}, the premise remains a focused legal rule, while the corresponding hypothesis is extracted as a multi-sentence chunk. This asymmetric structure allows the model's attention mechanism to evaluate a primary rule against a distant exception contained within the same local provision.

    While asymmetric spans capture intra-document context, resolving the second edge case, cross-document multi-hop reasoning, remains computationally prohibitive. Identifying semantic preemption that spans multiple independent laws represents a current research frontier in legal natural language processing and remains unverified for local governance applications. Recent frameworks attempt to solve this with heavy architectural overhead: Lu et al.~\cite{lu2023multi} map Entity Graphs to mine cross-document evidence paths, while Shen et al.~\cite{shen2025hopweaver} rely on large generative LLMs in HopWeaver to synthesize multi-hop reasoning paths. Both approaches violate the hardware constraints of consumer-grade LGU workstations. Consequently, edge-deployable screening systems bound their scope to single-premise, asymmetric span evaluations to avoid the quadratic memory scaling of multi-document fusion.

    \subsection{Dataset Partitions and Grouped Leakage Prevention}
    When evaluating machine learning models on constrained datasets, researchers often deploy standard random $k$-fold cross-validation to maximize testing coverage. However, Vabalas et al.~\cite{vabalas2019machine} demonstrated that applying naive random cross-validation to datasets under 1,000 items without strict group isolation introduces severe optimistic bias, causing models to appear artificially accurate due to sample overlap or information leakage across folds. In structured legal and paired-text scenarios where multiple hypothesis statements share an identical statutory premise, random sample splitting permits premise-side semantic features to bleed across training and evaluation partitions. To prevent optimistic bias in few-shot legal scenarios, contemporary evaluation frameworks require both static Train/Validation/Test partitions (e.g., 70/15/15) for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping, alongside premise-grouped cross-validation (GroupKFold) where entire statutory premise clusters are quarantined into either the training or evaluation partition to prevent cross-fold leakage.

    Beyond preventing optimistic bias, using a static partition allows for validation monitoring during fine-tuning. In datasets with limited volume, neural networks are susceptible to memorizing the training data rather than learning underlying logical patterns. Goodfellow et al.~\cite{goodfellow2016deep} establish that isolating a validation set allows architectures to employ early stopping criteria and halt training when performance on unseen validation data begins to degrade. Furthermore, as Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2021revisiting} note regarding few-sample fine-tuning, static separation enforces document-level boundaries and prevents clauses from a single draft ordinance from bleeding across training and test sets.

    \subsection{Minimum Detectable Effect}
    A standard critique of static splits on small datasets is that the resulting test set lacks the statistical power to reliably compare candidate architectures. However, Card et al.~\cite{card2020with} established that a test set's validity depends on its Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) at a target power (commonly 80\% power at $\alpha = 0.05$) rather than arbitrary size thresholds alone. Formally, statistical power ($1 - \beta$) quantifies the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis ($H_0$) when a true performance disparity exists between competing model architectures. In standard hypothesis testing, required sample size $N$ scales inversely with the square of the standardized effect size ($\delta = |\mu_1 - \mu_2|/\sigma$). Detecting marginal accuracy differences ($1\%\text{--}2\%$) requires large evaluation corpora ($N > 10^4$), whereas comparing fine-tuned models against unadapted zero-shot baselines with large effect sizes ($\delta > 0.80$) yields adequate power with holdout partitions of roughly 50 pairs.

    The practical application of MDE shifts how model performance is evaluated in specialized domains. In general NLP research, large test sets are required because models are often evaluated on benchmarks like GLUE to verify incremental gains of one to two percentage points over prior baselines~\cite{wang2019glue}. However, applying a base foundation model with no prior knowledge of local governance to a specialized legal task often yields poor unadapted baseline accuracy~\cite{chalkidis2020legal}. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2021when} report that on specialized legal tasks such as CaseHOLD, domain-adapted fine-tuning achieves substantial gains over general baselines (a 7.2\% F1 gain, or 12\% relative improvement over BERT), whereas unadapted models struggle on legal nuance. Because expected domain-adaptation deltas are large, legal NLP evaluations do not require thousands of test pairs to detect statistically valid differences, indicating that roughly 50 curated pairs can capture substantial leaps in reasoning accuracy.

\section{Explainable AI}
In legal informatics, a ``black box'' prediction is fundamentally unacceptable, regardless of nominal classification accuracy. Automated systems that flag normative conflicts between statutory enactments must provide verifiable reasoning (\textit{ratio decidendi}) behind their classifications to genuinely assist legislative analysis~\cite{doshi2017towards}. Without transparent justification, legal practitioners and legislative staff cannot trust, audit, or formally defend algorithmic determinations. Consequently, contemporary legal NLP literature emphasizes Explainable AI (XAI) not as an optional secondary layer, but as an intrinsic structural requirement of high-stakes legal decision support. This stance prioritizes faithful mathematical traces over ungrounded generative summaries.

    \subsection{Intrinsic Explainability}
    Within the explainability literature, researchers distinguish between Post-Hoc Surrogate Models and Intrinsic Explainability Mechanisms. Surrogate methods, such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)~\cite{ribeiro2016why} and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)~\cite{lundberg2017unified}, attempt to approximate the behavior of complex deep networks by fitting a simplified, interpretable linear model around individual predictions. Formally, intrinsic explainability extracts feature attributions directly from the internal mathematical parameters and representations of the model during inference. In contrast, post-hoc surrogate methods treat the deep neural network as an unobservable black box, approximating local decision boundaries by iteratively perturbing input tokens thousands of times and fitting auxiliary linear models over the resulting output shifts~\cite{rudin2019stop}. In text classification benchmarks, these external explainers are computationally demanding and inherently prone to approximation errors, as they explain an external proxy rather than the underlying network itself~\cite{rudin2019stop}. Instead, intrinsic approaches extract internal model representations (such as self-attention weights and contextualized hidden states) directly from transformer layers during the forward pass and offer a computationally lightweight mechanism for structural transparency.
    
    While using attention weights as a proxy for feature importance has sparked debate, empirical findings support their utility for targeted textual inspection. Jain and Wallace~\cite{jain2019attention} argued that raw attention distributions do not always correlate consistently with gradient-based feature importance. However, Wiegreffe and Pinter~\cite{wiegreffe2019attention} provided counter-evidence under diagnostic testing, showing that attention mechanisms can faithfully reflect internal prioritization. In statutory conflict detection and deontic reasoning, extracting token-level attention spans allows reviewers to identify the specific conflicting provisions (for example, isolating an ``absolute prohibition'' in a superior statute against an ``unauthorized permit'' in a local draft). This mapping establishes a direct visual link between textual evidence and classification decisions without incurring the computational overhead of auxiliary surrogate models.
    
    Complementing structural attention heatmaps in modern explainability frameworks is Confidence Calibration. Deep learning models, particularly multi-layer transformers, frequently exhibit severe overconfidence in erroneous predictions when evaluated on out-of-domain or specialized text. As Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017calibration} demonstrated, modern neural networks trained with standard cross-entropy loss are systematically miscalibrated, exhibiting pronounced overconfidence where output probability vectors assign extreme values that exceed the true empirical likelihood of correct classification. In high-stakes regulatory auditing, uncalibrated softmax scores generate distorted risk assessments. To restore probabilistic reliability, calibration literature advocates post-hoc temperature scaling applied directly to pre-softmax logit vectors. Temperature scaling introduces a learned strictly positive scalar parameter $\tau > 0$ that rescales logit magnitudes before softmax normalization without modifying the rank-order of class predictions; this formulation minimizes Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and aligns predicted probabilities with observed accuracy across decision thresholds.

    \subsection{Edge Deployment Feasibility}
    In edge and on-premise computing environments, operational feasibility requires strict adherence to memory and latency boundaries. Post-hoc explainability methods operate by perturbing input tokens or evaluating feature coalitions across many forward passes per document to approximate Shapley values or linear boundary slopes~\cite{ribeiro2016why, lundberg2017unified}. Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2020problems} examine the computational complexity of Shapley-value feature attributions, which require evaluating marginal contributions over subsets of features and can introduce misleading out-of-distribution estimates. From a systems perspective, Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020edge} establish that edge workstations operate under strict latency and memory budgets that constrain heavy iterative computation. In resource-constrained deployment settings, intrinsic extraction of native attention weights and calibrated logits provides a computationally viable pathway to transparency without requiring high-performance computing clusters.

    \subsection{Explanation Quality Metrics}
    Evaluating explanation quality in natural language processing requires distinguishing between two fundamental evaluation criteria: Plausibility and Faithfulness. Formally, \textit{plausibility} measures the extrinsic degree to which an explanation appears convincing and domain-intuitive to human legal practitioners, whereas \textit{faithfulness} measures the intrinsic fidelity with which an explanation reflects the actual internal computational mechanics and feature weights used by the model to produce its output~\cite{jacovi2020towards, doshi2017towards}. Jacovi and Goldberg~\cite{jacovi2020towards} demonstrate that optimizing explanations purely for plausibility frequently produces deceptive explanations that mask internal reasoning failures and biases. In legal domains, scholarship emphasizes that faithfulness must take precedence, as an explanation that sounds plausible while misrepresenting statutory text can lead to serious compliance failures.
    
    This trade-off is particularly evident in generative explainability approaches that deploy auto-regressive large language models to draft plain-language explanations of legal predictions. While generative summaries achieve high human plausibility scores, empirical legal informatics studies demonstrate that generative models frequently suffer from legal hallucinations, fabricating non-existent statutory citations or altering legal interpretations during text generation~\cite{linna2026challenges, katz2024gpt4}. Because generated natural language explanations are decoupled from the classifier's internal mathematical representations, they remain inherently unfaithful~\cite{jacovi2020towards}. Consequently, legal informatics literature increasingly favors extractive and attention-grounded visualizations that display the exact mathematical spans influencing classification to eliminate generative distortions.

    \subsection{Explainability in Multi-Stage Pipelines}
    In multi-stage NLP pipelines, literature on Explainable AI emphasizes situating interpretability mechanisms at the fine-grained inference stage rather than the coarse retrieval phase. In retrieve-then-entail systems, the initial retrieval phase functions as a high-throughput candidate selector across extensive document repositories. As Anand et al.~\cite{anand2020explainable} observe, computing dense feature attributions during coarse document filtering provides negligible analytical utility while incurring prohibitive computational latency. In contrast, the subsequent cross-encoder inference stage conducts fine-grained semantic entailment across a constrained candidate set; this focus makes it the mathematically appropriate locus for interpretability analysis.

    At this second stage, legal NLP frameworks use token-level saliency and attention visualizations to bridge model internals with human inspection~\cite{vig2019bertviz}. Mapping cross-attention weights and calibrated class probabilities back to corresponding spans in the premise and hypothesis texts provides structural verification of detected contradictions without generative paraphrasing risks. This dual mechanism of intrinsic attention mapping and post-hoc confidence calibration allows legislative researchers to trace classification outputs directly back to source statutory phrases. Thus, Stage~2 explainability supports human verification without slowing down Stage~1 corpus retrieval.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
As established in legal informatics literature, the Retrieve-then-Entail architecture represents the leading theoretical framework for statutory conflict detection and legislative consistency checking. Originating in the international benchmarks of the Competition on Legal Information Extraction and Entailment (COLIEE 2025 and 2026)~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, coliee2026proceedings}, this framework integrates two complementary theoretical pillars: a scalable Information Retrieval (IR) architecture to isolate relevant statutory context from expansive corpora, followed by a fine-grained Natural Language Inference (NLI) architecture for rapid deontic conflict detection. Specifically, this theoretical framework synthesizes the dense statutory retrieval methodology of Team AIIR Lab~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}, the multi-stage ensemble retrieval principles of Team JNLP~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, and the ModernBERT-based encoder classification methodology of Team KIS~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}.

    \subsection{Team AIIR Statutory Retrieval}
    In statute law retrieval, dense bi-encoder architectures offer an effective theoretical foundation for bridging the semantic gap between newly drafted provisions and expansive statutory corpora without relying on computationally demanding generative text decoding or manual taxonomy engineering~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}. Drawing from the empirical methodology developed by the AIIR Lab in recent COLIEE benchmarks, this retrieval pipeline operates across four theoretical dimensions: domain-adapted data augmentation, semantic vectorization, corpus pre-computation, and nearest-neighbor distance evaluation. This approach enables efficient candidate filtering before fine-grained logical verification begins. The dense bi-encoder workflow is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:phase1aiir}.

    To operationalize this dense bi-encoder architecture, the pipeline executes across four theoretical dimensions. First, targeted data augmentation and domain adaptation expose dense retrieval models to synthetically augmented legal variants, training the neural network to recognize identical statutory concepts across archaic terminology and modern drafting syntax to enhance robustness against lexical divergence. Second, semantic vectorization via bi-encoders (such as all-mpnet-base-v2) projects statutory provisions into a dense, continuous vector space where queries and corpus documents are encoded independently, optimizing embedding coordinates such that logically relevant statutory pairs exhibit high geometric proximity under cosine distance. Third, offline corpus pre-computation and vector indexing pre-calculate static embeddings across the statutory repository within an indexed vector database, decoupling computationally intensive encoding from live runtime queries and allowing dense retrieval to scale across extensive archives without real-time GPU re-encoding. Fourth, live query vectorization and proximity scoring project incoming draft provisions into the shared embedding space, enabling approximate nearest-neighbor search to calculate continuous proximity scores rapidly and deliver a high-recall candidate shortlist for downstream inference.

    \subsection{Team JNLP Multi-Stage Retrieval}
    Complementing pure bi-encoder retrieval, literature in computational law emphasizes multi-stage retrieval pipelines to balance candidate recall against ranking precision. Exemplified by Team JNLP's high-performing architecture in COLIEE benchmarks~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}, this multi-stage pipeline progressively filters statutory search spaces across three structured phases: Pre-Retrieval Candidate Pruning, Model Fine-Tuning, and Result Ensembling. Separating initial pruning from neural re-ranking keeps overall search tractable across large statutory collections. This multi-stage workflow is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:phase1jnlp}.

This cascaded structure provides a practical rationale for combining sparse lexical matching with dense semantic embeddings in statutory search. In the initial phase, heuristic distance thresholds and lexical filters eliminate clearly unrelated enactments, after which fine-tuned language models compute calibrated relevance scores across the remaining shortlist. Combining predictions through rank fusion retains both keyword specificity and conceptual semantic coverage in the resulting candidate set.

        \subsubsection{Pre-Retrieval}
        The primary objective of the pre-retrieval phase is to drastically reduce the expansive statutory search space while maintaining high recall across governing provisions. Team JNLP achieves this through a multi-stage candidate pruning procedure combining distance-based heuristic filtering with neural re-ranking~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}. Dense embeddings for statutory queries and corpus provisions are extracted using high-capacity multilingual representations. Rather than relying solely on raw cosine similarity, the methodology calculates multi-dimensional distance metrics (such as L1 distance bins), training a gradient-boosted decision tree to predict relevance and counter severe sample imbalance. Following initial filtering, a specialized passage re-ranker evaluates candidate texts and eliminates non-relevant provisions; this step narrows the candidate pool to top-ranked candidates while preserving recall.

        \subsubsection{Model Fine-Tuning}
        Once the candidate space is constrained, the framework applies targeted natural language models to perform fine-grained relevance scoring. Because fine-tuning large models across full parameter sets is computationally intensive, legal NLP frameworks employ Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques, such as LoRA and QLoRA. These techniques enable foundational language models to adapt to specialized statutory semantics with minimal memory overhead. Furthermore, because statutory candidate sets exhibit severe class imbalance where only a small minority of retrieved provisions contain true preemption conflicts, sampling strategies and asymmetric loss functions are applied during training to preserve sensitivity to positive conflict signals.

        \subsubsection{Result Ensemble}
        To maximize retrieval robustness and mitigate individual model variance, multi-stage retrieval pipelines aggregate relevance scores across distinct retrieval architectures. Relevance predictions are aggregated through weighted score aggregation; this process combines calibrated probabilities from complementary lexical and neural models. The weighting coefficients assigned to individual scoring components are optimized via systematic hyperparameter search algorithms such as Optuna. This optimization ensures balanced contributions between lexical keyword precision and dense semantic recall to deliver a stable candidate shortlist for downstream reasoning.

    \subsection{Team KIS Textual Entailment}
    In the second phase of legal retrieve-then-entail architectures, the theoretical objective transitions from broad candidate discovery to fine-grained deontic reasoning. To maintain high computational throughput and deterministic classification, modern legal NLP frameworks deploy non-generative cross-encoder architectures, exemplified by the ModernBERT-based rationale extraction methodology developed by Team KIS~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}. Evaluating paired sequences through bidirectional attention avoids the citation hallucination risks of autoregressive decoders.     \subsection{Theoretical Synthesis}
This synthesis integrates the foundational theoretical models reviewed throughout this chapter into a unified framework for ex-ante legislative conflict detection. While each individual methodology addresses an isolated phase of legal document processing, their conceptual integration establishes a principled retrieve-then-entail architecture. The theoretical foundation for statutory candidate discovery draws from the dense retrieval methodology established by the AIIR Lab~\cite{wu2025aiir, largey2026aiirlab}. Their approach demonstrates that targeted domain augmentation mitigates OCR noise in archival legal text, while continuous semantic vectorization enables high-throughput candidate identification across large statutory collections without relying on rigid keyword indices.

To balance candidate recall against precision, the framework incorporates the multi-stage ranking principles demonstrated by Team JNLP~\cite{coliee2025proceedings, goebel2026an}. This methodology uses lexical filtering (BM25) to prune extraneous documents, followed by neural scoring and rank fusion to isolate authoritative candidate enactments on standard computing hardware. For granular deontic conflict evaluation, the framework adopts the Team KIS encoder-based classification approach~\cite{kadowaki2025modernbert}. Formatting premise-hypothesis pairs into a continuous sequence evaluated via lightweight cross-encoders with token-level separation boundaries classifies logical relationships without generative hallucination risks.

Synthesizing these foundational frameworks establishes an end-to-end theoretical basis for automated preemption auditing. The retrieval stage narrows extensive statutory search spaces down to high-probability candidate provisions, while the textual entailment stage conducts deterministic normative conflict checks. Recent findings from the COLIEE 2026 proceedings~\cite{coliee2026proceedings, ngo2026nowj} reinforce this architectural consensus, validating that pipelined retrieval and textual entailment provide a reliable basis for automated legal consistency analysis. Keeping both stages non-generative and interpretable supports reproducible screening under local workstation limits.
