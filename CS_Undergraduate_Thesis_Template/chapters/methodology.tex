\chapter{Methodology}
This chapter explains the methodology used to build and evaluate the Coarse-to-Fine Semantic Conflict Detection System. It describes how Philippine legal texts are collected and cleaned, and how the two-stage Retrieve-then-Entail architecture is organized. The chapter also details the creation of the ground truth dataset and the selection of candidate models. Finally, it outlines the evaluation metrics and hardware constraints, which match the computing capacity of standard Local Government Unit (LGU) office workstations.


\section{Conceptual Framework}
This section maps how data flows through the proposed system. The architecture follows an Input-Process-Output (IPO) design organized into a two-stage ``Coarse-to-Fine'' retrieve-then-entail pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:conceptualframework}). In information retrieval and natural language processing, a coarse-to-fine retrieve-then-entail architecture is a cascaded processing design that couples a fast, broad candidate search phase with a computationally intensive, fine-grained semantic classification phase. In this research, Stage~1 is the coarse retrieval stage: it rapidly scans the dual statutory corpus across national statutes and local ordinances using hybrid sparse-dense search to filter the collection down to a compact shortlist of top-$k$ ($k = 50$) candidate provisions. Stage~2 is the fine inference stage: it pairs the draft ordinance section with each retrieved candidate statute to perform deep bidirectional cross-attention and compute the calibrated probability of a substantive legal contradiction. By decoupling high-throughput statutory candidate retrieval from compute-intensive cross-encoder inference, this two-stage design avoids the quadratic memory limits ($O(n^2)$) and runtime overhead that otherwise prohibit whole-corpus cross-attention; semantic conflict screening can therefore run on standard local government office computers without cloud GPU clusters or dedicated data centers. The workflow is divided into two operational phases: Phase~1 (Offline Foundation and Optimization) and Phase~2 (Live Online Retrieve-then-Entail Pipeline).

The flowchart depicts an Input-Process-Output workflow organized into modular processing layers; it shows how heterogeneous statutory archives are ingested, normalized, indexed, and screened for conflicts under local edge compute constraints. In Phase~2, an ex-ante ordinance draft undergoes structural boundary parsing and procedural boilerplate filtering before being routed through Stage~1 hybrid retrieval (fusing lexical BM25 and dense bi-encoder streams via Reciprocal Rank Fusion) and Stage~2 Cross-Encoder inference. This coarse-to-fine design restricts compute-intensive cross-attention to a focused candidate shortlist ($k=50$), allowing semantic conflict detection to execute on standard local government desktop workstations while producing transparent, explainable audit reports.

    \subsection{Phase 1: Offline Foundation and Optimization}
    This preliminary phase establishes the empirical foundation of the system by compiling the statutory corpus, building pre-computed retrieval indexes, and selecting candidate transformer models through structured benchmark screening. During dual-corpus ingestion and pre-computation, the system compiles a dual-jurisdiction statutory archive combining the Philippine national statutory collection (Republic Acts, Executive Orders, Presidential Decrees, Acts, Batas Pambansa, and Commonwealth Acts) with Davao City local legislation (archived landmark ordinances and digital LISSP ordinance records). The compiled corpus undergoes legal text normalization, optical character recognition (OCR) noise mitigation on historical scans, and structural section segmentation into granular, section-level regulatory units. Cleaned statutory provisions are then indexed offline: dense semantic representations are pre-computed using a lightweight transformer bi-encoder, while sparse inverted lexical indexes are constructed using BM25 ($k_1 = 1.5, b = 0.75$) alongside unsupervised statutory topic modeling. In parallel, the system constructs an offline Statutory Knowledge Graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$. Deterministic statutory parsers extract cross-statute citation networks and amendatory legislative lineages across national acts and city ordinances, mapping structural relationships ($\text{CONTAINS}$, $\text{CITES\_AUTHORITY}$, $\text{AMENDS}$, $\text{REPEALS}$, and $\text{GOVERNS\_DOMAIN}$) to establish topological validity and authority baselines prior to live query execution.

    Following corpus indexing, model benchmarking and selection evaluates candidate neural architectures against a curated Ground Truth Legal Benchmark stratified across three granular difficulty tiers: Tier~1 (Surface Lexical), Tier~2 (Structural Preemption), and Tier~3 (Latent Semantic). A multi-stage empirical screening and ablation process evaluates candidate bi-encoders by balancing inference latency against candidate Recall@$k$, alongside candidate cross-encoders evaluated on conflict detection precision and inference latency across full-precision and quantized configurations. Based on these empirical evaluations, the highest-performing models are selected for the prototype pipeline: an optimal dense bi-encoder for Stage~1 candidate retrieval, and an optimal fine-tuned cross-encoder for Stage~2 contradiction reasoning.

    \subsection{Phase 2: Live System Workflow}
    The live system executes the automated conflict detection pipeline through an Input-Process-Output structure when a newly drafted local ordinance is submitted for pre-enactment review. The workflow begins with system input ingestion, where a legislative researcher uploads a newly proposed ex-ante ordinance draft. Because draft measures originate within local government offices as digital-born documents (.docx or digital PDF), they are free from optical scanning noise. The ingestion pipeline segments the draft into individual substantive provisions, filtering out non-regulatory procedural boilerplate such as the Title, Separability, Repealing, and Effectivity clauses. The isolated substantive provisions are then vectorized in real time via the selected dense bi-encoder alongside sparse lexical token extraction.

    The computational process core then executes two complementary stages: hybrid candidate retrieval followed by natural language inference. In Stage~1 (Dual-Stream Hybrid Information Retrieval), the system narrows the comprehensive statutory search space down to a focused candidate pool. Stream~A executes lexical sparse search via BM25 to match exact statutory citations and numerical penalty amounts alongside administrative agency terms, while Stream~B executes dense semantic search via the selected bi-encoder, measuring cosine similarity between the draft provision and statutory embeddings to capture abstract regulatory intent. The ranked outputs are combined using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) integrated with a Statutory Hierarchy Safeguard that prioritizes higher-ranking national statutes over local provisions. To preserve candidate coverage when draft clauses reference statutory families omitted from initial lexical and dense rankings, Stage~1 integrates a graph-guided neighborhood expansion: explicit citations in the draft provision trigger localized multi-hop traversals over the Statutory Knowledge Graph, pulling governing national acts and amendatory predecessors directly into the top-$k$ candidate pool without exhausting local LGU memory.

    In Stage~2 (Natural Language Inference), each retrieved statutory candidate is paired with the draft ordinance provision into a single cross-encoder sequence. This sequence-pair classification formulation follows Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} and its adaptation to transformer cross-encoders by Reimers and Gurevych~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. The joint input sequence is formatted as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:cross_encoder_seq}:

    \noindent\textit{Cross-Encoder Joint Sequence Input Representation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{x} = [\text{CLS}] \, \text{Statutory Premise} \, [\text{SEP}] \, \text{Ordinance Hypothesis} \, [\text{SEP}]
        \label{eq:cross_encoder_seq}
        \eqcaption{Cross-Encoder Joint Sequence Input Representation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $[\text{CLS}]$ marks the classification sequence start and $[\text{SEP}]$ separates jurisdictional text boundaries.

    Equation~\ref{eq:cross_encoder_seq} combines the retrieved statutory section and the draft ordinance provision into a single token sequence separated by delimiter tokens. This allows every word in the statutory premise to interact directly with every word in the draft hypothesis through bidirectional cross-attention. This direct comparison is necessary because legal conflicts often depend on small details (such as specific penalty ceilings or statutory exceptions) that independent bi-encoder vectors can miss. The Stage~2 pipeline also cross-references candidate provisions against the Statutory Knowledge Graph to check amendatory status under \textit{lex posterior derogat legi priori} so that superseded statutory text is flagged before cross-attention scoring. The model's classification head then calculates calibrated probabilities across Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction, and outputs a conflict probability $P(\text{Contradiction})$.

    Finally, the system presents an audit trail displaying the top-$k$ shortlisted laws for human review. It compares the contradiction probability against a calibrated decision threshold $\tau^*$, tuned with an $F_2$-score weighting to prioritize catching true conflicts. When the probability meets or exceeds this threshold ($P(\text{Contradiction}) \ge \tau^*$), the system flags a potential legal conflict and classifies it into one of two alert types: a Vertical Preemption Alert for conflicts with superior national statutes under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine, or a Horizontal Inconsistency Notice for contradictions or overlaps with existing Davao City ordinances. Confirmed preemption contradictions are dynamically registered as prospective $\text{CONFLICTS\_WITH}$ relations within the knowledge graph. To help legislative researchers interpret the result, the user interface displays side-by-side text comparisons and highlighted conflicting phrases alongside model confidence scores. If the probability remains below the threshold ($P(\text{Contradiction}) < \tau^*$), the system reports that no candidate contradictions exceeded the screening threshold across national statutes or prior local enactments.


\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:research_design}
    This study uses an applied research design to build a practical software tool for local governance. The proponents use an experimental systems development approach to reduce the delays of manual ordinance review through automated screening. This setup connects principles from legal informatics and natural language processing to address a real local need. By focusing on practical transformer-based models, the design connects theoretical computer science with everyday government office work. The experiment tests whether a retrieve-then-entail pipeline can operate reliably in a local legislative setting.

    The experimental setup evaluates the retrieval and classification modules separately to measure search speed and accuracy independently. Building the system step-by-step allows the proponents to adjust the information retrieval and natural language inference stages using local legal texts. This engineering process ensures that the finished tool accounts for the writing styles found in both city ordinances and national statutes. In this way, applied research methods help turn modern NLP concepts into a usable tool for the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod. As a result, the findings establish a benchmark for automating pre-enactment legal checks on local legislation.

    The experimental design incorporates an incremental ablation methodology to evaluate both isolated components and composite system interactions. The pipeline is evaluated under two distinct structural configurations: a baseline coarse-to-fine retrieve-then-entail architecture coupling lexical BM25 and dense bi-encoders with cross-encoder NLI, and a neuro-symbolic graph-augmented pipeline incorporating the Statutory Knowledge Graph for candidate expansion and topological lineage verification. By benchmarking both architectures across the multi-tier legal dataset, the experimental design isolates the individual contributions of sparse keyword matching, dense latent semantics, cross-attention contradiction reasoning, and relational statutory network traversal. This comparative setup tests whether graph topological augmentation improves retrieval recall on unmentioned statutory preemption authorities while remaining within edge hardware constraints.

\section{Corpus Acquisition and Exploratory Analysis}
    \label{sec:corpus_acquisition}
    Building the legal database requires collecting text from different public repositories so that the system includes as many relevant laws as possible. These sources provide the national statutes and city ordinances needed to check draft legislation for consistency. To keep the language consistent and make vector processing simpler, the system gathers only the English-language versions of all legal texts. This choice matches Philippine statutory rules under Section~20, Chapter~5, Book~I of Executive Order No.~292 (Administrative Code of 1987)~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, which explicitly mandates that in the interpretation of laws promulgated in official languages, the English text shall control unless otherwise specifically provided. National statutes and local ordinances are gathered using separate automated scripts designed for the format of each source.

        \subsection{Corpus Composition}
        The Lawphil Project (Arellano Law Foundation) is the primary digitized extraction source for national statutory text because its documents are provided in clean HTML with a consistent layout and broad historical coverage~\cite{lawphil2000}. Automated scraping scripts browse through chronological and alphabetical indexes to collect Republic Acts, Batas Pambansa, Commonwealth Acts, Acts, Executive Orders, and Presidential Decrees. Using a custom web extraction pipeline built by the proponents, statutory extraction was conducted as of March 2026 and collected 25,432 unique legal records enacted between 1900 and 2026. The Official Gazette of the Republic of the Philippines~\cite{officialgazette} and the Chan Robles Virtual Law Library were used as secondary cross-references to verify enactment dates and chronological numbering, as well as historical amendment status. All collected national laws are converted into standardized JSON records with metadata, such as approval dates and section titles. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lawphil_web_interface}, the Lawphil repository presents statutory text in clean, unstyled HTML typography with a predictable hierarchical URL schema (e.g., \texttt{/statutes/repacts/ra2018/ra\_11035\_2018.html} for Republic Act No.~11035). This standardized structure separates introductory legislative headers from operative provisions and eliminates the need for dynamic JavaScript browser rendering; this predictable markup supports fault-tolerant web scraping.

In contrast, Lawphil keeps a standard HTML structure across all historical eras, clearly separating introductory preambles from operative legal sections. Scraping from this repository ensured uniform text formatting across the national collection (over 25,000 enactments) so that formatting differences do not skew vector embeddings during Stage~1 retrieval.             })

        Listing~\ref{lst:corpus_extraction} details the programmatic execution logic of the statutory web extraction script, outlining the sequential functions for annual index traversal and DOM parsing, followed by metadata extraction and provision boundary segmentation (with full scraping and dependency routines archived in Appendix~\ref{app:code_ingestion}). The script crawls legislative archives spanning from 1900 through 2026, parsing HTML document trees via BeautifulSoup while implementing rate-limiting delays to preserve source server availability. During execution, the pipeline extracts key statutory metadata (including enactment identifiers and approval dates) before passing the document body to a regex-based section segmenter that isolates individual statutory provisions into distinct JSON fields. Methodologically, this automated extraction provides uniform data serialization across all 25,432 national enactments, eliminating web scraping artifacts so that downstream embedding models evaluate pure statutory syntax.

        To capture the complete statutory search space, the national collection is paired with Davao City local legislation gathered across two complementary acquisition streams. The first channel consists of the official Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod Public Web Portal, which provides selective historical landmark ordinances and city uploads spanning approximately fifteen years across regulatory codes such as public safety and zoning~\cite{spdavao2026}. The second and primary channel comprises the local Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP), acquired directly through formal administrative coordination with the Sangguniang Panlungsod Secretariat and Information Technology division~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. Through this institutional coordination, local authorities granted the proponents direct access to digitized enactments via a shared repository for enactments passed primarily by the 19th and 20th City Councils between 2021 and 2025. Together, these two channels yielded 1,925 raw ordinance documents spanning from 1950 to 2025, which deduplicate on canonical enactment identifiers across archival folders to exactly 1,816 unique local ordinances.

        These 1,816 deduplicated documents represent the complete accessible digital corpus gathered within the proponents' institutional capabilities. In practice, the true cumulative historical total of ordinances enacted since Davao City was chartered under Commonwealth Act No.~51 in 1936 remains unrecorded online and unknown to local authorities. In institutional consultations, Sangguniang Panlungsod personnel confirmed that an official cumulative ledger does not exist because local legislation is numbered sequentially per calendar year and council term rather than in an unbroken cumulative sequence. Furthermore, the vast majority of historical enactments, particularly those prior to the 2021 LISSP digital initiative, exist exclusively as physical paper documents preserved in archive rooms and filing cabinets at the Sangguniang Panlungsod building on San Pedro Street; tallying them would require a manual page-by-page physical inventory. When combined with the 25,432 national statutes, the canonical statutory search space comprises 27,248 unique legal enactments ($N_{\text{sections}} = 177{,}423$).

        \subsection{Corpus Characteristics and Inherent Biases}
        \label{sec:corpus_biases}
        A close look at the collected national laws reveals a large imbalance across legal topics. Over 40\% of the national statutes consist of routine administrative actions, such as converting local schools, renaming roads, adjusting municipal boundaries, or granting broadcast franchises. In contrast, major regulatory areas that interact with city powers, such as public health and local taxation, make up less than 15\% of the total national collection. Searching through this raw distribution without filtering would cause search algorithms to return irrelevant administrative laws instead of actual regulatory rules. To keep search results useful, the system must separate these routine administrative laws from substantive regulatory provisions.

        The local ordinance archive also shows an uneven time distribution and gaps in older records. For ordinances passed before 2021, city record-keeping mainly focused on digitizing major ``Landmark Ordinances,'' leaving ordinary regulations from earlier decades in physical paper storage~\cite{spdavao2026}. As a result, the pre-2021 local records represent a selective collection of prominent laws rather than a complete legislative record. While post-2021 records from the digital LISSP database are much more complete, some numerical gaps remain due to missing committee drafts and unindexed attachments. These temporal biases mean that the available local archive contains substantially more recent ordinances than older enactments.

        A fundamental technical distinction must be maintained regarding the document media of the legislative corpus. All 1,816 deduplicated archival ordinance documents (from 1,925 collected files) received from the Sangguniang Panlungsod are historical scanned physical paper PDFs, rather than digital-born documents exported from word processing software. Older ordinances scanned at low resolution suffer from faded print, ink bleed-through, staple shadows, and rotational skew, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:davao_ordinance_scan}. The scanned copies also contain non-text items such as government stamps and handwritten marginal notes that confuse standard sentence splitting tools. Clean, digital-born text (documents exported directly from modern word processors or Google Docs without scanning noise) appears exclusively during Phase~2 live system testing when legislative staff submit newly drafted ex-ante measures for pre-enactment conflict review. At the language level, local ordinances sometimes include Cebuano and Filipino expressions in their introductory sections~\cite{dreisbach2021language}. These informal drafting habits differ noticeably from the standardized formal English found in national laws.

A machine learning model trained on such unbalanced data could score 99\% nominal accuracy simply by always predicting ``Neutral,'' while remaining unable to detect real legal conflicts.

        A key methodological decision in compiling this database is the explicit inclusion of Executive Orders (EOs) alongside congressional legislation. Under Article~VII, Section~17 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution and Book~III of Executive Order No.~292~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, Executive Orders are presidential acts providing rules of a general character in execution of statutory mandates. Unlike Supreme Court decisions that settle private adversarial controversies under \textit{stare decisis}, Executive Orders operate as \textit{ex-ante}, forward-looking statutory instruments possessing the full force of law. Modern Executive Orders establish binding nationwide regulatory mandates, such as Executive Order No.~26 (s.~2017) mandating nationwide public smoking bans, that legally preempt inconsistent local ordinances. Excluding Executive Orders would create an artificial regulatory gap and leave local contradictions against operative presidential mandates undetected.

        The coverage rates show that the national legal archive is nearly complete across all historical governance eras. Commonwealth Acts show full 100.00\% coverage across all 733 enactments, while American-era Acts (99.58\%) and Batas Pambansa (99.66\%) miss only 18 and 3 enactments, respectively. For Republic Acts, the 11,963 collected records cover 97.16\% of the 12,313 official total, with missing entries mainly being local bills or recent laws still being indexed. For Presidential Decrees, the 1,844 gathered records represent 90.57\% of the 2,036 numbered decrees from the Martial Law period, where uncollected entries were unpublished directives until the Supreme Court required full publication in \textit{Tañada v. Tuvera}~\cite{sc1985tanadavtuvera}. For Executive Orders, the 5,749 collected records represent 92.65\% of the 6,205 numbered orders issued across fifteen presidential terms and provide an extensive record of administrative policy; the official total of 6,205 sums the maximum numbered issuances across all fifteen presidential administrations (1935--2026) and colonial governorships (1901--1935) recorded in the Official Gazette and Lawphil archives as of March 2026.

        During preliminary exploratory research, the proponents conducted initial machine learning experiments on the national statutory archive and identified severe dataset imbalances. In the national collection of 25,432 statutes, routine executive issuances and administrative reorganizations comprise nearly half of all historical enactments, while specialized regulatory domains remain relatively small. Searching for a local regulatory ordinance across this unfiltered raw collection causes severe search drift because dense retrieval models readily match repetitive administrative phrasing across unrelated legal areas. To address these imbalances, the proponents formalized a two-stage topic modeling architecture that organizes the statutory archive into distinct thematic subject areas. In natural language processing, topic modeling is an unsupervised learning technique that discovers latent semantic themes across unstructured text corpora and partitions documents by shared topical content. In this pipeline, topic modeling partitions the 25,432 national statutes into distinct regulatory categories (such as public health and local taxation) so that routine administrative directives do not dominate the candidate shortlist during coarse retrieval.

        The topic classification pipeline groups documents automatically while running efficiently on Host~A. Statutory texts are first stripped of HTML tags and filtered against a curated list of 49 domain-specific legal stopwords, such as ``hereby'' and ``enacted'' (the complete stopword roster is cataloged in Appendix~\ref{app:lexicons_stopwords}), which appear throughout legal texts without helping distinguish legal topics. In information retrieval, term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) is a numerical statistic reflecting how important a word is to a document within a collection: terms that appear ubiquitously across all enactments receive minimal weights, whereas distinctive domain terms (such as ``quarantine'' or ``excise'') receive elevated weights~\cite{manning2008introduction}. Cleaned texts are converted into TF-IDF feature vectors across a 25,000-term vocabulary so that distinctive statutory vocabulary outweighs routine boilerplate. To handle vocabulary redundancy across tens of thousands of statutes, Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) performs linear dimensionality reduction grounded in Latent Semantic Analysis~\cite{deerwester1990indexing}, projecting the sparse 25,000-term matrix into 64 dense latent semantic dimensions. Following the latent semantic analysis principles of Landauer et al.~\cite{landauer1998introduction}, this 64-dimensional subspace captures underlying legal concepts while eliminating lexical noise, followed by standard $L_2$ vector normalization.

        Dividing statutory texts directly into a small set of broad categories risks mixing unrelated legislative topics. To prevent this, the normalized 64-dimensional vectors are first divided into $K = 28$ fine-grained clusters using MiniBatch $K$-Means clustering~\cite{sculley2010webscale} and then aggregated into 8 macro domains, following hierarchical cluster-reduction principles~\cite{aggarwal2012mining, grootendorst2022bertopic}. Setting $K = 28$ ($3.5\times$ the 8 target macro domains) separates repetitive laws, such as franchise charters or school conversions, into clean sub-clusters before grouping them into broader legal domains. The pipeline then adapts class-based TF-IDF ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$) from Grootendorst~\cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} to extract descriptive domain vocabulary: class-based TF-IDF modifies standard TF-IDF by aggregating all documents assigned to a given cluster into a single composite text, measuring which terms appear frequently within that specific legal category relative to their frequency across the entire statutory archive. The class-based term weighting is computed as follows:

        \noindent\textit{Class-Based Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$):}
        \begin{equation}
            c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF_{i, c} = \frac{|t_{i, c}|}{|c|} \times \log\left(1 + \frac{A}{\sum_{k} |t_{i, k}|}\right)
            \label{eq:c_tfidf}
            \eqcaption{Class-Based Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$)}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $|t_{i, c}|$ denotes the frequency of term $i$ in cluster $c$, $|c|$ is the total word count of cluster $c$, and $A$ indicates the average word count per cluster across the archive. This cluster-level weighting elevates terminology that specifically characterizes a legislative group while penalizing ubiquitous statutory phrasing.

        After fine-grained clusters are aggregated into macro domains, each document must be assigned a calibrated domain membership probability for topic-guided filtering. Following nearest-centroid classification principles~\cite{rocchio1971relevance, aggarwal2012mining} and temperature-scaled probabilistic mapping~\cite{guo2017calibration}, the macro legal domain centroid and calibrated assignment probability are defined in Equation~\ref{eq:macro_centroid}:

        \noindent\textit{Macro Legal Domain Centroid and Softmax Probability:}
        \begin{equation}
            \mathbf{C}_m = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_m} \mathbf{z}_i}{\left\|\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_m} \mathbf{z}_i\right\|_2}, \quad P(m \mid \mathbf{z}_i) = \frac{\exp(\tau \cdot \cos(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{C}_m))}{\sum_{j} \exp(\tau \cdot \cos(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{C}_j))}
            \label{eq:macro_centroid}
            \eqcaption{Macro Legal Domain Centroid and Softmax Probability}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $\mathbf{z}_i$ represents the normalized 64-dimensional SVD vector of document $i$, $\mathbf{C}_m$ denotes the normalized centroid of macro domain $m$, and $\tau = 6.0$ functions as the inverse temperature scaling parameter.

        The formulations in Equations~\ref{eq:c_tfidf} and~\ref{eq:macro_centroid} identify distinctive legal vocabulary and assign consistent topics across the national statutory corpus. Equation~\ref{eq:c_tfidf} calculates class-based TF-IDF ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$), which treats each group of laws as a combined document to find keywords that stand out in that group. In Equation~\ref{eq:macro_centroid}, document vectors are averaged into cluster centers $\mathbf{C}_m$. A temperature-scaled softmax function then converts cosine similarity into probabilities, applying the temperature-scaling formulation of Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017calibration} with $\tau = 6.0$ to keep probability scores well-separated. This allows the system to organize over 25,000 national statutes into eight regulatory domains using standard desktop memory, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:national_macro_domains}.

\section{Preprocessing and Normalization}
\label{sec:preprocessing_filtering}
While Section~\ref{sec:domain_categorization} and Section~\ref{sec:municipal_corpus_inventory} presented the exploratory national macro distributions and preliminary local statutory analysis that motivated our system design, transforming raw scraped gazettes and physical archival scans into dependable machine-readable representations requires source-specific preprocessing and text normalization pipelines. National statutes, local city archives, and live ex-ante drafts each present distinct document media and noise characteristics that must be resolved before provisions can be indexed for retrieval or evaluated for legal conflict. In the national statutory collection, continuous legislative texts must be cleansed of web markup artifacts, segmented into numbered provisions, and enriched with hierarchical metadata. In the local ordinance collection, physical scanning degradation and non-legislative administrative matter must be removed via vision-language modeling and state machine extraction. In live ex-ante drafts, digital-born text must be segmented into substantive clauses while filtering out procedural boilerplate. The following subsections detail the individual normalization architectures developed for each statutory source.

    \subsection{National Statutory Corpus Normalization}
    \label{sec:national_preprocessing}
    \label{sec:boilerplate_and_metadata}
    The national statutory archive comprises 25,432 legislative enactments harvested from online legal repositories. Processing these laws requires resolving web layout artifacts and structuring monolithic legislative texts into context-enriched retrieval units. First, automated regex parsers strip HTML markup and split each continuous statute into numbered provisions. Second, each isolated section is prepended with parent statutory metadata and long sections exceeding token limits are split via sliding-window sub-chunking.

        \subsubsection{Context Prepending and Long-Tail Sub-Chunking}
        \label{sec:national_context_chunking}
        Evaluating isolated statutory sections creates a risk of semantic dilution. In legal document processing, context prepending is a structural representation technique that prefixes parent document metadata (including the statutory instrument type, enactment number, statute title, and section catchline) to each extracted provision prior to tokenization. In statutory retrieval, an isolated regulatory section (such as an administrative penalty clause specifying a fine of five thousand pesos) often contains no explicit mention of the broader subject matter (such as commercial business licensing or building safety). Without parent metadata, dense embedding models generate ambiguous vector representations that match hundreds of unrelated statutory penalty clauses across the corpus. Context prepending resolves this ambiguity by concatenating the governing enactment identity and section title directly to the section text. By embedding this structural context into the input sequence, the transformer attention mechanism conditions the semantic representation of every sentence on its exact legislative source and legal hierarchy. Listing~\ref{lst:context_prepending} details the context prepending procedure implemented to format granular statutory provisions.

        \subsubsection{Vision-Language Document Ingestion Pipeline}
        \label{sec:local_vlm_ingestion}
        Transforming physical city paper archives into machine-readable text requires evaluating candidate document ingestion architectures under consumer hardware constraints. While contemporary measures from the digital LISSP database provide clean electronic text, historical enactments from earlier legislative terms exist predominantly as scanned physical PDF documents. To establish an optimal transcription strategy for the 1,816 local ordinances, the proponents formulated a planned candidate extraction experiment comparing three distinct technological paradigms: traditional open-source OCR via Tesseract v5.x~\cite{smith2007overview}, modern layout-aware deep learning OCR via Surya OCR~\cite{paruchuri2024surya}, and a quantized local vision-language model via Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. As an independent reference standard for transcription accuracy, the evaluation benchmark establishes a gold-standard digitization baseline using Google Gemini Flash 3.8 multimodal extraction coupled with comprehensive line-by-line human legal verification. The candidate pipelines are evaluated across Character Error Rate (CER), Word Error Rate (WER), Table Fidelity (TEDS), and Legal Marker Precision on a representative 60-document stratified test suite, with empirical benchmarking results formally reported in Chapter~4 (Section~\ref{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}).

        To test offline vision-language transcription on consumer hardware, the candidate VLM pipeline is configured around Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, a multimodal transformer that pairs a vision transformer backbone with a 3-billion parameter causal language model decoder. Local baseline testing runs on Host~B (AMD Ryzen 7 7435HS, 16~GB DDR5 memory, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5050 Laptop GPU with 8~GB GDDR6 VRAM under Windows 11), while high-throughput batch runs across remaining archival volumes use Google Kaggle cloud runtimes with NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs. Proprietary cloud document platforms such as Google Cloud Document AI~\cite{google2024documentai} were excluded from routine production due to recurring per-page API fees and legal data sovereignty restrictions under the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No.~10173)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. Configuring an open-weight vision-language candidate addresses these operational constraints by supporting local data control without ongoing API expenses.

        To balance operational feasibility with transcription fidelity across the full archival span, the proponents established a two-tier execution plan dividing local workstation testing from cloud-based batch processing. Contemporary enactments (2021--2025) and initial pipeline calibrations are assigned to Host~B, whereas the high-volume historical backlog (2016--2020) and physically degraded scans identified by the quality audit are routed to Google Kaggle cloud GPU instances~\cite{kaggle2026,bisong2019kaggle} (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_code_kaggle_vlm}). Two hardware considerations motivated this partitioning. Continuous autoregressive decoding across hundreds of multi-page PDF documents induces sustained thermal and power saturation on consumer laptop hardware, so offloading bulk runs prevents prolonged thermal load on Host~B. Furthermore, local laptop execution is bounded by an 8~GB VRAM ceiling that requires 4-bit NormalFloat4 quantization~\cite{dettmers2024qlora}, whereas Kaggle's 16~GB Tesla T4 runtime supports uncompressed 16-bit half-precision (\texttt{torch.bfloat16}) at higher spatial resolutions to test whether reduced quantization loss lowers error rates on faded or carbon-bleed scans~\cite{zheng2025empiricalstudyqwen3quantization,bai2025qwen25vl,baird1993defect}.

        The document ingestion pipeline begins by recursively discovering target PDF files across archival city directories. Individual enactments are loaded using PyMuPDF, which inspects document metadata and iterates through pages sequentially. Because physical scanning noise and layout defects degrade downstream OCR and NLP processing~\cite{baird1993defect,lopresti2008optical}, the ingestor applies a proponent-defined inset cropbox that trims 3\% from all four edges of each page:

        \noindent\textit{Archival Inset Perimeter CropBox Trimming:}
        \begin{equation}
            \text{CropBox} = \left[ 0.03 \cdot W, \; 0.03 \cdot H, \; 0.97 \cdot W, \; 0.97 \cdot H \right]
            \label{eq:cropbox_trim}
            \eqcaption{Archival Inset Perimeter CropBox Trimming}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $W$ and $H$ represent the physical page width and height in points. Equation~\ref{eq:cropbox_trim} defines the boundary for trimming 3\% ($0.03$) from each edge of a scanned page. This cropping step removes common scanner artifacts (such as dark borders and binder punch holes) found in older paper records. Removing these border marks before sending the image to the vision-language model prevents visual noise from being mistaken for punctuation or causing repetitive token errors. Keeping the bounding box focused on the legislative text supports cleaner transcription.

        Following perimeter trimming, the rasterization module renders each page at an image resolution of 105 dots per inch (DPI). Selecting 105 DPI provides a practical balance between character legibility and sequence length for high-density legal documents. The rendered image is converted to an RGB array and passed to the visual processor. The processor rescales page patches dynamically, clamping total image pixels between a minimum of $200{,}704$ pixels ($256 \times 28 \times 28$) and a maximum of $351{,}232$ pixels ($448 \times 28 \times 28$). Because the Qwen2.5-VL architecture allocates visual tokens in $28 \times 28$ patch increments using two-dimensional rotary position embeddings (2D-RoPE), bounding the pixel budget stabilizes sequence length and prevents out-of-memory GPU exceptions during long batch runs.

        To accommodate local workstation hardware constraints without sacrificing recognition fidelity, the model weights are quantized using 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) precision via the BitsAndBytes library~\cite{dettmers2024qlora}. The quantization configuration uses double quantization and computes attention projections in 16-bit floating point (\texttt{torch.float16}) using PyTorch scaled dot-product attention (\texttt{sdpa}). Under 4-bit NF4 compression, the model footprint occupies less than 3.5~GB of operational VRAM and executes comfortably on consumer laptop graphics hardware. Text generation is guided by an explicit verbatim system prompt instructing the model to transcribe all section titles, ordinals, dates, names, and monetary values without summarization or commentary. Generation settings enforce deterministic greedy decoding ($\text{do\_sample} = \text{False}$, $\text{use\_cache} = \text{True}$) bounded by a ceiling of 1,000 new tokens per page.

        The pipeline adopts a page-level processing architecture rather than ingesting entire multi-page documents in a single model forward pass. Ingesting full multi-page ordinances simultaneously would require concatenating thousands of visual patch tokens, which dilutes spatial self-attention and exceeds consumer GPU memory ceilings. In contrast, processing documents page by page enforces focused visual attention on discrete text blocks while scaling linearly ($O(N)$) across documents spanning single-page resolutions through 50-page tax codes. The pipeline inserts explicit page delineators ($\texttt{--- [Page } X\texttt{] ---}$) into the extracted text to maintain traceability to physical council records. Furthermore, page-level execution supports fault-tolerant checkpointing: the system inspects output files on disk and automatically resumes execution across interrupted batch queues by skipping previously completed files. Page-level garbage collection (\texttt{gc.collect()}) and explicit CUDA cache clearing (\texttt{torch.cuda.empty\_cache()}) are executed after each document to prevent memory fragmentation during multi-hour ingestion runs.

        \subsubsection{Substantive Legislative Body Extraction}
        \label{sec:local_substantive_extraction}
        Scanned local enactments generally contain three distinct parts: opening attendance rosters, the substantive legislative provisions, and concluding signature blocks. Separating the actual legal text from administrative details is essential for accurate conflict detection. Leaving councillor names in the text introduces personal names that distort Stage~1 dense retrieval embeddings, while including signature blocks adds unnecessary clutter to candidate hypotheses in Stage~2. Splitting documents using fixed page numbers does not work reliably across local archives, because short resolutions place legal provisions on the first page, whereas long regulatory codes spread attendance lists and committee rosters across several front pages.

        To handle these structural variations, the proponents developed an adaptive boundary state machine that extracts operative legal provisions based on standard statutory markers. The extraction module scans transcripts for recognized opening markers, including enactment designations (\texttt{ORDINANCE NO.}), formal titles (\texttt{AN ORDINANCE}), and preamble clauses (\texttt{WHEREAS}). Upon detecting an opening boundary, the state machine captures the enactment identifier and title along with the preamble and all operative sections (\texttt{SECTION 1}, \texttt{ARTICLE I}, \texttt{CHAPTER I}). Text capture stops immediately upon encountering formal closing markers, such as Sanggunian enactment notices (\texttt{ENACTED}), secretarial certifications (\texttt{CERTIFIED CORRECT}), vice-mayoral attestations (\texttt{ATTESTED}), or executive approval notations (\texttt{APPROVED}).

        The extraction module incorporates a fallback hierarchy to prevent data loss when archival scans have faded or missing boundary markers. If an enactment header is obscured, the extractor falls back to the long ordinance title; if the title is absent, it anchors on the initial operative section. Similarly, if formal certification headers are missing, the system terminates extraction following the standard effectivity section (\texttt{SECTION X. EFFECTIVITY}). To provide automated quality control over the extraction process, the state machine calculates an empirical boundary verification confidence score:

        \noindent\textit{Substantive Body Boundary Extraction Confidence Score:}
        \begin{equation}
            \text{Confidence}_{\text{extract}} = 0.35 \cdot C_{\text{start}} + 0.35 \cdot C_{\text{end}} + 0.30 \cdot C_{\text{sec}}
            \label{eq:extraction_confidence}
            \eqcaption{Substantive Body Boundary Extraction Confidence Score}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $C_{\text{start}}$ and $C_{\text{end}}$ denote boundary match confidences, and $C_{\text{sec}}$ measures section sequential coherence. Equation~\ref{eq:extraction_confidence} defines the confidence score $\text{Confidence}_{\text{extract}}$ used to check how cleanly the legislative text was extracted. The linear weighting coefficients ($0.35, 0.35, 0.30$) represent an empirical heuristic formulated by the proponents during preliminary calibration on local council documents: assigning equal dominant weights of $0.35$ to the opening boundary ($C_{\text{start}}$) and closing signature boundary ($C_{\text{end}}$) ensures that confirming both structural boundaries satisfies the acceptance threshold ($0.35 + 0.35 = 0.70$) even if historical scans contain minor section numbering anomalies ($C_{\text{sec}}$), while penalizing documents with truncated bodies or missing enactment markers. Documents scoring at or above $0.70$ move on to indexing, whereas lower-scoring files are flagged for manual review while preserving the full unpruned transcript on disk. Listing~\ref{lst:substantive_extraction} outlines the extraction logic.

        \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Adaptive State Machine for Substantive Legislative Body Extraction}, label={lst:substantive_extraction}]
def extract_substantive_body(raw_transcript: str) -> dict:
    """
    Extracts operative statutory provisions from local transcripts,
    stripping administrative attendance rosters and terminal signatures.
    """
    start_match = RE_ORD_NUM.search(raw_transcript) or RE_TITLE.search(raw_transcript)
    start_idx = start_match.start() if start_match else 0
    start_conf = 1.0 if start_match else 0.40

    search_region = raw_transcript[start_idx:]
    term_match = RE_TERMINAL.search(search_region)
    end_idx = start_idx + term_match.start() if term_match else len(raw_transcript)
    end_conf = 1.0 if term_match else 0.50

    substantive_text = raw_transcript[start_idx:end_idx].strip()
    sections = [int(n) for n in RE_ALL_SECTIONS.findall(substantive_text)]
    sec_conf = 1.0 if len(sections) >= 3 and sections[:3] == [1, 2, 3] else 0.40

    confidence = round(0.35 * start_conf + 0.35 * end_conf + 0.30 * sec_conf, 3)
    return {
        "status": "SUCCESS" if confidence >= 0.70 else "FLAGGED_LOW_CONFIDENCE",
        "confidence": confidence,
        "substantive_text": substantive_text
    }
        \end{lstlisting}

This algorithm parses raw local transcripts to extract substantive enactments while stripping opening attendance rosters and terminal signature blocks. In the function, regular expressions scan the text for opening enactment indicators and terminal attestation keywords, slicing the transcript between these indices and validating ordinal section continuity. The function computes the composite confidence score according to Equation~\ref{eq:extraction_confidence} and returns a status dictionary indicating whether the extraction succeeded or requires manual review. Isolating the substantive body ensures that extraneous councillor names and mayoral titles do not distort dense retrieval vector embeddings or trigger spurious preemption matches in Stage~2.

        \subsubsection{Automated Quality Audit and Conservative Normalization}
        \label{sec:local_quality_audit}
        Following raw transcription, the system executes an automated quality audit to detect and categorize transcription anomalies across seven mutually exclusive categories: definite OCR corruption, possible corruption, formatting noise, valid legal boilerplate, valid table structures, page metadata anomalies, and items requiring human review. The audit scanner identifies severe failure states, such as Unicode replacement characters (\texttt{\textbackslash ufffd}), foreign script hallucinations (CJK and Cyrillic character ranges), raw decoding markers (\texttt{undefined}), and autoregressive repetition loops. Short transcripts under 150 characters are also flagged because authentic local ordinances rarely contain so little text even after header removal. Documents exhibiting severe corruptions or insufficient length are quarantined into a dedicated review queue for manual verification before downstream indexing.

        To minimize false positives, the audit pattern matching explicitly accommodates authentic legislative structures. A broad repeated-character regular expression frequently flags Markdown table borders (\texttt{|---|---|}) as OCR corruption. To prevent this error, table boundary delimiters and header markers are explicitly excluded from symbol repetition rules. Valid legislative boilerplate notations such as \texttt{"xxx xxx xxx"}, which denote unamended text in amending ordinances, are protected using negative lookahead expressions. Geographic survey coordinates containing degree symbols ($^\circ$), minute marks ($'$), and second marks ($''$), as well as accented Filipino surnames (\textit{Pe\~na}, \textit{Dañguilan}), are validated against an extended Unicode whitelist. Furthermore, page sequences beginning at Page~2 due to excluded cover sheets are recognized as valid contiguous sequences rather than misordered files.

        Deterministic text normalization is performed via a conservative cleaning pipeline that strictly preserves legal meaning. The cleanup module applies Unicode Form KC normalization, strips invisible zero-width characters, removes stray VLM prompt echoes (such as \texttt{**[body text]:**} / \textit{zh\`engw\'en} markers), and collapses runaway character loops exceeding seven consecutive repetitions to a standardized three-character representation. All modifications are logged in an audit report with line-level traceability. Under strict architectural safeguards, automated scripts are prohibited from altering personal names, calendar dates, numerical monetary amounts, ordinance numbers, statutory citations, penalty clauses, or section numbering. Discrepancies involving these sensitive entities are routed exclusively to human legal researchers.

        Downstream from automated cleanup, the architecture incorporates an advisory language quality review module combining a curated Philippine legal and toponymic gazetteer, contextual language model perplexity scoring, and named-entity recognition. The gazetteer whitelists all 182 official barangays of Davao City, administrative districts, and local executive departments, along with statutory Latin maxims (\textit{in pari materia}, \textit{mutatis mutandis}, \textit{ultra vires}), so specialized terminology does not trigger false spelling alarms. Contextual perplexity scoring evaluates token predictability within a sliding context window; tokens exhibiting high pseudo-perplexity and characteristic alphanumeric substitution patterns (such as misreading numeral \texttt{0} as uppercase letter \texttt{O} in monetary figures) are assigned high diagnostic confidence. The system presents these candidate errors on an interactive review dashboard, coupling the transcribed text with coordinate bounding boxes linking back to the original physical PDF scan. Human annotators can then review highlighted anomalies prior to final vector indexing.

    \subsection{Ex-Ante Draft Ingestion and Statutory Exception Harmonization}
    \label{sec:draft_ingestion_segmentation}

    While historical national and local archives undergo batch offline normalization, newly drafted measures submitted for ex-ante review pass through a specialized live ingestion pipeline. Because proposed council drafts are born digital, the live workflow skips optical character recognition and segments text directly at formal section boundaries. The pipeline then filters non-operative boilerplate before passing substantive clauses into Stage~1 candidate retrieval. Finally, the system handles statutory exception clauses through full-section context windows and harmonic retrieval so valid local carve-outs do not trigger false preemption alarms.

        \subsubsection{Digital-Born Draft Ingestion and Boundary Parsing}
        \label{sec:draft_ingestion_pipeline}
        A critical operational distinction in this research separates the historical training archive from live ex-ante draft submissions. While the historical Davao City collection consists of scanned physical PDFs requiring optical character recognition and text normalization, newly drafted ordinances submitted for ex-ante review are digital-born documents. Legislative staff create these proposed measures using standard word processing software to generate clean electronic text or searchable PDF files. Because digital-born drafts contain authentic character encodings without scanning artifacts, the live pipeline bypasses OCR error correction entirely. This structural decoupling allows the preprocessor to focus immediately on automated section boundary parsing and clause segmentation.

        To evaluate legislative commands without semantic distortion, an automated regular expression parser splits the submitted draft at formal section boundaries into discrete operative blocks. In Philippine local legislative practice, proposed measures follow standardized structural templates established under city council rules and Republic Act No.~7160~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}. Real-world legislative drafts contain substantial procedural boilerplate that does not express substantive legal commands. If routine clauses such as title declarations and effectivity dates (or separability and repealing clauses) are fed into Stage~1 retrieval, they match thousands of administrative national statutes containing similar formulaic phrases. Listing~\ref{lst:provision_extractor} details the regular expression parser that segments live draft measures into discrete operative sections while filtering out procedural boilerplate.

        \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Code Snippet of the Operative Provision Extractor and Boilerplate Filter}, label={lst:provision_extractor}]
import re

def extract_operative_provisions(draft_text):
    """
    Parses digital-born draft ordinance into discrete sections,
    filtering out non-operative procedural boilerplate clauses.
    """
    # Regex pattern to segment text at formal section boundaries
    pattern = r'(SECTION\s+\d+\.?[^\n]*\n.*?)(?=(?:SECTION\s+\d+|CERTIFIED|ENACTED|\Z))'
    matches = re.findall(pattern, draft_text, re.DOTALL | re.IGNORECASE)
    
    # Procedural clauses that do not carry substantive regulatory commands
    boilerplate_keywords = ["TITLE", "SEPARABILITY", "REPEALING", "EFFECTIVITY"]
    operative_sections = []
    
    for match in matches:
        clean_chunk = ' '.join(match.strip().split())
        header = clean_chunk.split('.')[0] if '.' in clean_chunk else clean_chunk[:30]
        
        # Exclude non-operative procedural boilerplate
        if any(kw in header.upper() for kw in boilerplate_keywords):
            continue
            
        operative_sections.append({
            "section_header": header,
            "operative_text": clean_chunk
        })
        
    return operative_sections
        \end{lstlisting}

        Listing~\ref{lst:provision_extractor} presents the Python implementation of the operative provision extractor, featuring regular expression lookahead patterns and a procedural boilerplate keyword filter. This algorithm automates the structural segmentation of raw draft ordinances into clean, self-contained section blocks while discarding administrative clauses. As observed in the listing, the regular expression isolates text between formal section boundaries, while the keyword filter checks section headers against procedural markers such as Title and Separability clauses alongside Repealing and Effectivity clauses. Discarding these formulaic clauses prevents downstream retrieval models from matching generic legislative boilerplate against thousands of unrelated administrative acts. Consequently, only substantive regulatory and penal provisions advance to the candidate indexing and inference pipeline.

        \subsubsection{Harmonic Interpretation and Exception Handling}
        \label{sec:exception_handling_sub}
        A key challenge in conflict detection is distinguishing genuine normative contradictions from authorized statutory exceptions. In statutory interpretation, harmonic interpretation is the legal doctrine that provisions of law must be construed together to give effect to both general prohibitions and qualifying provisos so that apparent contradictions do not arise when an authorized exception applies~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. In legislative drafting, an exception clause is a statutory condition that permits an otherwise restricted activity under specified conditions or for designated public entities. If an automated conflict detection system evaluates a draft local ordinance solely against a general statutory prohibition without processing the accompanying exception clause, it generates a false positive preemption alert. In regulatory enactments, general prohibitions and permitted exceptions frequently appear within the same section using conditional syntax. Because statutory sections average between 90 and 122 tokens, an entire section fits comfortably within the 512-token input limit of candidate Cross-Encoders. The model's bidirectional attention mechanism reads the general prohibition and its qualifying exceptions simultaneously to prevent false positive contradiction alerts. This joint attention helps recognize local measures that implement authorized statutory exceptions as valid exercises of delegated authority.

        When a general prohibition and an authorizing exception reside in separate statutory sections, the pipeline relies on two-stage candidate retrieval. Under Philippine statutory construction, related provisions governing the same subject matter must be interpreted together as a harmonious whole under the doctrine of \textit{in pari materia}~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. If an ordinance draft relies on an authorized statutory exemption, evaluating a specific clause without retrieving the parent exception can trigger an incorrect conflict warning. The system addresses this challenge by using hybrid retrieval in Stage~1 to pull both general bans and specific exception clauses into the candidate shortlist. The Stage~2 Cross-Encoder then evaluates the draft against the authorizing exception clause and assigns an Entailment classification to confirm that the local measure operates within valid legislative authority.

    \section{Ground Truth Benchmark}
    \label{sec:ground_truth_construction}
    Evaluating candidate Cross-Encoder models in Stage~2 requires an expert-verified ground truth dataset reflecting the authentic legal realities of Philippine local legislation. Unlike general-domain natural language inference benchmarks that rely on crowdsourced sentence pairs, evaluating local conflict detection requires formal statutory interpretation skills grounded in administrative law. Because no existing NLP benchmark encompasses Philippine national statutes and local ordinances, the proponents constructed a faculty adviser-reviewed 350-pair Ground Truth benchmark (comprising 198 unique propositional instances across 22 authentic national statutory premise sections from 17 Republic Acts, averaging roughly 9 balanced hypothesis variants per premise and itemized in Appendix~\ref{app:ground_truth_inventory}).

    To support both machine learning generalization and an institutional post-deployment roadmap with the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod, the 350 pairs are organized into a five-panel multi-rater layout ($5\text{ panels} \times 70\text{ pairs} = 350\text{ pairs}$, with 152 overlapping pairs). This design provides a zero-leakage premise-grouped suite for Cross-Encoder evaluation while preparing a three-rater replication layout ($k = 3$) for 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers without inducing reviewer fatigue. Reading multi-sentence statutory provisions requires sustained legal concentration compared with short social-media texts. Dividing the $350 \times 3 = 1{,}050$ total scheduled legal judgments across 15 reviewers assigns 70 pairs per person, which is roughly half the 144--145 short-text items per annotator used in the batched crowd-review design of Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing}.

    \subsection{Sample Size and Statistical Power}
    \label{sec:sample_size_power}
    The determination of the benchmark sample size is grounded in prospective statistical power and sample size design for multi-class contingency evaluations~\cite{cohen1988statistical, faul2007gpower}. Under Cohen's power framework, detecting semantic conflicts across a three-class discrete decision space ($\mathcal{C} = \{\text{Entailment}, \text{Neutral}, \text{Contradiction}\}$) with a medium effect size ($w = 0.30$) at $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80\% power ($(1 - \beta) = 0.80$) requires a minimum of $N = 108$ items for goodness-of-fit ($\text{df} = (3-1) = 2$) and $N = 133$ items for $3 \times 3$ contingency matrices ($\text{df} = (3-1)(3-1) = 4$). Evaluating the pipeline on the benchmark's 198 unique propositional pairs exceeds both thresholds and yields nominal power exceeding 0.90 ($1 - \beta > 0.90$, specifically 97.2\% for $\text{df}=2$ and 94.1\% for $\text{df}=4$ at $w=0.30$) with a Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) of $w \le 0.25$. This sample size therefore provides sufficient power to detect non-trivial distributional shifts across the eight Macro Legal Domains without relying on post-hoc observed power estimates. Those eight domains reflect the core public services and regulatory police powers defined in Section~17 and Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160.

    This curated scale directly reflects established standards in specialized legal NLP benchmarking. While general-domain benchmarks like SNLI~\cite{bowman2015snli} rely on open crowdsourcing across tens of thousands of colloquial sentences, domain-specific legal NLI requires accredited legal professionals whose time and cognitive availability are severely constrained. High-quality, expert-adjudicated legal datasets consistently operate within this targeted scale: the SARA statutory tax benchmark comprises 276 entailment cases (176 training and 100 test samples)~\cite{holzenberger2020a}, recent COLIEE Task~4 legal entailment test suites provide roughly 80 test items per competition cycle~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, alshehri2026cross}, and ContractNLI~\cite{koreeda2021contract} relies on curated subsets across 607 contracts. Designed across a 350-pair evaluation suite (comprising 198 unique proposition instances) under a three-rater overlap structuring 1,050 scheduled independent legal judgments across the 15-researcher cohort, the evaluation framework establishes an empirical, statistically sound benchmark for local legislative review.

    \subsection{Dataset Partitioning and Stratified Generalization Setup}
    \label{sec:dataset_partitioning}
    To prevent data leakage and support reproducible model evaluation, the 350 Ground Truth instances are partitioned using a static 70/15/15 train, validation, and test split, stratified across both target classes (Contradiction, Entailment, and Neutral) and cognitive difficulty tiers (Tier~1 Quantitative, Tier~2 Structural, and Tier~3 Latent). This split provides 245 training pairs ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$), 52 validation pairs ($N_{\text{val}} = 52$), and 53 test pairs ($N_{\text{test}} = 53$). Operating within this sample size places the evaluation in a few-shot learning regime, which is well-suited for pretrained transformer language models~\cite{gao2021making}. In machine learning, few-shot task adaptation uses the foundational linguistic and semantic representations already encoded within deep pretrained encoders, fine-tuning classification heads and attention parameters on domain-specific instances without requiring thousands of newly labeled examples~\cite{gao2021making}.

    The proponents selected a 70/15/15 partition over a standard 80/10/10 split due to sample size constraints. In a 350-pair dataset (comprising 198 unique instances), an 80/10/10 allocation yields only 35 validation and 35 test pairs. A validation set of only 35 pairs is statistically fragile for fine-grained decision threshold calibration ($\tau^*$), where a single classification error alters observed accuracy by nearly 3\%. Furthermore, under the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) power analysis framework of Card et al.~\cite{card2020with}, smaller evaluation splits increase the minimum effect size needed to reach 80\% statistical power; increasing the validation and test allocations from 35 items to 52 and 53 pairs improves power for detecting large domain adaptation gains and stabilizes threshold calibration while retaining 70\% (245 pairs) for few-shot Cross-Encoder fine-tuning~\cite{vabalas2019machine}. Because the 198 unique propositional pairs are derived from 22 distinct statutory premise sections across 17 national statutes (averaging approximately 9 hypothesis variants per premise section), standard unconstrained cross-validation risks statutory premise memorization: a neural encoder could memorize the specific statutory vocabulary or syntactic structure of a national premise during training and exploit that familiarity to classify test hypotheses linked to the same premise. To eliminate this shortcut, this static split is paired with a strict 5-fold premise-grouped cross-validation regime (\texttt{GroupKFold}) in Section~\ref{sec:premise_grouped_cv}, where all hypothesis variants sharing a given national statutory premise are quarantined into either the training partition or the evaluation partition, forcing candidate cross-encoders to demonstrate inductive reasoning on unseen statutory enactments.

    \subsection{Asymmetric Pairing and Hypothesis Formulation}
    \label{sec:span_pairing}
    To construct a representative ground truth benchmark, candidate statutory premises must reflect the full breadth of Philippine national law. During the exploratory phase of this research, the proponents conducted preliminary machine learning experiments on the national statutes, uncovering severe class imbalances and repetitive drafting formulas across historical eras. These exploratory findings directly informed the consolidated topic modeling architecture detailed in Section~\ref{sec:domain_categorization}, which organizes the corpus into eight validated Macro Legal Domains such as local governance and public health. The proponents then sampled national statutory sections across these eight domains to establish the fixed premises for the evaluation benchmark.

    In natural language inference for legal documents, Asymmetric Span-Level Pairing is a sequence formulation strategy where a single, self-contained premise section from a superior statute is paired with a multi-sentence span ($N$ sentences) representing an entire operational section from a draft ordinance~\cite{yin2021docnli, koreeda2021contract}. This asymmetric formulation reflects the structural reality of Philippine legislative drafting. National statutes typically establish general normative principles or statutory prohibitions in one or two concise sentences. In contrast, local ordinances are operational administrative instruments: a single section regularly bundles the primary regulatory command together with local office designations and penalty schedules across three to six contiguous sentences. If an NLP pipeline splits an ordinance section into isolated single-sentence fragments, the classifier loses the qualifying conditions and penalty ceilings and can produce erroneous conflict determinations. Presenting the Cross-Encoder with the full multi-sentence ordinance section paired against the statutory premise supplies complete textual context for evaluating substantive preemption.

    To make the evaluation as realistic as possible, the drafted test sentences closely mirror the writing style of Davao City ordinances. The proponents analyzed local laws enacted between 2021 and 2025 to identify standard drafting phrases and administrative office names established under Philippine local legislative practice~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}. Each drafted clause uses standard enacting syntax, such as: ``\textit{SECTION X. [TITLE]. --- It shall be unlawful for any person, natural or juridical, within the territorial jurisdiction of Davao City, to...}'' The draft sentences also name real local enforcement bodies, such as the City Transport and Traffic Management Office (CTTMO), the City Health Office (CHO), the City Environment and Natural Resources Office (CENRO), and the Business Bureau of Davao City. Operational units such as the Anti-Vices Task Force (AVTF) and the Davao City Police Office (DCPO) are also included to reflect genuine local enforcement.

    The synthetic ordinance clauses are also linked to real administrative districts in Davao City. Draft rules specify geographic areas from urban centers like Poblacion and Talomo to suburban districts like Buhangin and Toril. Local offices such as the City Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Office (CDRRMO) and the City Social Welfare and Development Office (CSWDO) are included to match actual city administration. This realistic drafting prevents evaluators from reviewing artificial example sentences that differ from real legal texts. As a result, domain evaluators and legal researchers in the post-study evaluation cohort review draft provisions that reflect authentic measures referred to committee following First Reading and prepared for Second Reading plenary interpellation.

    To assist in formulating the draft ordinance clauses into authentic city legislative syntax without introducing uncontrolled generative distortions, the proponents used a large language model (\texttt{Gemini Flash 3.8}) strictly as an assistive drafting tool for syntactic rephrasing and templating. Operating under structured few-shot prompts grounded in the analyzed 2021--2025 Davao City ordinances and local legislative drafting conventions, the model rephrased human-specified regulatory commands and local agency designations into formal enacting clauses. Under this controlled human-in-the-loop framework, all statutory premises and ground truth labels were deterministically established by the proponents and validated against black-letter Philippine statutes (such as the penalty ceilings of Republic Act No.~7160 and price freeze limits of Republic Act No.~7581) and faculty adviser review. This structured assistance avoids the unconstrained generation pitfalls identified by Gururangan et al.~\cite{gururangan2018annotation} and Poliak et al.~\cite{poliak2018hypothesis}, where unchecked automated generation introduces superficial ``annotation artifacts'' (such as predictive negation tokens) that permit models to shortcut genuine statutory reasoning. While using an assistive LLM accelerates the formulation of realistic administrative phrasing, LLM-generated text carries known risks of stylistic uniformity and subtle lexical artifacts that deep classifiers could inadvertently exploit~\cite{gururangan2018annotation, poliak2018hypothesis}. To mitigate these risks, all candidate drafts underwent mandatory human post-editing and adversarial injection by the proponents: sentences were manually perturbed to invert deontic modalities (e.g., altering mandatory commands to permissive choices without modifying surrounding vocabulary), authentic local administrative designations were injected, and penalty numbers were varied across statutory boundaries. Furthermore, as an empirical safeguard against model reliance on LLM hypothesis artifacts, the proponents conduct a hypothesis-only baseline evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_only_baseline}, verifying that candidate cross-encoders cannot achieve competitive conflict detection without cross-attending to the authentic statutory premise. In specialized legal NLP benchmarks such as SARA (tax law)~\cite{holzenberger2020a}, DocNLI~\cite{yin2021docnli}, and Clash-of-Leges~\cite{italiani2026clash}, maintaining this rigorous separation between assistive syntactic phrasing and deterministic statutory ground truth ensures that models are evaluated on authentic legislative language while preserving the legal integrity of the gold labels.

    While the system uses multi-sentence spans to understand local context, it does not attempt cross-document multi-hop reasoning (detecting conflicts that appear only by combining two or more separate national laws). Resolving dependencies across multiple separate laws is an active research challenge in natural language inference. Current systems that attempt this require either complex knowledge graphs~\cite{lu2023multi} or very large generative language models~\cite{shen2025hopweaver}. Running those multi-premise models would exceed the consumer-grade hardware limits set for local government deployment. For this reason, the ground truth benchmark and the inference system focus strictly on single-premise evaluations.

    \subsection{Class Balancing and Adversarial Injection}
    \label{sec:class_balancing}
    In machine learning benchmark construction, class balancing refers to the deliberate equalization of sample counts across all target categories to prevent models from learning trivial majority-class decision rules~\cite{chawla2002smote}. In authentic local ordinance drafting, the vast majority of local provisions comply fully with national law, meaning true statutory contradictions represent rare edge cases (typically comprising under 1\% of random pairs). If an evaluation benchmark mirrored this natural skew (e.g., 90\% Entailment and 10\% Contradiction), a naive classifier that simply predicts the majority class would achieve a deceptively high 90\% nominal accuracy while remaining completely ineffective at identifying genuine statutory conflicts. To complement class balancing, adversarial injection is a dataset construction methodology where domain experts systematically formulate deceptive, hard-to-detect variations of test pairs to eliminate reliance on superficial annotation artifacts~\cite{gururangan2018annotation, poliak2018hypothesis}. In legal natural language processing, deep language models frequently exploit shallow lexical heuristics (such as assuming that any provision containing words like ``fine,'' ``prison,'' or ``unlawful'' constitutes a contradiction). Through adversarial injection, annotators formulate pairs that invert legal duties (modifying mandatory commands to permissive options) or introduce subtle penalty ceiling violations without altering surface vocabulary. This design forces candidate Cross-Encoders to demonstrate logical inference across statutory premises rather than relying on shallow lexical shortcuts.

    In standard machine learning on tabular data, researchers often handle uneven classes using methods like the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)~\cite{chawla2002smote}. However, the proponents avoid continuous vector oversampling for this legal text pipeline. SMOTE creates artificial examples by interpolating between numerical feature vectors ($x_{\text{new}} = x_i + \lambda(x_{zi} - x_i)$). In high-dimensional language models representing discrete words, averaging or blending token vectors creates artificial representations that confuse attention layers and carry no real legal meaning. Simple word replacement also fails with legal texts because statutory words have exact legal definitions; for example, swapping mandatory words like ``shall'' with permissive words like ``may'' fundamentally changes a legal duty.

    To build a balanced, reliable Ground Truth dataset, the researchers combined historical legal records with expert-guided text adjustments. First, the researchers gathered documented legal clashes from local government records, including mayoral veto messages and repealed or amended Davao City ordinances. Second, adapting the evaluation methods of Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} and Nie et al.~\cite{nie2020anli}, the proponents, under faculty adviser supervision, introduced conflicting rules into real statutory provisions. The authors reversed permissions, changed numerical zoning limits, and added conflicting penalties into ordinance clauses. This expert-guided process produced exactly 198 unique propositional pairs with a strictly balanced distribution across classes (exactly 66 Contradictions, 66 Entailments, and 66 Neutrals). When distributed across the five multi-annotator review panels ($5 \times 70 = 350$ instances) to enable inter-rater reliability analysis, the resulting 350-instance evaluation suite comprises 112 Contradictions, 128 Entailments, and 110 Neutrals. This distribution avoids severe class imbalance while reflecting multi-panel review coverage without creating unnatural legal phrasing.

    \subsection{Stratified Difficulty Tiers}
    \label{sec:difficulty_tiers}
    Building an effective evaluation benchmark requires pairs with varying levels of difficulty. In benchmark evaluation, difficulty tier stratification is the systematic division of an evaluation dataset into distinct subsets based on the cognitive and structural complexity required to resolve each test instance~\cite{koreeda2021contract, ribeiro2020beyond}. In this benchmark, the 350 pairs are partitioned into three granular tiers. Tier~1 (Surface and Quantitative Conflicts) evaluates direct, rule-based violations, such as numerical local penalties exceeding statutory caps (e.g., a PHP~15,000 local fine where Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160 sets a PHP~5,000 ceiling for cities) or explicit inversions of mandatory terms. Tier~2 (Structural Preemption and Jurisdictional Carve-Outs) evaluates institutional authority under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine, testing whether a local council legislates within a field reserved for national agencies (such as pesticide control under Presidential Decree No.~1144) or omits mandatory statutory exemptions. Tier~3 (Latent and Paraphrastic Conflicts) evaluates subtle normative contradictions where the ordinance and national statute share virtually no overlapping lexical tokens or citations. In Tier~3, the conflict arises from contradictory legal effects (such as a local draft mandating the detention of apprehended street minors, which directly violates the non-custodial diversion mandate of Republic Act No.~9344 without explicitly citing the statute). This stratified structure provides fine-grained diagnostic visibility into candidate model performance.

    To construct a rigorous evaluation suite, the proponents adapted the difficulty tiering methodology established by Koreeda and Manning in the ContractNLI benchmark~\cite{koreeda2021contract}. This approach is supported by the behavioral stress-testing frameworks of Ribeiro et al.~\cite{ribeiro2020beyond} and Naik et al.~\cite{naik2018stress}, which separate surface competence tests from semantic challenge sets. Following the broader principle in benchmark engineering of pairing routine cases with adversarial challenge subsets---as practiced in SQuAD~2.0~\cite{rajpurkar2018know}, FEVER~\cite{thorne2018fever}, and ANLI~\cite{nie2020anli}---the proponents allocated target shares of 30\% to Tier~1, 40\% to Tier~2, and 30\% to Tier~3 so that surface rules, structural preemption, and paraphrastic conflicts each receive substantial representation without allowing models to rely solely on lexical overlap. This distribution reflects the realistic challenges faced during legislative committee review while preventing models from depending on superficial keyword tricks.

    The three difficulty tiers map directly to established Philippine statutory preemption principles and natural language challenges, following the distinction between rule-based and value-based legal conflicts described by Sartor~\cite{sartor2005legal} and Bench-Capon and Sartor~\cite{benchcapon2003theory}. Tier~1 evaluates surface and quantitative preemption, covering direct penalty ceiling violations under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160 and explicit clashes between mandatory and permissive words (\textit{shall} versus \textit{may}). Tier~2 adapts the ``negation by exception'' concept from Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} to evaluate unhonored statutory exemptions and local overreach under \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce} and \textit{City of Manila v. Laguio, Jr.}~\cite{scp2005manilavlaguio}. Tier~3 evaluates latent and paraphrastic contradictions, testing whether the model detects substantive legal friction when a draft redefines terms without sharing exact statutory words. Partitioning the benchmark across the realized 104/142/104 tier allocation ensures a balanced test set that reflects real committee work while preventing models from exploiting simple word overlap~\cite{mccoy2019right}.

    Grounding Tier~3 contradictions in legal doctrine ensures that models are evaluated on genuine language understanding rather than keyword presence. Under established Supreme Court rulings, a local council cannot enact indirect prohibitions or administrative conditions that narrow or defeat rights protected by national statutes~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce, scp2005manilavlaguio}. In Philippine statutory preemption jurisprudence, conflict encompasses both literal verbal clashes (\textit{verba legis}) and the frustration of national statutory purpose and policy (\textit{ratio legis}), as reaffirmed in \textit{Mosqueda v. Pilipino Banana Growers \& Exporters Association}~\cite{scp2016mosqueda}. In Tier~3 latent conflicts, an ordinance may not explicitly mention or verbally negate a national statute, yet its operational effect narrows, conditions, or defeats statutory guarantees. For instance, when a national statute guarantees universal, non-discriminatory access to a public service (such as tertiary tuition exemptions under Republic Act No.~10931 or juvenile diversion under Republic Act No.~9344), a local draft that imposes unauthorized local residency preconditions or custodial detentions creates an actionable normative preemption conflict under administrative law, even though the local draft frames the restriction as an administrative qualification. While reasonable legal minds may debate the line between permissible local administrative discretion and impermissible statutory narrowing, incorporating such latent policy collisions reflects authentic Philippine legal reality and tests whether cross-attention architectures capture deeper deontic tensions beyond surface token matching. To enforce this distinction during testing, the proponents verified that all 104 Tier~3 draft sentences contain zero numbers or monetary amounts. Without explicit numerical clues, the Cross-Encoder must evaluate the underlying legal meaning of the text, such as recognizing when an anti-vagrancy draft detains minors in violation of Republic Act No.~9344. This design ensures that the benchmark provides an authentic diagnostic test of semantic preemption for the subsequent analysis in Chapter~4.

    \subsection{Expert Legal Annotation Framework and Benchmark Verification}
    \label{sec:annotation_panel}
    Classifying statutory text pairs requires domain-specific legal grounding to ensure dependable, authentic ground truth labels. To establish a rigorous evaluation benchmark for model training and ablation while navigating city administrative clearance timelines, the operational benchmark dataset was systematically curated by the research team following authentic Davao City legislative syntax (2021--2025 enactments) and Philippine local drafting conventions, assisted by \texttt{Gemini Flash 3.8} for grammatical rephrasing, and formally reviewed and verified through iterative academic supervision by the thesis adviser. This adviser review was conducted to verify the technical accuracy, absence of synthetic token corruption, and logical consistency of the paired provisions against the cited national statutes and local drafting conventions, rather than functioning as a formal legal bar adjudication. This established the \textbf{Faculty Adviser-Reviewed Ground Truth Benchmark} ($N = 350$ evaluation pairs, comprising 198 unique instances across 22 statutory premises from 17 national statutes) used for all supervised cross-encoder training and decision threshold calibration, as well as zero-leakage premise-grouped cross-validation reported in this study. In parallel, to establish a gold-standard institutional validation framework, the proponents formulated a structured multi-rater evaluation design tailored for 15 active legal researchers serving the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod. Following administrative routing through the Human Resource Management Office (HRMO) and the City Administrator's Office pursuant to the Sangguniang Panlungsod referral, this expert panel framework is codified as an institutional post-deployment roadmap to capture authentic practitioner consensus once city clearance is concluded. Unlike general NLP tasks that rely on open crowdsourcing, statutory conflict detection requires specialized legal interpretation to differentiate superficial lexical overlap from genuine normative preemption~\cite{snow2008cheap, zheng2021when, chalkidis2022lexglue}.

    To establish an objective and transparent panel structure for post-deployment execution, the proponents gathered baseline qualifications during the initial intake communication. The intake request systematically recorded two key criteria from each volunteer: highest educational attainment and total years of professional experience in legislative drafting. Using these reported credentials, the 15 researchers were organized into five balanced sub-panels of three members each ($k = 3$). Within each three-member sub-panel, the evaluator possessing the highest educational attainment and the longest drafting tenure was designated as that panel's Senior Annotator. The proponents then communicated these panel assignments and Senior Annotator designations in a follow-up confirmation email to establish operational readiness for the post-study rollout.

    Following the consensus guidelines adapted from Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing}, a label is accepted into the post-deployment Ground Truth if at least two of the three reviewers in a sub-panel agree. In cases where all three reviewers select different categories, resulting in a three-way split with no majority, the pair is referred to that panel's designated Senior Annotator for binding qualitative adjudication~\cite{gao2022hierarchical, artstein2008inter}. The Senior Annotator reviews the pair against the standardized codebook to resolve whether the split arose from statutory ambiguity or an overlooked proviso. This hierarchical adjudication step ensures that every pair in the 350-pair benchmark (comprising 198 unique instances) reaches an authoritative label while preserving the initial independent ratings for calculating Fleiss' Kappa ($\kappa$). Maintaining this structured division between initial independent scoring and expert tie-breaking safeguards both statistical validity and qualitative consistency across the dataset.

    To recognize the time and professional expertise dedicated by the participating Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers, evaluators who complete their assigned annotation batches during post-deployment rollout are awarded an official Certificate of Contribution (see Appendix~\ref{app:annotator_certificate}). This certificate is a formal token of appreciation and institutional documentation acknowledging their voluntary advisory assistance and professional review of statutory conflict screening workflows during the institutional deployment phase. Each certificate is endorsed by the Computer Studies Department's Assistant Dean and the thesis adviser, together with the course instructor and student proponents, to confer formal institutional validity. This acknowledgment formalizes the collaboration between the university research team and the local legislative staff and recognizes the legal professionals supporting the study.

    In strict adherence to the ethical guidelines of the University Research Ethics Board (UREB) and the statutory mandates of Republic Act No.~10173 (the Data Privacy Act of 2012)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}, participation in the expert annotation exercise is entirely voluntary. Prior to receiving evaluation materials, each participant completes an electronic informed consent agreement affirming voluntary participation and the freedom to withdraw at any point without administrative penalty or professional consequence. To protect evaluator confidentiality, participant names and councilor or committee affiliations are permanently decoupled from annotation records and replaced with anonymized rater codes (such as Annotator P1-A through Annotator P5-C); participants only optionally disclose their legal names at the conclusion of the study if they elect to receive the formal Certificate of Contribution for their official Civil Service 201 records. Evaluated text pairs consist exclusively of enacted public statutes and model local drafting clauses derived from enacted city legislation, involving no confidential council communications, unreleased political deliberations, or proprietary personal data.

    \subsection{Annotation Workload Allocation}
    \label{sec:batched_annotation}
    To prevent reviewer fatigue while keeping the results statistically sound, this study adapts the batched review framework developed by Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing} alongside balanced incomplete block design principles for multi-rater NLP annotation~\cite{bowman2015snli, mchugh2012interrater}. In that earlier study, each item required three independent evaluations to determine a majority label. Managing a collection of 12,054 short texts required over 36,000 individual reviews. By dividing this work among 250 students, the workload was reduced to about 145 items per person. This demonstrated how batched distribution can maintain a three-vote overlap without placing an unrealistic burden on reviewers.

    This study applies the same mathematical allocation to the legal domain. Because reviewing multi-sentence legal provisions requires much more concentration than reading short messages, the dataset is smaller, but the distribution logic is identical. Dividing 1,050 total judgments across 15 legal researchers caps each reviewer's assignment at 70 pairs. The exact workload allocation for the legal reviewers is calculated in Equation~\ref{eq:workload_allocation}:

    \noindent\textit{Annotator Workload Allocation Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    N &= 350 \text{ premise-hypothesis pairs} \\
    k &= 3 \text{ independent votes per pair} \\
    P &= 15 \text{ legal annotators} \\[6pt]
    W &= N \times k = 350 \times 3 = 1,050 \text{ total annotations} \\[6pt]
    \text{Work per person} &= \frac{W}{P} = \frac{1,050}{15} = \mathbf{70 \text{ pairs per annotator}}
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:workload_allocation}
    \eqcaption{Annotator Workload Allocation Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $N$ represents the benchmark size, $k$ is the multi-rater replication factor, $P$ is the annotator pool size, and $W$ denotes total evaluations. Equation~\ref{eq:workload_allocation} details the mathematical derivation of the annotator workload distribution, dividing the total required annotations $W$ among the pool of $P = 15$ legal researchers. With a benchmark size of $N = 350$ premise-hypothesis pairs evaluated under a three-rater replication factor ($k = 3$), the workload plan requires a total of $W = 1,050$ independent legal judgments. The formulation allocates this workload into five disjoint 70-pair sets and assigns one set of 70 pairs to each three-member sub-panel. Capping individual reviewer workload at 70 pairs prevents cognitive fatigue and attention degradation during complex legal evaluation while preserving full multi-rater overlap for Fleiss' Kappa reliability calculations.

    To support reviewers across their 70-pair assignments and reduce cognitive fatigue, the proponents developed structured reference materials. Reviewing statutory pairs requires sustained effort, as reviewers must compare detailed legal clauses against local drafts. In the evaluation packet, evaluators receive spreadsheets with complete statutory text so they do not have to read isolated sub-clauses out of context. Each reviewer also receives the complete Legal Annotation Codebook (Appendix~\ref{app:annotator_guidebook}) for full procedural and doctrinal context, alongside a quick-reference one-page summary sheet outlining the \textit{Magtajas} decision hierarchy and statutory penalty ceilings. These reference materials enable reviewers to evaluate statutory pairs systematically and consistently during institutional deployment.

    \subsection{Inter-Rater Reliability}
    \label{sec:inter_rater_reliability}
    Within the institutional multi-rater annotation framework designed for Sangguniang Panlungsod panel deployment, inter-rater concordance is formally measured using Fleiss' Generalized Multi-Rater Kappa ($\kappa$)~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}. In statistical annotation methodology, Fleiss' Kappa evaluates agreement across a fixed cohort of raters assigning categorical ratings to a shared set of items. While raw percentage concordance reflects nominal agreement, it fails to account for agreement occurring purely by stochastic chance. Fleiss' Kappa calculates the true proportion of agreement achieved above chance expectation and establishes an objective statistical metric for consensus reliability. This metric is selected instead of Cohen's Kappa because Cohen's formula is limited to exactly two raters, whereas this study assigns three reviewers to each pair. The resulting score is interpreted using the guidelines from Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977measurement}, where a $\kappa$ score between 0.61 and 0.80 indicates ``Substantial Agreement.''

    Using this target in natural language inference is supported by Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli} in their baseline work on the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) benchmark. Evaluating text relationships involves human judgment; in their study, Bowman et al. reported an overall Fleiss' Kappa of 0.70 (scoring 0.77 for Contradiction, 0.72 for Entailment, and 0.60 for Neutral). They demonstrated that achieving very high agreement ($> 0.80$) in language inference is rare and usually happens only with overly simplified test sentences. Because Philippine local ordinances are more complex than general English sentences, it is unrealistic to expect agreement above 0.70. Therefore, for the post-deployment institutional study, the evaluation plan targets a minimum $\kappa$ threshold of 0.61 pursuant to Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977measurement}, which confirms substantial agreement among the legal reviewers upon full deployment.

    \section{Pipeline Architecture}
    \label{sec:pipeline_architecture}
    The live conflict detection workflow uses a two-stage retrieve-then-entail architecture designed to combine rapid statutory retrieval with precise logical preemption analysis (with core Stage~1 retrieval and Stage~2 inference modules provided in Appendix~\ref{app:code_retrieval} and Appendix~\ref{app:code_inference}). Separating coarse candidate retrieval from fine natural language inference avoids quadratic cross-attention over the entire statutory database on consumer workstations. The system divides conflict screening into two sequential operational phases: coarse candidate retrieval across the dual statutory corpus and fine natural language inference over paired provisions. Auxiliary structural safeguards, including a Statutory Knowledge Graph and a deterministic penalty ceiling verifier, support both stages without altering verbatim statutory text.

    \subsection{Stage 1 Hybrid Retrieval Pipeline}
    \label{sec:stage1_hybrid_pipeline}
    The first operational phase begins when a user submits a draft local ordinance for review. In information retrieval, hybrid search combines sparse lexical search and dense semantic vector search to capture both exact terminology and conceptual relevance. Sparse lexical search using BM25 evaluates exact token matches, including statutory citations and numerical penalty amounts, based on term frequency and document length normalization~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}. In contrast, dense semantic search using a transformer Bi-Encoder maps draft and statutory texts into a shared continuous embedding space, computing cosine similarity to identify conceptually related provisions that share few identical words~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Combining these two retrieval channels using linear score interpolation is problematic because their raw scoring distributions are fundamentally incompatible: BM25 produces unbounded positive scores that fluctuate with query length, whereas dense bi-encoders output cosine similarities strictly bounded between $-1.0$ and $+1.0$. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) is a rank-aggregation algorithm that merges multiple retrieval rankings based strictly on the ordinal positions of documents rather than their raw numerical scores~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal}. By assigning higher reciprocal rank scores to statutory provisions that appear near the top of both the lexical and semantic retrieval streams, RRF produces a balanced candidate shortlist without requiring empirical score calibration:

    \noindent\textit{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) Scoring:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{1}{k_{\text{rrf}} + \text{rank}_m(d)}
        \label{eq:reciprocal_rank_fusion}
        \eqcaption{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) Scoring}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{BM25}, \text{Dense}\}$ represents the candidate retrieval models, $\text{rank}_m(d)$ denotes the ordinal position of document $d$ produced by model $m$, and $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$ is the smoothing constant.

    Equation~\ref{eq:reciprocal_rank_fusion} defines the Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) formula, combining the ranked search results from BM25 and the dense Bi-Encoder into a single list. RRF avoids having to normalize raw scores, since BM25 scores vary widely based on document length while cosine similarities stay between $-1$ and $1$. By focusing on rank positions with a smoothing parameter ($k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$), the formula gives priority to provisions that score well in both keyword and semantic searches. This weighting builds a balanced pool of candidates for Stage~2 inference.

    Standard linear score interpolation ($\alpha S_{\text{BM25}} + (1-\alpha) S_{\text{Dense}}$) requires min-max normalization across unbounded BM25 distributions, which fluctuates across diverse queries. In contrast, Reciprocal Rank Fusion operates solely on ordinal rankings and avoids score-scale mismatches between lexical and dense retrievers. The resulting fused score ranks provisions that demonstrate both lexical precision and semantic relevance at the top of the candidate pool. Listing~\ref{lst:stage1_hybrid_retrieval} presents the procedural logic of the dual-stream hybrid retrieval and rank fusion pipeline.

    \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Pseudocode of the Stage 1 Reciprocal Rank Fusion Hybrid Provision Retrieval Pipeline}, label={lst:stage1_hybrid_retrieval}]
def retrieve_top_k_candidates(draft_section, statutory_corpus, k=50, k_rrf=60, beta=1.20):
    # 1. Retrieve top-100 candidates from sparse BM25 and dense Bi-Encoder
    bm25_results = search_bm25(corpus=statutory_corpus, query=draft_section, top_n=100)
    dense_results = search_dense(corpus=statutory_corpus, query=draft_section, top_n=100)

    # 2. Compute Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) scores across candidate pool
    candidate_pool = set(bm25_results.keys()) | set(dense_results.keys())
    fused_scores = {}
    for prov_id in candidate_pool:
        rank_bm25 = bm25_results.get_rank(prov_id, default=101)
        rank_dense = dense_results.get_rank(prov_id, default=101)
        rrf_score = (1.0 / (k_rrf + rank_bm25)) + (1.0 / (k_rrf + rank_dense))
        
        # Apply hierarchy safeguard: boost primary national Republic Acts by 20%
        if prov_id.startswith("RA_") or is_primary_act(prov_id):
            rrf_score *= beta
        fused_scores[prov_id] = rrf_score

    # 3. Sort candidates by fused score and return top-k shortlist
    return sorted(fused_scores.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:k]
    \end{lstlisting}

    Listing~\ref{lst:stage1_hybrid_retrieval} outlines the Python implementation of the Stage~1 hybrid retrieval pipeline, combining BM25 keyword search, dense Bi-Encoder retrieval, and Reciprocal Rank Fusion with hierarchy boosting. This code demonstrates how the system narrows down 164,620 national statutory provisions across 25,432 enactments to a focused shortlist of top-$k$ candidates ($k = 50$). In this routine, the draft section is searched independently across sparse and dense indices to retrieve the top 100 candidates from each method. The algorithm then iterates across all retrieved provisions, calculates the RRF score using Equation~\ref{eq:reciprocal_rank_fusion}, and applies a 20\% score boost ($\beta = 1.20$) to primary national statutes (Republic Acts) to ensure important laws are not buried under administrative issuances. Sorting the combined scores yields a compact candidate shortlist that supplies relevant legal context to Stage~2 inference.

    \subsection{Stage 1 Retrieval Safeguards}
    \label{sec:retrieval_safeguards}
    The Statutory Hierarchy Safeguard is an algorithmic re-ranking mechanism that operationalizes the constitutional doctrine of statutory supremacy established in the landmark \textit{Magtajas} doctrine~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce} and the delegated legislative limits of Republic Act No.~7160 (specifically Sections~16 and~458). In the Philippine legal hierarchy, an ordinance enacted by a local sanggunian is legally subordinate to an Act of Congress. In the raw national statutory corpus, routine executive orders and administrative issuances comprise nearly half of all enactments (48.01\%) and outnumber primary congressional legislation by thousands of documents. If a retrieval algorithm assigns equal weight across all document types, standard lexical queries can become saturated with administrative issuances and displace governing Republic Acts from the top candidate ranks. The Statutory Hierarchy Safeguard applies a calibrated score multiplier ($\beta = 1.20$) to primary national statutes during rank fusion. This weighting keeps primary national laws prominent within the candidate shortlist and operationalizes the constitutional supremacy of statutory law over delegated administrative rules.

    Using a two-stage retrieve-then-entail setup introduces potential search challenges when comparing draft ordinances against large statutory collections. If the coarse search misses the governing national law, the inference model cannot detect the conflict regardless of its analytical capability. Legal conflicts often appear through indirect wording, where local drafts use permissive phrasing or leave out specific statutory terms. Standard search tools can also miss isolated prohibitions tucked inside long, otherwise compliant legislative codes.     A central design concern across the 25,432 national statutes is avoiding premature pruning of multi-domain enactments. Foundational Philippine statutes such as Republic Act No.~7160 function as omnibus codes that span local administration and local taxation simultaneously. Hard-filtering candidates by a single predicted topic immediately excludes the governing code whenever a draft clause touches another administrative area.     Separating Stage~1 retrieval from Stage~2 inference is essential when running on standard office hardware. Full-text inputs also dilute model attention and can bury specific penalty clauses under thousands of routine procedural words. The hybrid retriever in Stage~1 performs a coarse-to-fine filter ($164{,}620 \rightarrow 50 \rightarrow 1$) that removes over 99.9\% of unrelated provisions before Stage~2 inference. This separation allows the Cross-Encoder to focus its attention on high-probability candidate provisions.

    To test these architectural mechanisms, the proponents design an ablation evaluating nine candidate Stage~1 retrieval configurations across the 164,620 provisions of the 25,432 national statutes and the 350-pair ground truth evaluation set (198 unique instances). The experiments measure candidate recall across top-$k$ cutoffs alongside ranking precision and query latency on consumer hardware. Each configuration isolates the contribution of lexical weighting and dense representations as well as hierarchy boosting. The empirical retrieval findings and difficulty tier ablations are reported in Chapter~4 (Section~\ref{sec:results_stage1_retrieval}).

    \subsection{Stage 2 Conflict Detection Pipeline}
    \label{sec:stage2_nli_pipeline}
    After receiving the top-$k$ candidate shortlist from Stage~1, the pipeline moves to logical evaluation using the Natural Language Inference (NLI) module. The system formats an input sequence for each candidate provision, pairing the retrieved national law (the premise) with the draft ordinance (the hypothesis). To guide the model's attention, the pipeline places a $[CLS]$ token at the start of the sequence and a $[SEP]$ token between the two texts. This structure helps the model distinguish the existing law from the proposed draft. The combined text is then processed by the selected Cross-Encoder model. The model's attention mechanism compares every word in the premise against every word in the hypothesis to evaluate their relationship. A classification layer outputs calibrated softmax probabilities labeling the relationship as Entailment, Contradiction, or Neutral. If the contradiction probability crosses the designated threshold, the Explainable AI (XAI) module highlights the conflicting text for user review.

    To execute full-document pre-enactment audits on resource-constrained local government workstations without prohibitive processing delays or memory exhaustion, the Stage~2 inference pipeline incorporates complementary efficiency mechanisms that reduce token overhead and sequential call latency. First, rather than routing every structural section indiscriminately to the Cross-Encoder, the pipeline filters out formulaic administrative clauses (such as Title, Definition of Terms, Separability, Repealing, and Effectivity clauses) identified via the regular expression parser in Section~\ref{sec:boilerplate_and_metadata}. As established in the local corpus census (Section~\ref{sec:local_boilerplate_filtering}), procedural clauses account for 32.09\% (4,109 of 12,803) of local ordinance sections while carrying zero substantive regulatory commands; filtering them before inference directs cross-encoder attention exclusively toward operative legal duties without discarding regulatory provisions.

    Second, standard transformer implementations enforce static padding up to the model's maximum sequence length (such as 256 or 512 tokens) and allocate fixed blocks of zero-padding memory. Because transformer self-attention exhibits quadratic complexity ($O(L^2)$) with respect to input length $L$, computing cross-attention over padding tokens expends substantial matrix multiplication operations on uninformative positions. The pipeline replaces static padding with dynamic sequence padding, clamping tensor dimensions strictly to the length of the longest clause within each mini-batch (which typically spans 60 to 90 tokens for statutory sections) to curtail quadratic attention operations relative to static maximum ceilings. Third, adapting the multi-candidate passage reranking paradigm of Nogueira and Cho~\cite{nogueira2020passage}, the pipeline aggregates candidate pairs across all substantive sections into a unified flattened mini-batch rather than processing them in iterative per-section loops. Executing this unified tensor through a single vectorized forward pass eliminates repeated call overhead and uses multi-threaded central processing unit vectorization (AVX2/AVX-512 SIMD parallelism).

    Finally, to protect against subtle numerical preemption that deep neural cross-encoders occasionally overlook on surface arithmetic, the Stage~2 pipeline integrates a deterministic Symbolic Integer Ceiling Verifier. Under Section~458(a)(1)(iii) of Republic Act No.~7160, local sanggunians are strictly prohibited from prescribing ordinance fines exceeding Five Thousand Pesos (\textpeso 5,000.00) or imprisonment exceeding one year. The symbolic verifier scans operative penal clauses using regular expression pattern matching to extract numerical fine amounts (\texttt{PHP\textbackslash s*[0-9,]+}). If an extracted integer penalty exceeds the statutory \textpeso 5,000.00 ceiling, the provision triggers an immediate deterministic Contradiction flag alongside statutory citation to Section~458. This hybrid neuro-symbolic mechanism safeguards against false negatives on quantitative limits while preserving pure neural cross-attention for complex deontic reasoning.

    \subsection{Statutory Knowledge Graph and Lineage Architecture}
    \label{sec:statutory_knowledge_graph_architecture}
    To augment the two-stage retrieve-then-entail pipeline with structural intelligence, the system incorporates a multi-relational Statutory Knowledge Graph that models institutional authority and amendatory lineages across the dual statutory corpus. Rather than operating solely as an isolated retrieval index, the knowledge graph is a shared topological backbone supporting both coarse candidate retrieval in Stage~1 and fine cross-encoder inference in Stage~2. Mapping explicit statutory cross-references and constitutional delegations alongside legislative amendments into a directed multi-graph gives the computational pipeline point-in-time statutory awareness. This structural representation also prevents obsolete or repealed laws from entering the candidate pool.

        \subsubsection{Multi-Relational Graph Formulation and Schema}
        \label{sec:graph_formulation_schema}
        Formally, the statutory knowledge base is structured as a heterogeneous, directed multi-relational graph:
        \begin{equation}
            \mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})
            \label{eq:knowledge_graph_definition}
            \eqcaption{Multi-Relational Statutory Knowledge Graph Formulation}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $\mathcal{V}$ denotes the set of heterogeneous legal vertices, $\mathcal{E} \subseteq \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{R} \times \mathcal{V}$ represents the set of directed, labeled edges $(u, r, v)$, and $\mathcal{R}$ designates the vocabulary of canonical legal relation types.

        The vertex set $\mathcal{V} = \mathcal{V}_{\text{enact}} \cup \mathcal{V}_{\text{prov}} \cup \mathcal{V}_{\text{dom}}$ comprises three distinct entity classes. Enactment nodes $u \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{enact}}$ represent the 27,248 unique legislative instruments (25,432 national acts and 1,816 deduplicated Davao City local ordinances), storing metadata attributes including enactment year, legislative era, official title, and jurisdictional tier (see Appendix~\ref{app:kg_visual_views} for complete topological and attribute views). Provision nodes $v \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{prov}}$ represent the 177,423 discrete statutory sections and ordinance articles, encapsulating the raw text, token length, operative status, and parent enactment identifier. Domain nodes $w \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{dom}}$ designate the eight macro legal domain hubs established in Section~\ref{sec:domain_categorization}.

        The relation vocabulary $\mathcal{R}$ formalizes six distinct juridical relations:
        \begin{equation}
            \begin{aligned}
                \mathcal{R} = \{ & \text{CONTAINS}, \text{GOVERNS\_DOMAIN}, \text{CITES\_AUTHORITY}, \\
                                 & \text{AMENDS}, \text{REPEALS}, \text{CONFLICTS\_WITH} \}
            \end{aligned}
            \label{eq:relation_vocabulary}
            \eqcaption{Canonical Legal Relation Vocabulary}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where structural relations $\text{CONTAINS}$ link enactments to their constituent provisions, thematic relations $\text{GOVERNS\_DOMAIN}$ map provisions to macro legal domains, vertical authority edges $\text{CITES\_AUTHORITY}$ connect local ordinances to governing national statutes, and horizontal lineage edges $\text{AMENDS}$ and $\text{REPEALS}$ capture legislative modifications under \textit{lex posterior derogat legi priori}. Semantic preemption edges $\text{CONFLICTS\_WITH}$ are dynamically instantiated when Stage~2 cross-encoder inference predicts a preemption contradiction exceeding the validation-calibrated operational threshold $\tau^*$.

        This graph architecture supports both processing stages through a complementary division of labor. During Stage~1 candidate retrieval, the retriever queries the graph to perform 1-hop and 2-hop neighborhood expansion, following $\text{CONTAINS}$ edges to pull adjacent sister provisions and $\text{AMENDS}$ edges to pull subsequent amendatory enactments while immediately pruning provisions that carry incoming $\text{REPEALS}$ edges. During Stage~2 cross-encoder inference, the pipeline traverses hierarchical paths to prepend canonical statutory lineages (\texttt{[Code > Chapter > Section]}) to the premise text. This path prefix supplies the self-attention matrix with structural authority context, while discrete attribute constraints on provision nodes (such as fine ceilings under Republic Act No.~7160 Section~458) inform symbolic ceiling verification.

        \subsubsection{Deterministic Relation Extraction and Preemption Linkage}
        \label{sec:relation_extraction_lineage}
        The automated construction of the knowledge graph from unstructured legal texts follows a sequential multi-stage extraction pipeline. In the initial phase, an entity normalization module parses surface textual references (such as ``Republic Act No. 7160,'' ``R.A. 7160,'' and ``The Local Government Code of 1991'') and maps them to unified canonical entity identifiers via regular expression aliasing dictionaries. Standardizing entity identifiers aligns multi-word references to identical graph vertices and prevents fragmentation across legislative eras. Limiting normalization to explicit aliasing patterns also keeps entity linkage transparent and auditable for legislative staff.

        Following canonical normalization, deterministic regular expression parsers scan operative statutory provisions to extract explicit cross-references and statutory delegations. The parser identifies statutory patterns referencing Acts of Congress alongside historical Commonwealth Acts and Presidential Decrees to generate directed $\text{CITES\_AUTHORITY}$ edges that establish the vertical statutory hierarchy. Concurrently, an amendatory lineage parser identifies explicit modification formulas mandated by Philippine legislative drafting manuals, recognizing phrases such as ``is hereby amended to read as follows'' and ``is hereby repealed in its entirety.'' Parsing these clauses instantiates directed $\text{AMENDS}$ and $\text{REPEALS}$ edges linking amendatory ordinances back to historical parent enactments.

        Assigning enactment timestamps to vertices enables point-in-time statutory validity tracking. When an incoming $\text{AMENDS}$ edge is detected, the graph engine assigns a modified status attribute to the predecessor provision, while an incoming $\text{REPEALS}$ edge assigns an obsolete status attribute. This state tracking ensures that the automated audit evaluates draft legislation against the live, operative state of positive law rather than superseded historical provisions. When optical character recognition noise generates truncated numerical citations on pre-LGC typewriter archives, the pipeline applies fuzzy string matching against the verified Lawphil enactment gazette or routes unresolved citations into diagnostic quarantine pools to avoid creating false edges.

        \subsubsection{Topological Graph Experiments and Diagnostic Validation}
        \label{sec:graph_experiments_validation}
        To assess the structural integrity and network properties of the constructed Statutory Knowledge Graph, the proponents formulate five standalone graph experiments executed directly on the network topology using NetworkX. These experiments verify whether automated regex extraction recovers genuine institutional hierarchies before the graph is integrated into live screening. Each diagnostic targets a distinct network property, spanning authority concentration, scale-free degree fit, thematic clustering, global connectivity, and acyclic amendatory lineage. Together, these checks establish whether the extracted graph accurately reflects positive Philippine law.

        The first experiment evaluates statutory authority through In-Degree Centrality and PageRank. Normalized In-Degree Centrality measures the direct citation volume directed toward each national statute:
        \begin{equation}
            C_{\text{in}}(v) = \frac{|\{u \in \mathcal{V} \mid (u, \text{CITES\_AUTHORITY}, v) \in \mathcal{E}\}|}{|\mathcal{V}| - 1}
            \label{eq:in_degree_centrality}
            \eqcaption{Normalized In-Degree Citation Centrality Formulation}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $C_{\text{in}}(v)$ quantifies the proportion of legal enactments citing statute $v$. Complementing direct citation counts, statutory PageRank models the recursive propagation of legal authority across multi-tiered legislative delegations:
        \begin{equation}
            PR(u) = \frac{1 - d}{|\mathcal{V}|} + d \sum_{v \in \mathcal{N}_{\text{in}}(u)} \frac{PR(v)}{|\mathcal{N}_{\text{out}}(v)|}
            \label{eq:statutory_pagerank}
            \eqcaption{Statutory Authority PageRank Formulation}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $\mathcal{N}_{\text{in}}(u)$ denotes the set of vertices citing statute $u$, $\mathcal{N}_{\text{out}}(v)$ denotes the outgoing citations of vertex $v$, and $d = 0.85$ represents the canonical damping factor. Computing these centrality metrics is an empirical sanity check that verifies whether foundational organic statutes (specifically Republic Act No.~7160) emerge as dominant structural hubs governing local law.

        The second experiment evaluates network degree distribution to determine whether statutory citations follow a natural scale-free topology. By fitting degree distributions to a power-law formulation ($P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$), this test checks whether positive law exhibits Pareto dynamics in which a compact nucleus of primary codes concentrates most citations while specialized enactments sit in the long tail. The third experiment applies unsupervised community detection using the Louvain modularity optimization algorithm. Partitioning the citation graph into topological communities evaluates whether legal enactments cluster into substantive regulatory subjects through citation density alone and measures alignment with the eight supervised macro legal domains.

        The fourth experiment conducts a global connectivity audit to quantify network integration and identify disconnected legislation. It measures the proportion of local ordinances within the Giant Connected Component (GCC) anchored to national law while isolating orphan vertices with zero in-degree and zero out-degree to separate independent ceremonial ordinances (such as street renamings) from optical scanning artifacts. The fifth experiment validates legislative lineage consistency through Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) cycle detection and amendatory depth profiling. Because legislative modifications must follow a chronological progression, simple cycle detection across all $\text{AMENDS}$ subgraphs confirms the absence of recursive amendatory loops. Concurrently, depth profiling isolates the longest amendatory chains in local legislative history, measuring cumulative generational revisions on complex instruments such as the Davao City Revenue Code.

        \subsubsection{Knowledge Graph Integration Ablation}
        \label{sec:kg_integration_ablation_methodology}
        To resolve whether knowledge graph topology should inform the cross-encoder's input linguistic representation or its post-softmax decision boundary, the proponents formulate a 4-way ablation framework evaluated on the authentic Tier~3 landmark jurisprudential suite. Injecting metadata prefix tokens directly into the transformer prompt could introduce attention distribution shift between contrasting jurisdictional labels, whereas dynamic thresholding leaves verbatim statutory text untouched. Comparing both mechanisms under identical candidate pairs isolates whether topological proximity is safer as a post-hoc calibration signal. This comparative design tests four discrete architectural configurations:

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item \textbf{Configuration A (Pure Neural Baseline):} Evaluates the fine-tuned candidate Stage~2 cross-encoder strictly on authentic verbatim statutory texts without metadata prefixes, using the global static decision threshold $\tau_{\text{base}}$.
            \item \textbf{Configuration B (Topologically Calibrated Dynamic Thresholding):} Retains 100\% authentic verbatim input text while modulating the operational decision boundary $\tau^*(u, v)$ as a continuous function of shortest-path graph distance $d_G(u, v)$ pursuant to Equations~\ref{eq:topological_proximity} and~\ref{eq:topological_dynamic_threshold}, lowering the screening threshold by $\Delta_{\tau} \cdot \gamma(u, v)$ for enactments sharing direct citation or domain links in $\mathcal{G}$.
            \item \textbf{Configuration C (Prompt-Conditioned Jurisdictional Metadata Conditioning):} Prepends canonical serialized knowledge graph node attributes directly to the input sequence (e.g., \texttt{[JURISDICTION: NATIONAL | DOMAIN: ...]} for premises and \texttt{[JURISDICTION: LOCAL | DOMAIN: ...]} for hypotheses), evaluated under the static threshold $\tau_{\text{base}}$.
            \item \textbf{Configuration D (Combined Model):} Evaluates the prompt-conditioned metadata input sequences under the topologically calibrated dynamic threshold $\tau^*(u, v)$.
        \end{enumerate}

        Benchmarking these four configurations isolates the empirical trade-offs between representation-level prompt conditioning and post-hoc decision boundary calibration. Specifically, this ablation tests whether cross-attention layers attend to substantive legal semantics or shift attention onto contrasting jurisdictional prefix tokens. Measuring sensitivity and specificity across authentic Supreme Court and Davao City disputes establishes whether verbatim input preservation outperforms metadata prompting. These findings identify the safest operational paradigm for local legislative screening.

    \subsection{Baseline Selection}
    \label{sec:baseline_selection}
    To evaluate whether the proposed two-stage retrieve-then-entail architecture achieves meaningful empirical advances over standard approaches, the proponents establish formal baselines across both operational stages and graph expansion grounded in legal informatics literature. For coarse statutory retrieval, the pipeline is benchmarked against pure Okapi BM25 lexical matching~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}, which is a standard baseline for statutory information retrieval in COLIEE Task~3~\cite{rabelo2022coliee}. Because legislative drafting relies heavily on explicit section citations and verbatim penalty amounts, BM25 provides a strong lexical anchor. Choosing BM25 as the Stage~1 baseline tests whether dense semantic vector representations and Reciprocal Rank Fusion genuinely improve candidate recall, especially across paraphrased or indirect preemption queries where exact keyword matching fails.

    For fine natural language inference, the pipeline establishes a dual-baseline framework combining an unadapted task baseline with a canonical supervised foundation. Following the legal textual entailment baseline established by Rabelo et al.~\cite{rabelo2022coliee} for COLIEE Task~4, an unadapted zero-shot Cross-Encoder tests whether off-the-shelf general-domain or multi-task language models can detect statutory preemption without local domain adaptation. This baseline establishes the empirical lower bound and directly quantifies the severity of affirmative bias. Complementing this, the proponents establish the standard bert-base-uncased model (110M parameters) developed by Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} as the canonical supervised baseline. Fine-tuned under identical training conditions on the curated statutory pairs, standard BERT isolates the performance difference contributed by legal domain pretraining and warm NLI initialization over traditional masked language modeling.

    For graph-augmented candidate expansion and lineage tracking, the system benchmarks the neuro-symbolic knowledge graph against the unaugmented dual-stream (BM25 + Bi-Encoder) retrieval baseline. This comparison evaluates whether traversing explicit statutory citation edges and amendatory lineages recovers missing governing authorities in multi-section legislative drafts where pure lexical and semantic search fail to capture unstated legal hierarchies. Measuring candidate recall alongside citation extraction accuracy isolates the operational value of graph neighborhood expansion. Any recovered governing provisions are then passed forward to Stage~2 inference under identical scoring rules.

    \section{Model Selection and Optimization}
    \label{sec:model_selection}
    Before deploying the two-stage pipeline, the proponents evaluate candidate neural models across both retrieval and inference stages to identify the most accurate and computationally efficient architectures. Rather than selecting arbitrary models or relying on closed external cloud APIs, candidate architectures are bounded by the computing resources of local government hosts. Specifically, models must satisfy parameter and memory ceilings that allow timely execution on standard desktop and laptop hardware. Once the selected models are calibrated, their weights and tokenizer configurations are permanently pinned for offline execution (\texttt{local\_files\_only = True}). This offline configuration ensures that the production pipeline runs within air-gapped local government IT environments without external cloud dependencies.

In this stage, models undergo supervised task adaptation on the training set ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$) and validation split ($N_{\text{val}} = 52$) across standard learning rates and batch sizes ($\eta \in \{1, 2, 3, 5\} \times 10^{-5}$, $B \in \{8, 16\}$), testing empirical loss convergence and cost-sensitive threshold calibration ($\tau^* \in [0.25, 0.50]$). Finally, in the third stage of finalist benchmarking, the top-performing models on the validation split, along with the canonical bert-base-uncased baseline, are selected as finalists for full out-of-sample evaluation on the 350-pair ground truth dataset (comprising 198 unique instances).

    Restricting the final evaluation to the top five validation models alongside the standard baseline anchor is guided by four practical methodological considerations. First, tuning hyperparameters and selecting models strictly on validation data ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$) protects the integrity of the test set to prevent holdout data snooping and keep out-of-sample generalization metrics unbiased~\cite{dwork2015reusable}. Second, restricting pairwise statistical comparisons to six finalists controls family-wise error rates, avoiding the inflated risk of false positives inherent in uncorrected testing across dozens of models~\cite{dietterich1998approximate} while enabling dependable pairwise significance testing against bert-base-uncased ($\chi^2, p < 0.05$). Third, benchmarking across model scales isolates efficiency trade-offs, determining whether incremental accuracy gains from 400M-parameter architectures justify their substantial latency and VRAM footprint on local office hardware~\cite{schwartz2020green, strubell2019energy}. Fourth, selecting top-performing candidates across distinct model families ensures the finalist cohort spans diverse inductive biases, including pre-aligned NLI reasoners, long-context encoders, unaligned general foundations, and legal-domain pretrained transformers. The empirical zero-shot screening statistics and admission decisions across all 28 candidate models are formally reported in Chapter~4 (Section~\ref{sec:results_stage2_training}).

    \subsection{Overfitting Controls and Calibration}
    \label{sec:overfitting_controls}
    Because fine-tuning deep transformer models in a few-shot setting ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$) can lead to memorizing training phrasing, the proponents use several regularization techniques. First, model optimization uses the AdamW algorithm with weight decay ($\lambda = 0.01$), as developed by Loshchilov and Hutter~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}. This weight decay keeps network parameters small and prevents individual weights from growing disproportionately large. Second, dropout layers with a rate of $p = 0.1$ are applied across attention layers and the classification head~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}. By turning off a random subset of neurons during training passes, dropout encourages the model to learn broader semantic features rather than depending on specific word patterns.

    Third, training uses early stopping based on holdout validation loss~\cite{prechelt1998early}. The system measures validation loss and the validation Macro $F_1$-score after every training epoch on the 52-pair validation set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$). If holdout validation loss does not improve over two consecutive checks ($\text{patience} = 2$), training stops automatically. The system then restores the checkpoint that achieved the lowest validation loss so that the model does not overfit the training data. Finally, the learning rate warms up linearly over the first 10\% of training steps before decreasing steadily, which helps keep gradient updates stable during the initial phase of fine-tuning.

    Recent empirical findings from the COLIEE 2026 legal textual entailment benchmarks (Task~4) demonstrated that neural language models frequently exhibit affirmative bias, defaulting toward entailment or neutral classifications when confronted with complex statutory syntax~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. In local legislative review, failing to detect an authentic conflict permits an invalid ordinance to proceed to enactment. To counter this affirmative bias, the proponents calibrate the decision threshold for the contradiction class using validation holdout curves. Rather than assuming an uncalibrated argmax decision rule, the threshold is adjusted to maximize the Contradiction F1-Score while maintaining high specificity. This calibration ensures that genuine preemption risks are surfaced to legislative staff rather than being suppressed by ambiguous phrasing.

    \section{Evaluation Metrics}
    \label{sec:evaluation_metrics}
    Evaluating the two-stage pipeline requires separate metrics for each operational phase. The coarse retrieval phase in Stage~1 prioritizes candidate recall over final classification, whereas the inference phase in Stage~2 emphasizes cost-sensitive conflict recall alongside high precision to limit false alerts. Complementing these neural metrics, dedicated graph metrics evaluate the topological validity and retrieval benefit of the Statutory Knowledge Graph. Finally, non-parametric statistical hypothesis tests assess whether performance differences across candidate models and legal domains are statistically reliable.

    \subsection{Stage 1 Retrieval Metrics}
    \label{sec:stage1_metrics}
    Measuring search performance in Stage~1 requires ranking metrics rather than standard classification accuracy~\cite{manning2008introduction}. In information retrieval, ranking metrics evaluate how effectively a search system places relevant documents at or near the top of a retrieved candidate list. In this pipeline, Stage~1 does not produce a final classification decision; its objective is to ensure that the governing national statute is successfully captured within the top-$k$ candidate shortlist ($k \in \{10, 30, 50\}$) passed to Stage~2. Recall at rank $k$ ($\text{Recall@}k$) defines the proportion of test queries for which the target statutory provision appears within the top $k$ retrieved positions. If a governing statute fails to appear within the top-$k$ cutoff, the downstream Cross-Encoder cannot evaluate the pair, which causes a false negative retrieval omission. Complementing candidate recall, Mean Reciprocal Rank ($\text{MRR@}k$) measures retrieval precision by averaging the multiplicative inverse of the rank of the first relevant document across all test queries ($1/\text{rank}$). A high Recall@50 ensures candidate safety for downstream inference, while a higher MRR indicates that relevant provisions rank near the top of the shortlist. The metric is formalized as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Recall at Rank $k$ ($\text{Recall@}k$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Recall@}k = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \mathbf{1}\left(\text{rank}(d_q^*) \le k\right)
        \label{eq:recall_at_k}
        \eqcaption{Recall at Rank $k$ ($\text{Recall@}k$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $|Q|$ is the total number of test queries, $d_q^*$ is the correct national statute paired with query $q$, and $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is an indicator function that equals one when the target law ranks within the top $k$ candidates.

    Complementing candidate recall, Mean Reciprocal Rank at rank $k$ ($\text{MRR@}k$) evaluates how early the target statute appears within the candidate ranking~\cite{manning2008introduction}:

    \noindent\textit{Mean Reciprocal Rank at Rank $k$ ($\text{MRR@}k$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MRR@}k = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{\text{rank}(d_q^*)} \cdot \mathbf{1}\left(\text{rank}(d_q^*) \le k\right)
        \label{eq:mrr_at_k}
        \eqcaption{Mean Reciprocal Rank at Rank $k$ ($\text{MRR@}k$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $d_q^*$ is the correct national statute retrieved for query $q$, with $1/\text{rank}(d_q^*)$ representing the reciprocal rank score.

    Equations~\ref{eq:recall_at_k} and~\ref{eq:mrr_at_k} define the ranking metrics used to test Stage~1 retrieval across the 25,432 national statutes. Because the goal of coarse retrieval is to create a top-$k$ shortlist ($k \in \{10, 30, 50\}$) rather than making final decisions, standard accuracy is not suitable. $\text{Recall@}k$ measures the percentage of test queries where the correct statute appears in the top-$k$ shortlist, while $\text{MRR@}k$ measures how close to the top that statute ranks. High Recall@50 ensures that Stage~2 inference receives the governing statute needed to detect a conflict, while higher MRR places the most relevant law near the top of the list for legal researchers to review. These two metrics guide the selection of the best retrieval models in Chapter~4.

    In this formula, a target statute retrieved at rank one receives a reciprocal score of 1.0, while a statute retrieved at rank ten receives 0.10. The 350 curated premise-hypothesis pairs form the query benchmark for testing Stage~1. By issuing the 350 draft clauses as queries against the 164,620 provisions of the 25,432 national statutes, the proponents measure how often candidate retrieval models locate the paired statutory provision. Calculating $\text{Recall@}10$, $\text{Recall@}30$, and $\text{Recall@}50$ verifies whether the coarse retriever supplies a dependable candidate pool for subsequent natural language inference.

    \subsection{Stage 2 Inference Metrics}
    \label{sec:stage2_metrics}
    The Stage~2 Cross-Encoder evaluates candidate statutory pairs across three discrete logical categories: $\mathcal{C} = \{\text{Entailment}, \text{Neutral}, \text{Contradiction}\}$. Following standard evaluation conventions in information extraction and natural language processing~\cite{chinchor1992muc4, vanrijsbergen1979information}, the baseline inference performance metrics compute per-class precision, recall, and harmonic $F_1$-scores. Evaluating each category individually prevents high accuracy on frequent neutral pairs from masking failures on minority contradiction cases. Macro-averaging across all three labels then summarizes balanced performance across the full NLI taxonomy as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Class-Specific Precision, Recall, and $F_1$-Score:}
    \begin{equation}
        P_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c}, \quad R_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c}, \quad F_{1,c} = \frac{2 \cdot P_c \cdot R_c}{P_c + R_c}
        \label{eq:class_metrics}
        \eqcaption{Class-Specific Precision, Recall, and $F_1$-Score}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $TP_c$, $FP_c$, and $FN_c$ represent the true positive, false positive, and false negative classifications for category $c$, respectively. Precision reflects how reliably the model avoids false alarms, while Recall measures the proportion of genuine legal relationships successfully detected.

    \noindent\textit{Macro-Averaged $F_1$-Score Across Logical Classes:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Macro } F_1 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|} \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} F_{1,c}
        \label{eq:macro_f1}
        \eqcaption{Macro-Averaged $F_1$-Score Across Logical Classes}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $|\mathcal{C}| = 3$ ensures that performance across all three categories is evaluated equally without allowing dominant neutral pairs to skew the outcome.

    Equations~\ref{eq:class_metrics} and~\ref{eq:macro_f1} define the performance metrics for Stage~2 inference across the three NLI categories: Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction ($|\mathcal{C}| = 3$). Precision ($P_c$) measures how well the model avoids false alarms, Recall ($R_c$) measures how many actual conflicts or relationships are caught, and the $F_1$-score balances both. Macro $F_1$ averages these scores equally across all three classes ($1/3$ each) so that a model cannot achieve an artificially high score simply by predicting the majority class. This provides a balanced measure for comparing candidate models during screening.

    In statutory conflict detection, classification errors carry fundamentally unequal operational costs~\cite{elkan2001foundations, fawcett2006roc}. Cost-sensitive evaluation is a machine learning methodology that incorporates asymmetric misclassification costs into performance metrics and decision threshold calibration~\cite{elkan2001foundations}. In conventional classification, false positives (Type~I errors) and false negatives (Type~II errors) are penalized equally. In local legislative review, however, their operational consequences are highly asymmetric. A false negative (failing to identify an illegal or preempted ordinance section) allows a legally defective measure to advance to enactment, risking judicial invalidation by the Supreme Court under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce} and enforcement of unlawful penalties against citizens. Conversely, a false positive (flagging a compliant provision for committee review) requires a legal researcher to inspect the side-by-side text comparison and dismiss the informational notice. To reflect this asymmetric penalty structure, the evaluation adapts the $F_2$-score, which weights recall twice as heavily as precision ($\beta = 2$) to prioritize capturing potential contradictions.

    Because a missed preemption allows a defective rule to advance while a false alarm only adds brief manual inspection, defaulting to a 50\% cutoff ($P \ge 0.50$) introduces unnecessary screening risk. Standard classifiers default to a symmetric decision threshold of $P(\text{Contradiction}) \ge 0.50$, which assumes that false alarms and missed conflicts cause identical harm. In foundational cost-sensitive learning theory, Elkan~\cite{elkan2001foundations} proved that when false negative costs exceed false positive costs ($C(FN) > C(FP)$), setting the decision boundary at 0.50 is mathematically suboptimal and increases missed detections~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. Tuning the contradiction threshold on held-out validation curves aligns classification behavior with pre-enactment legislative safety.

    To address this cost asymmetry, the proponents adapt the generalized $F_\beta$ formulation of Van Rijsbergen~\cite{vanrijsbergen1979information} and Chinchor~\cite{chinchor1992muc4} with $\beta = 2$ and calibrate a validation-tuned decision boundary $\tau_{\text{conflict}}$ for contradiction alerts. Rather than using a fixed 0.50 cutoff, the pipeline sweeps candidate thresholds across $\tau \in [0.25, 0.50]$ in increments of $\Delta \tau = 0.01$ exclusively on the validation set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$, $N = 52$) following empirical decision calibration principles~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. Restricting the sweep to $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$ prevents test-set leakage while enforcing a minimum precision floor ($P_{\text{conflict}} \ge 0.70$) to keep false alarms manageable. The cost-sensitive contradiction metrics are computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Cost-Sensitive Contradiction $F_2$-Score:}
    \begin{equation}
        F_{2, \text{conflict}} = \frac{5 \cdot P_{\text{conflict}} \cdot R_{\text{conflict}}}{4 \cdot P_{\text{conflict}} + R_{\text{conflict}}}
        \label{eq:f2_conflict}
        \eqcaption{Cost-Sensitive Contradiction $F_2$-Score}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $P_{\text{conflict}}$ and $R_{\text{conflict}}$ denote Precision and Recall on the Contradiction class, weighting recall twice as heavily as precision ($\beta = 2$) to aggressively capture potential legal conflicts.

    \noindent\textit{Optimal Contradiction Decision Threshold Calibration ($\tau^*$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \tau^* = \arg\max_{\tau \in [0.25, 0.50]} F_{2, \text{conflict}}(\tau; \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}})
        \label{eq:optimal_threshold}
        \eqcaption{Optimal Contradiction Decision Threshold Calibration ($\tau^*$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$ denotes the 52 validation pairs used to sweep candidate decision thresholds $\tau \in [0.25, 0.50]$ while enforcing an empirical precision floor of $P_{\text{conflict}} \ge 0.70$.

    Equations~\ref{eq:f2_conflict} and~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold} define the cost-sensitive evaluation metric and decision threshold calibration for conflict alerts. This design reflects a practical reality: missing a real conflict (a false negative) allows an invalid ordinance to pass, which can lead to legal challenges, whereas a false alarm (a false positive) is quickly dismissed by a legal researcher. In Equation~\ref{eq:f2_conflict}, setting $\beta = 2$ weights recall twice as heavily as precision; this calibration prioritizes catching potential conflicts. Equation~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold} searches for the best decision threshold $\tau^*$ between $0.25$ and $0.50$ on the 52 validation pairs ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$), while keeping precision at or above $0.70$. This tuning helps the system flag genuine conflicts reliably without overwhelming researchers with too many false alarms.

    To extend static threshold calibration into a neuro-symbolic decision engine, the system integrates topological distance over the Statutory Knowledge Graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$. While the validation-calibrated static threshold $\tau_{\text{base}}$ establishes a dependable global baseline, candidate statutes that share direct authority citations or substantive regulatory domains with an ordinance warrant heightened screening sensitivity. To achieve this without altering the authentic verbatim text sequence, the proponents formulate the Topologically Calibrated Dynamic Decision Threshold:

    \noindent\textit{Topological Institutional Proximity ($\gamma(u, v)$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma(u, v) = H(u, v) \cdot \frac{1}{d_G(u, v)}
        \label{eq:topological_proximity}
        \eqcaption{Topological Institutional Proximity ($\gamma(u, v)$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $H(u, v) \in \{0.5, 1.0\}$ represents the jurisdictional authority hierarchy multiplier ($H = 1.0$ for vertical statutory preemption under \textit{Magtajas v. Pryce Properties}, and $H = 0.5$ for horizontal intra-jurisdictional consistency), and $d_G(u, v)$ denotes the shortest-path geodesic distance between candidate statute $u$ and ordinance context $v$ traversing the Statutory Knowledge Graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})$. If $u$ and $v$ are disconnected in $\mathcal{G}$, $d_G(u, v) \to \infty$ and $\gamma(u, v) = 0$.

    \noindent\textit{Topologically Calibrated Dynamic Decision Threshold ($\tau^*(u, v)$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \tau^*(u, v) = \tau_{\text{base}} - \Delta_{\tau} \cdot \gamma(u, v)
        \label{eq:topological_dynamic_threshold}
        \eqcaption{Topologically Calibrated Dynamic Decision Threshold ($\tau^*(u, v)$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\tau_{\text{base}}$ is the baseline cost-sensitive decision threshold calibrated on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$ via Equation~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold}, and $\Delta_{\tau} = 0.15$ is the maximum dynamic calibration bandwidth, bounding the operational screening threshold within $\tau^*(u, v) \in [\tau_{\text{base}} - 0.15, \tau_{\text{base}}]$.

    Equations~\ref{eq:topological_proximity} and~\ref{eq:topological_dynamic_threshold} establish a continuous, mathematically bounded decision framework. When an enactment $u$ directly cites or amends $v$ ($d_G = 1$, $H = 1.0$), $\gamma = 1.0$ and the screening threshold tightens to $\tau_{\text{base}} - 0.15$, flagging potential contradictions between established statutory authorities. When $u$ and $v$ share a common macro-domain via $\text{GOVERNS\_DOMAIN}$ edges ($d_G = 2$), $\gamma = 0.5$ and the threshold shifts to $\tau_{\text{base}} - 0.075$. When enactments are distantly situated ($d_G = 3$, $\tau^* = \tau_{\text{base}} - 0.05$) or completely unlinked ($d_G \ge 4$ or $\infty$, $\tau^* = \tau_{\text{base}}$), the model retains the conservative baseline threshold $\tau_{\text{base}}$ to shield valid local police power enactments from uncalibrated false alarms. During live inference, a candidate provision triggers a formal conflict alert whenever its softmax contradiction probability satisfies $P(\text{Contradiction} \mid p_i, h) \ge \tau^*(u, v)$.

    When evaluating complete multi-section ordinances during live system execution, Stage~1 retains its full safety buffer of $k = 50$ candidates for each substantive section ($S_i$). To prevent runtime delays when scoring up to 500 total candidate pairs per document, the Stage~2 inference engine employs vectorized mini-batch collation ($B = 16$). Batching evaluates candidate provisions in parallel tensor forward passes rather than sequential single-item loops to keep execution feasible on both GPU hosts and standard LGU CPU workstations. To consolidate candidate provision scores into an overall section verdict, the pipeline applies section-level max-pooling over candidate probabilities. Section-level max-pooling is an aggregation function that maps the set of $k$ individual contradiction probabilities for a given draft section to its maximum observed value: $P(\text{Conflict} \mid S_i) = \max_{j} P(\text{Contradiction} \mid P_j, S_i)$. In statutory review, an ordinance section that complies with 49 retrieved provisions but contradicts a single governing statute remains legally invalid under statutory preemption. Computing an arithmetic mean across candidate scores would dilute a high contradiction probability (such as 0.85) down to an undetectable fraction; that dilution would trigger a critical false negative. Max-pooling isolates the peak conflict probability across all candidate pairs so that an authentic legal violation triggers a system alert regardless of the number of non-conflicting candidates retrieved. The formula is computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Section-Level Max-Pooled Conflict Probability:}
    \begin{equation}
        P(\text{Conflict} \mid S_i) = \max_{j \in [1, k]} P(\text{Contradiction} \mid P_j, S_i)
        \label{eq:section_conflict_prob}
        \eqcaption{Section-Level Max-Pooled Conflict Probability}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $P_j$ ranges over all $k = 50$ candidate statutory provisions retrieved for draft section $S_i$.

    Equation~\ref{eq:section_conflict_prob} calculates the overall conflict score for a draft section ($S_i$) by taking the highest contradiction probability among all $k = 50$ retrieved candidate laws. This approach, known as max-pooling, is used during full-document review where each draft clause is compared against multiple candidates. Taking the highest score ensures that if a draft section clearly conflicts with even one national law, an alert is triggered, even if the other 49 retrieved candidates are neutral or unrelated. This directs the reviewer's attention to the specific statute causing the conflict and prevents an actual violation from being hidden by non-conflicting candidates.

    A section triggers an operative conflict warning if and only if $P(\text{Conflict} \mid S_i) \ge \tau^*$, with the system highlighting the specific governing candidate provision $P_j^* = \arg\max_{j} P(\text{Contradiction} \mid P_j, S_i)$ that produced the highest conflict probability. This max-pooling aggregation ensures that an authentic statutory clash ranked lower in the candidate shortlist is not obscured by neutral or harmonious provisions appearing at higher retrieval ranks. Separating section-level aggregation from candidate retrieval also preserves per-pair explainability for human reviewers. Listing~\ref{lst:stage2_inference_aggregation} outlines the operational inference and conflict aggregation procedure.

    \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Pseudocode of the Stage 2 Cost-Sensitive Cross-Encoder Inference and Section-Level Conflict Aggregation}, label={lst:stage2_inference_aggregation}]
def detect_section_conflicts(draft_section, candidate_provisions, model, threshold=0.35, batch_size=16):
    """
    Stage 2 Cost-Sensitive Cross-Encoder Inference:
    Scores candidate law pairs and flags conflicts using max-pooling.
    """
    # 1. Pair each candidate statutory provision (premise) with the draft section (hypothesis)
    pairs = [(provision.text, draft_section.text) for provision in candidate_provisions]

    # 2. Run mini-batch inference through the fine-tuned Cross-Encoder
    contradiction_scores = []
    for i in range(0, len(pairs), batch_size):
        batch = pairs[i : i + batch_size]
        # Get softmax probabilities across [Entailment, Neutral, Contradiction]
        probabilities = model.predict_probabilities(batch)
        # Extract contradiction class probability (index 2)
        batch_contradictions = [p["contradiction"] for p in probabilities]
        contradiction_scores.extend(batch_contradictions)

    # 3. Find candidate provision with highest contradiction score (Max-Pooling)
    peak_score = max(contradiction_scores)
    peak_index = contradiction_scores.index(peak_score)
    governing_provision = candidate_provisions[peak_index]

    # 4. Apply cost-sensitive decision threshold
    has_conflict = peak_score >= threshold

    return {
        "alert": has_conflict,
        "conflict_probability": peak_score,
        "conflicting_law": governing_provision.citation,
        "conflicting_text": governing_provision.text
    }
    \end{lstlisting}

    Listing~\ref{lst:stage2_inference_aggregation} outlines the Python implementation of the Stage~2 Cross-Encoder inference and section-level conflict aggregation routine. This algorithm is provided to illustrate the mini-batch tensor processing and cost-sensitive thresholding that transforms candidate provision pairs into actionable preemption alerts. In the procedure, the candidate statutory provisions and draft section are paired into premise-hypothesis sequences and evaluated in vectorized mini-batches ($B = 16$) through the fine-tuned model. The function extracts softmax contradiction probabilities, identifies the peak score via max-pooling according to Equation~\ref{eq:section_conflict_prob}, and checks whether this peak crosses the calibrated decision threshold $\tau^*$. If an alert is triggered, the routine returns the conflicting statutory citation along with its verbatim text and model confidence, delivering immediate diagnostic transparency to the committee review and Second Reading preparation dashboard.

    \subsection{Statutory Knowledge Graph Evaluation Metrics}
    \label{sec:kg_evaluation_metrics}
    Evaluating the performance and utility of the Statutory Knowledge Graph requires assessing both its intrinsic topological properties and its operational impact on downstream candidate retrieval. Intrinsically, the graph must accurately represent institutional statutory hierarchies, scale-free degree distributions, and non-circular amendatory progressions. Operationally, the graph must successfully recover governing statutory provisions that drop below statistical similarity thresholds during coarse retrieval. Combining network-level diagnostics with retrieval recall metrics captures both structural soundness and downstream utility.

    Intrinsic topological evaluation begins with network degree distribution and power-law scaling analysis~\cite{barabasi1999emergence}. In legal citation networks, an authoritative statutory corpus typically exhibits scale-free behavior where a core set of primary organic codes attracts an overwhelming share of legislative citations~\cite{katz_2023_legal_nlp}. To determine whether the empirical citation network follows this scale-free power-law topology ($P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma}$), the proponents compute the coefficient of determination ($R_{\text{power}}^2$) using linear regression over log-transformed degree frequencies:

    \noindent\textit{Scale-Free Power-Law Degree Distribution Goodness of Fit ($R^2$):}
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\text{power}}^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{k} \left(\ln P(k) - (-\gamma \ln k + c)\right)^2}{\sum_{k} \left(\ln P(k) - \overline{\ln P(k)}\right)^2}
        \label{eq:power_law_r2}
        \eqcaption{Scale-Free Power-Law Degree Distribution Goodness of Fit ($R^2$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $P(k)$ represents the empirical probability density of degree $k$, $\gamma$ is the estimated power-law scaling exponent, $c$ is the intercept constant, and $\overline{\ln P(k)}$ denotes the mean log-frequency across observed degrees.

    Equation~\ref{eq:power_law_r2} calculates the goodness of fit between the observed citation degree distribution and the theoretical power-law distribution. A high coefficient of determination ($R_{\text{power}}^2 \ge 0.85$) confirms that the statutory network exhibits scale-free Pareto dynamics rather than random connectivity. This result indicates that citation extraction isolates primary organic statutes. Evaluating the fitted scaling exponent alongside $R_{\text{power}}^2$ also verifies whether authority concentration remains consistent across historical eras.

    Complementing scale-free verification, the structural cohesion of legislative subject domains is evaluated using Louvain community modularity~\cite{blondel2008fast}. Partitioning the graph into communities measures whether enactments cluster along substantive legal subjects through citation links alone:

    \noindent\textit{Louvain Community Modularity Formulation ($Q$):}
    \begin{equation}
        Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i, j} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)
        \label{eq:louvain_modularity}
        \eqcaption{Louvain Community Modularity Formulation ($Q$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $A_{ij}$ is the adjacency weight between enactments $i$ and $j$, $k_i$ and $k_j$ denote node degrees, $m = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij} A_{ij}$ is the total network edge count, and $\delta(c_i, c_j)$ indicates whether nodes $i$ and $j$ reside in identical topological communities.

    Equation~\ref{eq:louvain_modularity} quantifies the strength of division of the statutory graph into modular communities. A high modularity value ($Q > 0.40$) indicates dense intra-domain statutory citations paired with sparse inter-domain linkages. Values above this threshold indicate that positive law forms natural topical clusters corresponding to substantive administrative areas. Comparing those unsupervised clusters against the eight supervised macro domains tests whether citation structure reflects thematic governance boundaries.

    To verify legislative lineage consistency, the amendatory subgraph $\mathcal{G}_{\text{amend}} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}_{\text{AMENDS}} \cup \mathcal{E}_{\text{REPEALS}})$ is evaluated for Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) compliance. Because legislative amendments operate chronologically under \textit{lex posterior}, amendatory relationships must remain strictly acyclic. The pipeline executes simple cycle detection algorithms across all amendatory edges to confirm that the count of directed cycles satisfies $|\mathcal{C}_{\text{cycle}}| = 0$, mathematically verifying the absence of recursive amendatory loops. Enforcing acyclicity guards against erroneous regex links that could otherwise corrupt point-in-time validity lookups.

    To evaluate downstream operational utility, the proponents measure Graph-Expanded Candidate Recall ($\text{Recall@}k_{\text{graph}}$), assessing candidate recovery when combining hybrid search with 1-hop and 2-hop graph neighborhood expansion:

    \noindent\textit{Graph-Expanded Candidate Retrieval Recall ($\text{Recall@}k_{\text{graph}}$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Recall@}k_{\text{graph}} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \mathbf{1}\left(d_q^* \in \mathcal{K}_q^{(k)} \cup \mathcal{N}_1(\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}) \cup \mathcal{N}_{\text{cite}}(h_q)\right)
        \label{eq:recall_at_k_graph}
        \eqcaption{Graph-Expanded Candidate Retrieval Recall ($\text{Recall@}k_{\text{graph}}$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$ denotes the baseline top-$k$ candidate set retrieved via Stage~1 hybrid search, $\mathcal{N}_1(\cdot)$ represents 1-hop topological neighbors following structural containment and authority citation edges, and $\mathcal{N}_{\text{cite}}(h_q)$ designates statutory provisions directly referenced within query clause $h_q$.

    Equation~\ref{eq:recall_at_k_graph} evaluates whether expanding the candidate pool along statutory authority edges recovers target governing laws that dropped below the top-$k$ cutoff during isolated bi-encoder scoring. Alongside graph recall, the extraction accuracy of deterministic regex parsers across $\text{AMENDS}$ and $\text{REPEALS}$ formulas is evaluated using precision and recall summarized by the harmonic $F_1$-score ($F_{1, \text{lineage}}$) benchmarked against adviser-verified ground truth amendatory annotations.

    \subsection{Statistical Hypothesis Testing}
    \label{sec:statistical_comparisons}
    To evaluate whether performance disparities between candidate Cross-Encoder models represent statistically significant differences rather than random sampling fluctuations, the proponents employ formal non-parametric hypothesis tests recommended by Dem{\v{s}}ar~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. In machine learning evaluation, non-parametric statistical tests evaluate differences between algorithms without requiring assumptions of normality or continuous error distributions~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Parametric paired $t$-tests assume that classification errors follow a continuous Gaussian distribution, an assumption violated by discrete, binary classification outcomes (correct versus incorrect) on small evaluation sets ($N = 53$). McNemar's test is a non-parametric statistical test for paired nominal data that analyzes $2 \times 2$ contingency tables of discordant predictions~\cite{mcnemar1947note}. By isolating cases where two competing models produce divergent classifications (cases where Model~A is correct and Model~B is incorrect, and vice versa), McNemar's test evaluates whether one model's marginal superiority is statistically significant. Complementing pairwise comparisons, the Friedman test is a non-parametric two-way analysis of variance by ranks that evaluates multiple candidate models across multiple datasets or task domains~\cite{friedman1937use}. By ranking candidate models within each of the eight Macro Legal Domains and assessing the consistency of their average ranks, the Friedman test verifies whether an architecture achieves consistent performance across all statutory subject areas without assuming normal distributions.

    When comparing the classification accuracy of two competing Stage~2 models (such as a fine-tuned NLI candidate versus standard bert-base-uncased or legal-bert-base) evaluated on the identical 53-item test set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$), the proponents employ McNemar's test for pairwise significance~\cite{mcnemar1947note}. The test constructs a $2 \times 2$ contingency table analyzing discordant predictions, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_contingency}. In this contingency framework, cell $b$ represents items correctly classified by Model~A but misclassified by Model~B, while cell $c$ represents items correctly classified by Model~B but misclassified by Model~A. Under the null hypothesis $H_0: p_b = p_c$, the two models have equivalent error rates.

    Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_contingency} presents the $2 \times 2$ contingency matrix of discordant and concordant predictions used by McNemar's test for pairwise model significance testing. This matrix structure is incorporated to isolate the exact instances where two competing Cross-Encoder architectures disagree on the 53-item test split ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$). In the layout, cells $a$ and $d$ capture concordant outcomes where both models are simultaneously correct or incorrect, while off-diagonal cells $b$ and $c$ record discordant predictions where only Model~A or Model~B classifies the pair correctly. Under the null hypothesis, the two models share equivalent error rates ($p_b = p_c$). By evaluating only discordant pairs rather than raw overall accuracy, the test provides high statistical sensitivity to marginal algorithmic improvements while remaining valid under non-normal classification error distributions.

    Because the Stage~2 evaluation conducts three primary pairwise model comparisons ($m = 3$: comparing the leading validation-selected model against the unadapted Zero-Shot Baseline, standard bert-base-uncased, and domain-adapted legal-bert-base), the proponents apply the standard Bonferroni correction~\cite{demsar2006statistical}, which sets the adjusted threshold to $\alpha' = \alpha / m = 0.05 / 3 \approx 0.0167$. Furthermore, when expanding candidate model explorations to larger suites (e.g., $k \approx 10$ architectures and hyperparameter configurations), running exhaustive pairwise McNemar tests produces $\binom{k}{2} = 45$ comparisons and introduces severe statistical conservatism under Bonferroni correction ($\alpha' = 0.05 / 45 \approx 0.0011$). To handle larger candidate pools without losing statistical power, the proponents follow Dem{\v{s}}ar's two-step testing procedure~\cite{demsar2006statistical}: first executing the omnibus Friedman test across all $k$ models simultaneously, and only upon rejecting the global null hypothesis ($p < 0.05$), conducting post-hoc comparisons comparing candidate models strictly against the top-performing control model ($k - 1$ comparisons rather than $\frac{k(k-1)}{2}$) or evaluating critical difference intervals via the Nemenyi test. These complementary corrections control Type~I error inflation across both focused baseline checks and multi-model ablations.

    For pairwise statistical significance, following the recommendations of Dietterich~\cite{dietterich1998approximate} for comparing supervised machine learning algorithms, the proponents employ McNemar's chi-squared test~\cite{mcnemar1947note} with Edwards' continuity correction~\cite{edwards1948note}. The test evaluates whether discordant classifications between Model~A and Model~B occur in equal proportions:

    \noindent\textit{McNemar's Chi-Squared Test with Edwards' Continuity Correction:}
    \begin{equation}
        \chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c} \sim \chi^2_1
        \label{eq:mcnemar_test}
        \eqcaption{McNemar's Chi-Squared Test with Edwards' Continuity Correction}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $b$ represents items correctly classified by Model~A but misclassified by Model~B, $c$ represents items correctly classified by Model~B but misclassified by Model~A, and the numerator incorporates Edwards' continuity correction ($-1$) for discrete cell frequencies.

    Equation~\ref{eq:mcnemar_test} defines McNemar's chi-squared test with Edwards' continuity correction for comparing paired predictions. The formula measures whether the number of times Model~A succeeds where Model~B fails ($b$) differs significantly from the reverse case ($c$). The minus-one correction accounts for discrete count data, and dividing by total disagreements ($b+c$) produces a test statistic that follows a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom. In practice, this test checks whether one model outperforms another on difficult borderline cases rather than by chance. If the resulting $p$-value falls below the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold ($\alpha' = 0.0167$), the null hypothesis is rejected and the accuracy difference between the two models is statistically significant.

    Beyond pairwise significance, assessing the consistency of candidate architectures across the eight Macro Legal Domains without making restrictive distributional assumptions requires non-parametric multi-model ranking. For this purpose, the proponents apply the Friedman non-parametric two-way analysis of variance by ranks~\cite{friedman1937use, demsar2006statistical}. Methodologically, because the eight Macro Legal Domains represent thematic partitions of the local legal corpus rather than completely independent experimental blocks, this Friedman rank test operates as a domain-stratified consistency check to evaluate whether relative architectural rankings remain stable across diverse areas of law. For $k$ candidate models evaluated across $N = 8$ distinct legal domains, each model is assigned a rank $r_i^j$ within domain $i$, where the best-performing model receives rank 1. The average rank of model $j$ across all domains is given by $R_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^j$. The omnibus rank statistic is computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Friedman Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks ($\chi_F^2$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \chi_F^2 = \frac{12N}{k(k+1)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^k R_j^2 - \frac{k(k+1)^2}{4}\right]
        \label{eq:friedman_test}
        \eqcaption{Friedman Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks ($\chi_F^2$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $N = 8$ is the number of macro legal domains, $k$ is the number of candidate models, and $R_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^j$ is the average rank of model $j$ across all domains ($r_i^j$ being the rank of model $j$ in domain $i$).

    Equation~\ref{eq:friedman_test} computes the Friedman rank test statistic ($\chi_F^2$) to compare candidate models across multiple legal domains without assuming normal distributions. The formula uses the average ranks ($R_j$) of each model across the eight macro legal domains, comparing observed rank differences against what would be expected if all models performed equally. Under the null hypothesis that all models have similar performance across domains, $\chi_F^2$ follows a chi-squared distribution with $k - 1$ degrees of freedom. If the test rejects this null hypothesis ($p < 0.05$), it indicates meaningful performance differences across legal domains, allowing post-hoc tests such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Nemenyi comparisons to identify which models generalize best across different areas of law~\cite{demsar2006statistical}.

    \section{System-Level Evaluation}
    \label{sec:system_evaluation_framework}
    While isolated benchmarks measure retrieval recall and natural language inference accuracy separately, evaluating the live utility of the system requires testing both stages as an integrated pipeline. In real legislative drafting, an inference engine cannot detect a contradiction if the retrieval engine fails to supply the governing statute, and a retrieval engine is useless if the inference engine misclassifies the pair. Following the multi-stage system evaluation standards established in legal information extraction competitions~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, alshehri2026cross} and fact verification benchmarks~\cite{thorne2018fever}, the proponents implement a structured Three-Tier Evaluation Framework. This multi-tier framework connects controlled statistical benchmarking with full-document stress testing. Together, these three evaluation tiers systematically assess whether the complete pipeline can operate reliably within the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod.

    The first evaluation tier measures the combined algorithmic performance of Stage~1 retrieval and Stage~2 inference when executed sequentially without human intervention. Adapting the joint scoring formulation established in the FEVER fact verification framework by Thorne et al.~\cite{thorne2018fever}, the cascaded benchmark feeds only the local draft hypothesis clause $h_q$ from the 53 held-out test pairs into Stage~1 coarse retrieval. The retrieval engine searches across all 27,248 national statutes and local ordinances to extract a candidate shortlist of the top-$k$ provisions ($\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$), which is then passed directly to Stage~2 for inference. A test item is scored as a Cascaded True Positive if and only if Stage~1 retrieves the correct governing statute $p_q^*$ within the top-$k$ shortlist and Stage~2 correctly classifies the pair $(p_q^*, h_q)$ as a Contradiction:

    \noindent\textit{End-to-End Pipeline Hit Indicator (FEVER Metric):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Hit}_{\text{casc}}(q) = \mathbf{1}\left(p_q^* \in \mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}\left(\hat{y}(p_q^*, h_q) = y_q^*\right)
        \label{eq:cascaded_hit}
        \eqcaption{End-to-End Pipeline Hit Indicator (FEVER Metric)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $p_q^*$ is the target governing statute, $\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$ is the candidate shortlist of top-$k$ provisions retrieved in Stage~1, and $\hat{y}(p_q^*, h_q) = y_q^*$ indicates whether Stage~2 correctly classifies the candidate pair as a contradiction.

    Equation~\ref{eq:cascaded_hit} defines the joint pipeline hit metric ($\text{Hit}_{\text{casc}}$) based on the end-to-end evaluation approach of Thorne et al.~\cite{thorne2018fever}. This metric counts a test item as a success only when both stages work correctly in sequence. Specifically, the formula multiplies two check conditions: whether Stage~1 retrieval includes the true governing statute ($p_q^*$) in its top-$k$ shortlist, and whether Stage~2 inference predicts the correct gold label ($y_q^*$). Combining retrieval recall and inference accuracy this way prevents a high score in one stage from hiding failures in the other. In real legislative workflows, successfully catching a conflict requires both finding the relevant statute and correctly identifying the contradiction.

    Adapting this joint metric from the FEVER fact verification framework~\cite{thorne2018fever} separates retrieval omissions ($p_q^* \notin \mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$) from classification errors ($\hat{y} \neq y_q^*$). This separation ensures that neither pipeline stage masks failures in the other during end-to-end testing. To evaluate the contribution of the Statutory Knowledge Graph within this cascaded setup, Tier~1 benchmarks the pipeline across two candidate pool formulations: unaugmented hybrid search ($\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$) and graph-expanded search ($\mathcal{K}_{q, \text{graph}}^{(k)} = \mathcal{K}_q^{(k)} \cup \mathcal{N}_1(\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}) \cup \mathcal{N}_{\text{cite}}(h_q)$). Comparing end-to-end pipeline hits ($\text{Hit}_{\text{casc}}$) under both configurations tests whether relational citation and amendatory traversal recover governing statutory candidates that fall below statistical similarity cutoffs.

    The second evaluation tier assesses how the complete coarse-to-fine pipeline processes full, multi-section legislative drafts rather than isolated sentence pairs. Adapting the capability-matrix and equivalence-partitioning methodology of Ribeiro et al.~\cite{ribeiro2020beyond} alongside the document-level legal NLI frameworks of Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} and Yin et al.~\cite{yin2021docnli}, the proponents apply stratified purposive sampling across the functional legislative typologies uncovered by the unsupervised topic modeling pipeline, constructing exactly one representative full-length draft ordinance for each discovered local legislative category ($N = 6$ documents). To evaluate resilience against document-length variance and distracting administrative context as formulated by Naik et al.~\cite{naik2018stress}, the six drafts are intentionally varied across the empirical length quantiles of the local archive, spanning concise six-section road-closure and disaster-appropriation measures up to comprehensive sixteen-section regulatory codes. Each draft replicates the exact typographical layout of Sangguniang Panlungsod enactments without artificial running headers or footers, and incorporates category-specific legal instruments required under local legislative practice~\cite{philgov1991ra7160, spdc2022ra00122}, including full multi-article Memoranda of Agreement, Deeds of Usufruct, Corporate Secretary's Certificates, notarial acknowledgments, and tabular appropriation or Transfer Certificate of Title schedules. Within each document, most sections remain compliant with superior law while targeted operative provisions contain deliberate statutory violations. The preprocessor can then chunk and audit every section independently against the statutory archive.

    Evaluating full documents measures both the sensitivity of the pipeline to authentic legal defects and its ability to avoid false alarms on compliant text. Unlike isolated sentence pairs, complete ordinance drafts interleave substantive prohibitions with routine administrative clauses and attached legal schedules. Measuring detection on full drafts therefore requires tracking both conflict recall and false-alarm resistance across every extracted section. Following standard diagnostic classification metrics~\cite{altman1994diagnostic, fawcett2006roc}, the proponents calculate Section-Level Sensitivity to measure the percentage of injected conflicts successfully flagged, paired with Section-Level Specificity to assess false-alarm resistance:

    \noindent\textit{Section-Level Specificity for Full-Document Stress Testing:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Specificity}_{\text{sec}} = \frac{TN_{\text{sec}}}{TN_{\text{sec}} + FP_{\text{sec}}}
        \label{eq:section_specificity}
        \eqcaption{Section-Level Specificity for Full-Document Stress Testing}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $TN_{\text{sec}}$ denotes compliant draft sections correctly passing without triggering a false alarm, and $FP_{\text{sec}}$ denotes compliant draft sections erroneously flagged as contradictory.

    Equation~\ref{eq:section_specificity} defines Section-Level Specificity ($\text{Specificity}_{\text{sec}}$) for the Tier~2 full-document test. This metric measures how well the pipeline avoids false alarms when checking long drafts that include standard administrative sections and boilerplate clauses. The formula calculates the percentage of compliant sections ($TN_{\text{sec}}$) that correctly pass without triggering an alert out of all non-conflicting sections. In local legislative work, frequent false warnings on routine clauses lead to alert fatigue; as a result, researchers begin to doubt the tool. Maintaining high specificity ensures that conflict alerts remain credible and that researchers can focus their attention on genuine legal risks.

    This perturbation stress test assesses whether the system can analyze complete legislative documents before deployment in committee reviews. To determine whether graph-guided candidate expansion resolves retrieval omissions on unmentioned statutory preemption authorities, the Tier~2 full-document evaluation incorporates a dedicated ablation study comparing two operational modes: an unaugmented baseline pipeline relying strictly on raw text similarity, and a graph-augmented neuro-symbolic pipeline that uses explicit citations in the draft ordinance to traverse the Statutory Knowledge Graph. In the unaugmented baseline, when an ex-ante draft provision introduces an unauthorized fine or regulatory mandate without explicitly citing the governing national act, standard hybrid search may fail to retrieve the unmentioned national statute into its top-$k$ candidate shortlist; that omission leads to undetected preemption defects. Under the graph-augmented pipeline, explicit statutory references located anywhere within the legislative draft trigger 1-hop and 2-hop traversals across the knowledge graph, pulling parent statutory frameworks and amendatory lineages into the candidate pool for all draft sections. This experimental ablation directly tests whether graph expansion moves preemption detection toward the oracle candidate ceiling while sustaining high Section-Level Specificity ($\text{Specificity}_{\text{sec}} \ge 85\%$) across compliant administrative clauses.

    The third evaluation tier validates the practical utility and legal soundness of the system using documented historical legal disputes and active expert review. Rather than relying solely on synthetic tests, the proponents systematically assembled an authentic judicial validation suite of eight landmark legal controversies and local enactments originating strictly within Davao City. These historical cases encompass both judicial invalidations where local enactments were struck down for exceeding statutory boundaries, and landmark rulings where the Supreme Court affirmed the validity of Davao City ordinances under local police power. By subjecting the pipeline to actual local disputes that reached the judiciary, this tier evaluates whether algorithmic contradiction detection mirrors authentic judicial reasoning. The resulting test suite spans multiple substantive categories, including agrochemical oversight, structural zoning, revenue powers, and public health mandates.

    The judicial cases examine distinct facets of city governance. The first group covers environmental regulation and structural zoning. In \textit{Mosqueda v. Pilipino Banana Growers \& Exporters Association, Inc.}~\cite{scp2016mosqueda}, the Supreme Court invalidated Davao City Ordinance No.~0309-07 (imposing a total ban on aerial pesticide spraying in agricultural banana plantations) for violating equal protection and invading the exclusive regulatory jurisdiction vested in the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority under Presidential Decree No.~1144. Conversely, in the Davao City Watershed Protection dispute under Ordinance No.~0310-07~\cite{spdavao2007ord0310}, the city's total prohibition on mineral extraction operates in direct vertical tension with State mineral concessions under Republic Act No.~7942. For urban structural governance, the Supreme Court in \textit{Hon. Leoncio Evasco, Jr. v. Alex P. Monta{\~n}ez}~\cite{scp2018evascovmontanez} reversed the Court of Appeals and explicitly upheld Davao City Ordinance No.~092-2000 regulating outdoor billboards. The Court affirmed that under Section~458(a)(4)(iv) of Republic Act No.~7160, city councils possess express statutory police power to regulate advertising structures harmoniously alongside the National Building Code (Presidential Decree No.~1096).

    The remaining landmark disputes focus on local revenue authority, public health, and delegated traffic administration. In \textit{City of Davao and Tanjili v. ARC Investors, Inc.}~\cite{scp2022davaovarc}, the Supreme Court voided Davao City's local business tax assessment under Ordinance No.~158-05, ruling that an LGU cannot unilaterally reclassify a passive holding company as a non-bank financial intermediary without Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas authorization. Similarly, in \textit{City of Davao v. First Meridian Development, Inc.}~\cite{scp2022firstmeridian} and \textit{Rock Steel Resources, Inc. v. City of Davao}~\cite{ctarocksteel139}, local business tax assessments under Ordinance No.~158-05 were challenged, with the Court of Tax Appeals expressly invalidating Section~423's mandatory payment-under-protest condition as contrary to Section~195 of Republic Act No.~7160. By contrast, the Court in \textit{City of Davao v. Court of Appeals and GSIS}~\cite{scp2005davaovgsis} affirmed city taxing authority over government corporate assets after Republic Act No.~7160 withdrew prior statutory exemptions. In delegated public health and traffic safety, Davao City enacted landmark measures setting stricter local standards, such as the Comprehensive Anti-Smoking Ordinance (Ordinance No.~0367-12)~\cite{spdavao2012ord0367} and the Comprehensive Speed Limit Ordinance (Ordinance No.~0270-23)~\cite{spdavao2023ord0270} pursuant to Republic Act No.~4136 and Joint JAO 2018-01.

    \section{Prototype and User Interface}
    \label{sec:prototype_development}
    While the Tier~3 jurisprudential suite benchmarks model decisions against historical judicial holdings, verifying whether the system functions as an interactive advisory tool requires packaging the pipeline for active legislative practitioners. To support this workflow, the completed pipeline is deployed as an accessible, single-page web application designed for standard office desktop computers in local government units (with architectural templates and low-fidelity interface wireframes detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:prototype_dashboard} and Appendix~\ref{app:ui_wireframes}). The interface uses a clean, intuitive layout that mirrors familiar document conversion tools. Users begin by uploading digital draft ordinances in common text or PDF formats through a drag-and-drop file ingestion area. After submission, an active progress indicator keeps the user informed while the backend pipeline parses operative sections, runs Stage~1 hybrid retrieval across the 27,248-enactment statutory knowledge base, and executes Stage~2 Cross-Encoder inference. This streamlined layout avoids complex parameter settings or technical menus so that legal staff can run pre-enactment conflict checks quickly during active committee sessions.

    When the analysis finishes, the interface presents a structured diagnostic dashboard organized to support rapid human verification. The top of the screen displays an executive summary showing the total number of audited sections, the count of flagged potential conflicts, and the overall statutory compliance status of the draft. Below the summary, an interactive table lists each audited draft section alongside its corresponding Stage~1 candidate provisions. Clicking any flagged item expands a dedicated review card that presents the local ordinance section and the retrieved governing statute in a direct side-by-side comparative reading view. Placing the two texts side by side allows legal researchers to inspect the alleged contradiction immediately without switching between multiple browser tabs or searching through printed statute digests. Reducing tab switching lowers extraneous cognitive load during legislative review~\cite{sweller1988cognitive}.

    To make the system's reasoning transparent and easy to interpret, the interface incorporates an Explainable AI (XAI) module focused directly on reading clarity and practical decision support~\cite{doshi2017towards, rudin2019stop}. Rather than displaying abstract activation matrices or relying on static keyword dictionaries, the module derives token-level attribution directly from the internal cross-attention tensors of the selected fine-tuned Stage~2 Cross-Encoder during evaluation. For each candidate pair formatted as $[\text{[CLS]}, P_1, \dots, P_m, \text{[SEP]}, H_1, \dots, H_n, \text{[SEP]}]$, the system extracts the attention vector of the classification token $\text{[CLS]}$ across all sequence positions from the final transformer layer ($l$), averaged across all $H$ attention heads: $\mathbf{a}_{\text{CLS}} = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \mathbf{A}_{l, h}[0, :]$. Using the fast tokenizer's character offset mapping, subword attention weights are mapped back to exact character spans in the original texts without altering or summarizing the authentic statutory wording. Saliency weights are min-max normalized as $\tilde{w}_i = \frac{w_i - \min(\mathbf{w})}{\max(\mathbf{w}) - \min(\mathbf{w}) + \epsilon}$, mapping non-trivial attention weights ($\tilde{w}_i \ge 0.15$) onto a continuous red color gradient ($\alpha \in [0.08, 0.70]$) rendered directly over the text. This intrinsic attribution naturally concentrates attention mass upon discordant deontic operators, distinguishing strict prohibitions (such as \textit{prohibited}, \textit{unlawful}, or \textit{shall not}) from statutory permissions (such as \textit{authorized}, \textit{allowed}, or \textit{may}).

    To support actionable decision-making and prevent cognitive fatigue during prolonged legislative sessions, each review card incorporates an interactive Explainable Attention Heatmap toggle switch alongside a visual gradient legend bar ($0.0 \rightarrow 1.0$). Legislative officers conducting rapid initial review can leave the heatmap active to isolate contested modal operators in seconds, or toggle the highlight layer off to inspect the clean, unpolluted legal prose in its natural state. Directly above the comparative text columns, each card incorporates a calibrated Confidence Rate bar with a dynamic decision threshold marker ($\tau^*(u, v)$); this indicator shifts along the horizontal track according to the calibrated baseline cutoff ($\tau_{\text{base}}$) and topological relaxation ($\Delta_{\tau} \cdot \gamma(u, v)$) defined in Equations~\ref{eq:topological_proximity} and~\ref{eq:topological_dynamic_threshold}. Direct statutory citations ($d_G = 1$) and shared regulatory domain links ($d_G = 2$) lower the marker relative to unlinked baseline provisions ($d_G \ge 4$) so that users see how relational graph proximity adjusts each screening decision. Furthermore, the dashboard organizes detected inconsistencies using a two-tiered alert hierarchy. Critical Vertical Preemption Alerts are displayed with red warning banners, signaling that a proposed local provision directly contradicts a superior national statute under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine and risks being declared \textit{ultra vires}. Informational Horizontal Notices are shown with blue informational banners, notifying the researcher that the draft overlaps with an existing Davao City ordinance and may cause unintended duplication or implied repeal. Accompanying each alert is a concise, plain-language \textit{ratio decidendi} summary explaining the legal basis of the warning (for example, stating that a local penalty of ten thousand pesos exceeds the five-thousand-peso statutory maximum authorized under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160). Legal researchers can review these highlights and explanations to decide whether the draft should be cleared, amended, or referred for committee debate; this step maintains human oversight throughout the local legislative process.

    \section{End-User Usability Evaluation}
    \label{sec:user_evaluation_study}
    To evaluate how the automated conflict detection system operates within actual local legislative workflows once institutional clearance is concluded, the study formulates an end-user evaluation design grounded in descriptive survey research. While computational benchmarks quantify retrieval recall and pairwise natural language inference accuracy, verifying whether the system is a viable decision-support assistant requires assessing its practical utility, task-technology fit, and software usability when operated by local legislative staff. The evaluation study is designed as a descriptive, cross-sectional assessment where participants test the prototype on realistic local ordinance drafts and record their ratings through a structured quantitative instrument. Collecting structured ratings from practitioner users complements automated accuracy scores with direct feedback on interface clarity and workflow alignment.

    The target evaluation cohort comprises the 15 active legal researchers serving the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod. These professional evaluators represent the city's three legislative council districts and routinely perform preliminary statutory compliance audits and draft ordinance preparation under Republic Act No.~7160~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. In local legislative administration, legal researchers examine proposed drafts before committee presentation, checking that draft clauses do not exceed devolved local powers or conflict with national legislation. Because these participants perform legislative auditing as their regular professional responsibility, their planned quantitative ratings will provide an authentic assessment of whether the software aligns with the cognitive and administrative demands of city governance.

    The planned testing procedure follows a structured, hands-on simulation replicating the post-First Reading committee review phase leading into Second Reading plenary deliberations. Each testing session is structured across three standardized steps. First, evaluators attend a brief ten-minute orientation introducing the prototype's assistive advisory role, input format requirements, and the distinction between vertical preemption alerts and horizontal consistency notices. Second, researchers interact directly with the single-page prototype dashboard on standard local government desktop computers matching the Host~A specification. Evaluators upload multi-section draft ordinances containing both fully compliant regulatory clauses and injected preemption edge cases under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160. Researchers inspect the retrieved Stage~1 statutory provisions, examine the side-by-side comparative views, evaluate the highlighted deontic command words, and review the plain-language ratio decidendi summaries. Third, upon completing the hands-on review, researchers complete a standardized quantitative survey hosted on Google Forms to record their evaluations.

    To ensure methodological rigor while keeping the survey concise and free of cognitive fatigue during demanding council schedules, the quantitative instrument integrates two validated psychometric frameworks. First, overall software usability and learnability are measured using Brooke's 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS)~\cite{brooke1996sus}. The SUS instrument is widely recognized across human-computer interaction and legal informatics research for its high reliability and quick completion time. The 10 items use a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 for strongly disagree to 5 for strongly agree) and alternate between positive and negative statements to control for acquiescence bias. Second, task-specific utility and workflow alignment are measured using a targeted 4-item questionnaire adapted from Davis' Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)~\cite{davis1989perceived} and Goodhue and Thompson's Task-Technology Fit (TTF) framework~\cite{goodhue1995task}. These four items evaluate essential dimensions of legislative auditing: search efficiency, preemption risk detection, diagnostic clarity of highlighted phrases, and routine committee adoption viability. An optional open-ended question is included at the end of the survey to capture qualitative feedback on specific statutory edge cases and recommendations for institutional deployment. Providing this explicit questionnaire mapping anchors every survey metric in established research literature and supports structured descriptive analysis of the prototype's real-world viability.

    Following descriptive survey research principles, the statistical analysis focuses on interpretable measures of central tendency, variability, and composite usability scoring. For each survey item, participant responses on the 5-point Likert scale are summarized using the Mean ($\mu$), Standard Deviation ($\sigma$), and Median. Reporting both parametric and non-parametric descriptive statistics summarizes the response distribution across all evaluation dimensions without imposing normality assumptions on ordinal survey data. To summarize overall software usability across the ten SUS items, the raw ratings are also converted into a standardized composite score from 0 to 100:

    \noindent\textit{System Usability Scale Composite Score:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{SUS}} = 2.5 \times \left[ \sum_{i \in \text{odd}} (x_i - 1) + \sum_{i \in \text{even}} (5 - x_i) \right]
        \label{eq:sus_score}
        \eqcaption{System Usability Scale Composite Score}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $x_i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ denotes the raw 5-point Likert response for survey item $i$, with odd items ($i \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9\}$) representing positively phrased statements and even items ($i \in \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$) representing negatively phrased statements.

    Equation~\ref{eq:sus_score} defines the canonical formula for calculating the composite System Usability Scale score ($S_{\text{SUS}}$) established by Brooke~\cite{brooke1996sus}. The System Usability Scale is a standardized psychometric questionnaire that quantifies user perceptions of software usability on a composite 0-to-100 scale. The instrument alternates between positively phrased statements (odd items) and negatively phrased statements (even items) to control for response bias so that respondents read and evaluate each statement rather than agreeing indiscriminately. The scoring formula normalizes the raw 5-point Likert ratings: subtracting 1 from the raw ratings of positive odd items and subtracting the raw ratings of negative even items from 5 converts all responses to a standardized 0-to-4 scale. Multiplying the sum of these converted scores by 2.5 produces the final composite index ($0 \le S_{\text{SUS}} \le 100$). The resulting score is benchmarked against the empirical evaluation frameworks established by Bangor et al.~\cite{bangor2008empirical, bangor2009determining} and Sauro and Lewis~\cite{sauro2012quantifying}. Under Sauro and Lewis's curved grading scale, a composite score of 68 represents the average benchmark (centered within Grade C), while scores of 70 and above enter Bangor et al.'s acceptable usability tier. Furthermore, scores of 80.8 or above reach the 90th percentile or higher (Grade A or A+ under Sauro and Lewis), which approaches Bangor et al.'s empirical mean for excellent usability ($M = 85.5$).

    To verify the internal consistency reliability of the 4-item Technology Acceptance and Task-Technology Fit scale prior to reporting aggregate findings, the evaluation plan specifies computing Cronbach's alpha ($\alpha$)~\cite{cronbach1951coefficient}. Cronbach's alpha is a psychometric reliability coefficient that measures the internal consistency of a multi-item survey scale, determining whether individual survey items reliably capture the same underlying psychological construct. A score of $\alpha \ge 0.70$ is adopted as the standard benchmark for adequate internal consistency in research instruments~\cite{taber2018use}, indicating that responses reflect a coherent evaluation of legislative workflow utility rather than unrelated perceptual variance. Furthermore, to evaluate consistency in legal determinations across researchers during the interactive committee review simulation, inter-rater consensus is measured using Fleiss' Kappa ($\kappa$)~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}. Computing Fleiss' Kappa verifies that user verdicts on test drafts reflect shared legal standards rather than subjective individual impressions.

    The evaluation procedure strictly adheres to the ethical and data privacy mandates of Republic Act No.~10173 (the Data Privacy Act of 2012)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. Participation in the evaluation study is entirely voluntary, and all participants complete an electronic informed consent form prior to survey submission. To protect evaluator confidentiality, participant names, specific committee assignments, and councilor affiliations are permanently decoupled from response records and indexed exclusively through anonymized identification tokens (such as Evaluator A-01 through A-15). Survey submissions are stored on secure university research storage and analyzed solely in aggregate descriptive form for academic thesis reporting.

The official repository is publicly accessible on GitHub:

    To support computational transparency and auditability, the repository implements structured reproducibility safeguards across four operational dimensions (with accompanying reproducible execution notebooks cataloged in Appendix~\ref{app:notebooks}, and the complete authorship and AI usage disclosure documented in Appendix~\ref{app:authorship_ai_disclosure}). First, the project enforces permanent commit hash versioning, wherein all empirical tables and model checkpoints reported in this thesis are permanently tagged with their respective Git commit hashes (such as Commit cc1c33d for statutory corpus ingestion and Commit 4dd7c85 for legal instruments) to ensure version traceability. Second, training and evaluation scripts maintain deterministic execution control by pinning global random seeds ($\text{seed} = 42$) across PyTorch, NumPy, and Python runtime environments to eliminate stochastic variation. This script loads the hybrid BM25 and semantic vector index over statutory provisions, executes asymmetric NLI scoring across four curated local test cases, and outputs explainable ratio decidendi under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine on standard CPU hardware. Fourth, the architecture enforces config-driven parameter decoupling, isolating experimental hyperparameters (including learning rates, batch sizes, sequence lengths, and decision thresholds $\tau^*$) from core execution scripts into modular configuration files. Separating configuration from code lets researchers evaluate candidate models and recalibrate operational thresholds without modifying the core inference scripts.

    \section{Methodological Constraints}
    \label{sec:methodological_constraints}
    The first constraint involves the language of the legislative documents. Following Executive Order No.~292 (the Administrative Code of 1987) and national publishing standards, the statutory archive and local ordinance records examined in this study are limited to official English-language enactments. Because of this, the automated pipeline does not process vernacular Cebuano-Visayan phrases, colloquial regional idioms, or mixed Taglish expressions that occasionally arise during informal committee deliberations or floor debates. While operative legal provisions in the Philippines are formally enacted in English, statutory questions that depend on localized dialectal nuances require manual review by legal researchers. This constraint establishes a clear boundary for the tokenizer and avoids confusion when evaluating formal statutory commands against informal draft notes.

    The second constraint involves the age and physical condition of the local ordinance archive. The historical enactment records obtained from the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod display uneven chronological density, where ordinances passed between 2021 and 2025 benefit from clean digital records within the LISSP. In contrast, older local enactments dating from 1945 to 2020 exist primarily as physical carbon-copy scans, marked by faded ink, page skew, and photocopy marks. Although the vision-language ingestion pipeline may substantially reduce transcription errors compared to traditional optical character recognition, residual character errors on pre-1980 archival enactments inevitably affect extraction fidelity. The retrospective evaluation of local ordinances prior to the modern digital era is therefore focused on landmark enactments that have undergone manual verification alongside high-resolution scanning.

    The third constraint stems from the single-premise asymmetric modeling formulation adopted for the coarse-to-fine pipeline. The natural language inference engine models vertical conflict detection as an asymmetric pair, matching an individual candidate statutory premise ($p_i$) against an operative draft ordinance block ($h_q$). This structural formulation effectively captures direct contradictions where a single national statute explicitly invalidates an ordinance clause, such as unauthorized penalty escalation under Republic Act No.~7160. However, the system does not inherently detect multi-statute cumulative conflicts, where an ordinance provision complies with each national statute in isolation but breaches a combined legislative framework formed by multiple distinct codes. Expanding the inference mechanism to support multi-premise reasoning across multiple statutes exceeds local workstation constraints and is left for future work.

    The final constraint defines the institutional and operational role of the conflict detection system within local governance. The pipeline is designed exclusively as an ex-ante assistive advisory tool intended for legal researchers during committee review preparation rather than an autonomous decision maker. The system produces calibrated confidence estimates and highlighted text spans to point out potential legal friction, but it does not make binding statutory determinations or substitute for judicial review under the Supreme Court. Furthermore, political questions, policy choices, and socio-economic trade-offs remain exclusively with city council members. Defining this operational boundary ensures that the system functions strictly within established principles of administrative law to safeguard human oversight throughout the local legislative process.
