\chapter{Methodology}
This chapter explains the methodology used to build and evaluate the Coarse-to-Fine Semantic Conflict Detection System. It describes how Philippine legal texts are collected and cleaned, and how the two-stage Retrieve-then-Entail architecture is organized. The chapter also details the creation of the ground truth dataset and the selection of candidate models. Finally, it outlines the evaluation metrics and hardware constraints, which are chosen to match the computing capacity of an average Local Government Unit (LGU).


\section{Conceptual Framework}
This section maps how data flows through the proposed system. The architecture follows an Input-Process-Output (IPO) design organized into a two-stage ``Coarse-to-Fine'' retrieve-then-entail pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:conceptualframework}). By decoupling broad statutory candidate retrieval from compute-intensive textual entailment classification, the framework overcomes the high computational demands and memory limits that traditionally impede local legal NLP deployment. The workflow is divided into two operational phases: Phase~1 (Offline Foundation and Optimization) and Phase~2 (Live Online Retrieve-then-Entail Pipeline).

    Figure~\ref{fig:conceptualframework} illustrates the complete end-to-end information flow of the conflict detection architecture, detailing the functional separation between offline foundation pre-computation (Phase~1) and the live pre-enactment review pipeline (Phase~2). The flowchart depicts an Input-Process-Output workflow organized into modular processing layers, highlighting how heterogeneous statutory archives are ingested, normalized, indexed, and logically verified under local edge compute constraints. In Phase~1, the system compiles the dual statutory knowledge base (combining 25,432 national statutes and 1,664 local ordinances), builds pre-computed BM25 and dense embedding indexes, and screens candidate neural architectures against an adviser-reviewed 350-pair ground truth benchmark. In Phase~2, an ex-ante ordinance draft undergoes structural boundary parsing and procedural boilerplate filtering before being routed through Stage~1 hybrid retrieval (fusing lexical BM25 and dense bi-encoder streams via Reciprocal Rank Fusion) and Stage~2 Cross-Encoder inference. This coarse-to-fine design restricts compute-intensive cross-attention to a focused candidate shortlist ($k=50$), allowing deep semantic conflict detection to execute efficiently on standard local government desktop workstations while producing transparent, explainable audit reports.

    \subsection{Phase 1: Offline Foundation and Optimization}
    This preliminary phase establishes the empirical foundation of the system by compiling the statutory corpus, building pre-computed retrieval indexes, and selecting candidate transformer logic engines through a structured benchmark screening process.

    During dual-corpus ingestion and pre-computation, the system compiles a comprehensive dual-jurisdiction statutory archive combining the Philippine national statutory collection (Republic Acts, Executive Orders, Presidential Decrees, Acts, Batas Pambansa, and Commonwealth Acts) with Davao City local legislation (historical Landmark Ordinances and digital LISSP ordinance records). The compiled corpus undergoes legal text normalization, optical character recognition (OCR) noise mitigation on historical scans, and structural section segmentation into granular, self-contained regulatory units ($\sim 200\text{--}300$ tokens). Cleaned statutory provisions are then indexed offline: dense semantic representations are pre-computed using a lightweight transformer bi-encoder, while sparse inverted lexical indexes are constructed using BM25 ($k_1 = 1.5, b = 0.75$) alongside unsupervised statutory topic modeling.

    Following corpus indexing, model benchmarking and selection evaluates candidate neural architectures against a curated Ground Truth Legal Benchmark stratified across three granular difficulty tiers: Tier~1 (Surface Lexical), Tier~2 (Structural Preemption), and Tier~3 (Latent Semantic). A multi-stage empirical screening and ablation process evaluates candidate bi-encoders by balancing inference latency against candidate Recall@$k$, alongside candidate cross-encoders evaluated across precision, latency, and quantization axes. Based on these empirical evaluations, the highest-performing models are selected as the production engines: an optimal dense bi-encoder for Stage~1 candidate retrieval, and an optimal fine-tuned cross-encoder for Stage~2 contradiction reasoning.

    \subsection{Phase 2: Live System Workflow}
    The live system executes the automated conflict detection pipeline when a newly drafted local ordinance is submitted for pre-enactment review, operating through an Input-Process-Output structure.

    The workflow begins with system input ingestion, where a legislative researcher uploads a newly proposed ex-ante ordinance draft. Because draft measures originate within local government offices as digital-born documents (.docx or digital PDF), they are free from optical scanning noise. The ingestion pipeline segments the draft into individual substantive provisions, filtering out non-regulatory procedural boilerplate such as the Title, Separability, Repealing, and Effectivity clauses. The isolated substantive provisions are then vectorized in real time via the selected dense bi-encoder alongside sparse lexical token extraction.

    The computational process core then executes two complementary stages: hybrid candidate retrieval followed by natural language inference. In Stage~1 (Dual-Stream Hybrid Information Retrieval), the system narrows the comprehensive statutory search space down to a focused candidate pool. Stream~A executes lexical sparse search via BM25 to match exact statutory citations, numerical penalty amounts, and administrative agency terms, while Stream~B executes dense semantic search via the selected bi-encoder, measuring cosine similarity between the draft provision and statutory embeddings to capture abstract regulatory intent. The ranked outputs are combined using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) integrated with a Statutory Hierarchy Safeguard prioritizing higher-ranking national statutes over local provisions, extracting the Top-$k$ candidate provisions without overburdening local LGU compute resources.

    In Stage~2 (Natural Language Inference), following the standard sequence-pair classification formulation established by Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} and adapted for transformer cross-encoders by Reimers and Gurevych~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}, each retrieved statutory candidate is paired with the draft ordinance provision into a single cross-encoder sequence:

    \noindent\textit{Cross-Encoder Joint Sequence Input Representation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{x} = [\text{CLS}] \, \text{Statutory Premise} \, [\text{SEP}] \, \text{Ordinance Hypothesis} \, [\text{SEP}]
        \label{eq:cross_encoder_seq}
        \eqcaption{Cross-Encoder Joint Sequence Input Representation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $[\text{CLS}]$ marks the classification sequence start and $[\text{SEP}]$ separates jurisdictional text boundaries.

    Equation~\ref{eq:cross_encoder_seq} combines the retrieved statutory section and the draft ordinance provision into a single token sequence separated by delimiter tokens. This allows every word in the statutory premise to interact directly with every word in the draft hypothesis through bidirectional cross-attention. This direct comparison is necessary because legal conflicts often depend on small details---such as specific conditions, penalty ceilings, or exceptions---that independent bi-encoder vectors can easily miss. The model's classification head then calculates calibrated probabilities for Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction, outputting a conflict probability $P(\text{Contradiction})$.

    Finally, the system presents an audit trail showing the top-$k$ shortlisted laws for human review. It compares the contradiction probability against a calibrated decision threshold $\tau^*$, tuned with an $F_2$-score weighting to prioritize catching true conflicts. When the probability meets or exceeds this threshold ($P(\text{Contradiction}) \ge \tau^*$), the system flags a potential legal conflict and classifies it into one of two alert types: a Vertical Preemption Alert for conflicts with superior national statutes under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine, or a Horizontal Inconsistency Notice for contradictions or overlaps with existing Davao City ordinances. To help legislative researchers interpret the result, the user interface displays side-by-side text comparisons, highlighted conflicting phrases, and model confidence scores. If the probability remains below the threshold ($P(\text{Contradiction}) < \tau^*$), the system reports that no direct conflicts were found, confirming consistency with both national statutes and prior local enactments.


\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:research_design}
    This study uses an applied research design to build a practical software tool for local governance. The researchers use an experimental systems development approach to solve the delays and difficulties of manual ordinance review through automated detection. This setup connects principles from legal informatics and natural language processing to address a real local need. By focusing on practical transformer-based models, the design connects theoretical computer science with everyday government office work. The experiment directly tests whether a retrieve-then-entail pipeline can operate reliably in a local legislative setting.

    The experimental setup tests the retrieval and classification modules separately to measure search speed and accuracy independently. Building the system step-by-step allows the researchers to adjust the information retrieval and natural language inference stages using local legal texts. This engineering process ensures that the finished tool accounts for the writing styles found in both city ordinances and national statutes. In this way, applied research methods help turn modern NLP concepts into a usable tool for the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod. As a result, the findings establish a clear benchmark for automating pre-enactment legal checks on local legislation.

\section{Corpus Acquisition and Exploratory Analysis}
    \label{sec:corpus_acquisition}
    Building the legal database requires collecting text from different public repositories so that the system includes as many relevant laws as possible. These sources provide the national statutes and city ordinances needed to check draft legislation for consistency. To keep the language consistent and make vector processing simpler, the system gathers only the English-language versions of all legal texts. This choice matches Philippine statutory rules under Section~20, Chapter~5, Book~I of Executive Order No.~292 (Administrative Code of 1987)~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, which explicitly mandates that in the interpretation of laws promulgated in official languages, the English text shall control unless otherwise specifically provided. National statutes and local ordinances are gathered using separate automated scripts designed for the format of each source.

        \subsection{Corpus Composition}
        The Lawphil Project (Arellano Law Foundation) serves as the primary digitized extraction source for national statutory text because its documents are provided in clean HTML, follow a consistent layout, and offer broad historical coverage~\cite{lawphil2000}. Automated scraping scripts browse through chronological and alphabetical indexes to collect Republic Acts, Batas Pambansa, Commonwealth Acts, Acts, Executive Orders, and Presidential Decrees. Using a custom web extraction pipeline built by the proponents, statutory extraction was conducted as of \textbf{March 2026}, collecting 25,432 unique legal records enacted between 1900 and 2026. The Official Gazette of the Republic of the Philippines~\cite{officialgazette} and the Chan Robles Virtual Law Library were used as authoritative secondary cross-references to verify enactment dates, chronological numbering, and historical amendment status. All collected national laws are converted into standardized JSON records with metadata, such as dates of approval, titles, and section numbers. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lawphil_web_interface}, the Lawphil repository presents statutory text in clean, unstyled HTML typography with a predictable hierarchical URL schema (e.g., \texttt{/statutes/repacts/ra2018/ra\_11035\_2018.html} for Republic Act No.~11035). This standardized structure separates introductory legislative headers from operative provisions and eliminates the need for dynamic JavaScript browser rendering, facilitating rapid, fault-tolerant web scraping.

def extract_statutory_corpus(base_url, start_year=1900, end_year=2026):
    """Extracts, sanitizes, and structures national statutes from the online archive."""
    statute_records = []
    for year in range(start_year, end_year + 1):
        year_index = fetch_page(f"{base_url}/year/{year}")
        statute_links = parse_index_links(year_index)
        for link in statute_links:
            # Fetch raw web page and parse HTML DOM tree

            soup = BeautifulSoup(html_content, "html.parser")
            
            # Extract statutory metadata and clean document body
            metadata = extract_header_metadata(soup)
            body_text = clean_document_body(soup)
            sections = segment_into_sections(body_text)
            
            statute_records.append({
                "statute_id": metadata.id,
                "title": metadata.title,
                "instrument": metadata.type,
                "date_enacted": metadata.date,
                "sections": sections
            })
    return statute_records

        Listing~\ref{lst:corpus_extraction} details the programmatic execution logic of the statutory web extraction script, outlining the sequential functions for annual index traversal, DOM parsing, metadata extraction, and provision boundary segmentation. The script is structured to crawl legislative archives spanning from 1900 through 2026, parsing HTML document trees via BeautifulSoup while implementing rate-limiting delays to preserve source server availability. During execution, the pipeline extracts key statutory metadata---including enactment identifiers, approval dates, and legislative titles---before passing the document body to a regex-based section segmenter that isolates individual statutory provisions into distinct JSON fields. Methodologically, this automated extraction guarantees uniform data serialization across all 25,432 national enactments, eliminating web scraping artifacts and ensuring that downstream embedding models evaluate pure statutory syntax.

        To capture the complete statutory search space, the national collection is paired with Davao City local legislation gathered across two complementary acquisition streams. The first channel consists of the official Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod Public Web Portal, which provides selective historical landmark ordinances and city uploads spanning approximately fifteen years, capturing prominent regulatory codes in public safety, zoning, and health~\cite{spdavao2026}. The second and primary channel comprises the local Legislative Information Support System Program (LISSP), acquired directly through formal administrative coordination with the Sangguniang Panlungsod Secretariat and Information Technology division~\cite{citygov2025lissp}. Through this institutional coordination, local authorities granted the proponents direct access to digitized enactments via a shared repository, encompassing enactments passed primarily by the 19th and 20th City Councils between 2021 and 2025. Together, these two channels yielded an archive of exactly 1,664 local enactments spanning from 1950 to 2025. These 1,664 documents represent the complete accessible digital corpus gathered within the proponents' institutional capabilities. In practice, the true cumulative historical total of ordinances enacted since Davao City was chartered under Commonwealth Act No.~51 in 1936 remains unrecorded on the internet and unknown to local authorities. In institutional consultations, Sangguniang Panlungsod personnel confirmed that an official cumulative ledger does not exist because local legislation is numbered sequentially per calendar year and council term rather than in an unbroken cumulative sequence. Furthermore, the vast majority of historical enactments, particularly those prior to the 2021 LISSP digital initiative, exist exclusively as physical paper documents preserved in archive rooms and filing cabinets at the Sangguniang Panlungsod building on San Pedro Street, requiring manual page-by-page physical inventorying to tally. When combined with the 25,432 national statutes, the total statutory search space comprises \textbf{27,096 legal enactments} ($N_{\text{sections}} = 176,421$).

        \subsection{Corpus Characteristics and Inherent Biases}
        \label{sec:corpus_biases}
        A close look at the collected national laws reveals a large imbalance across legal topics. Over 40\% of the national statutes consist of routine administrative actions, such as converting local schools, renaming roads, adjusting municipal boundaries, or granting broadcast franchises. In contrast, major regulatory areas that interact with city powers, such as public health, land zoning, and local taxation, make up less than 15\% of the total national collection. Searching through this raw distribution without filtering would cause search algorithms to return irrelevant administrative laws instead of actual regulatory rules. To keep search results useful, the system must separate these routine administrative laws from substantive regulatory provisions.

        The local ordinance archive also shows an uneven time distribution and gaps in older records. For ordinances passed before 2021, city record-keeping mainly focused on digitizing major ``Landmark Ordinances,'' leaving ordinary regulations from earlier decades in physical paper storage~\cite{spdavao2026}. As a result, the pre-2021 local records represent a selective collection of prominent laws rather than a complete legislative record. While post-2021 records from the digital LISSP database are much more complete, some numerical gaps remain due to missing committee drafts and unindexed attachments. These temporal biases mean that the available local archive contains substantially more recent ordinances than older enactments.

        A fundamental technical distinction must be maintained regarding the document media of the legislative corpus. All 1,664 archival ordinance documents received from the Sangguniang Panlungsod are historical scanned physical paper PDFs, rather than digital-born documents exported from word processing software. Older ordinances scanned at low resolution suffer from faded print, ink bleed-through, staple shadows, and rotational skew, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:davao_ordinance_scan}. The scanned copies also contain non-text items such as government stamps, official signatures, and handwritten marginal notes that confuse standard sentence splitting tools. Clean, digital-born text (documents exported directly from modern word processors or Google Docs without scanning noise) appears exclusively during Phase~2 live system testing when legislative staff submit newly drafted ex-ante measures for pre-enactment conflict review. At the language level, local ordinances sometimes include regional terms and administrative Taglish in their introductory sections~\cite{dreisbach2021language}. These informal drafting habits differ noticeably from the formal, standardized English found in national laws.

A machine learning model trained on such unbalanced data could score 99\% nominal accuracy simply by guessing ``Neutral'' every time, while remaining unable to detect real legal conflicts.

        A key methodological decision in compiling this database is the explicit inclusion of \textbf{Executive Orders (EOs)} alongside congressional legislation. Under Article~VII, Section~17 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution and Book~III of Executive Order No.~292~\cite{philgov1987eo292}, Executive Orders are presidential acts providing rules of a general character in execution of statutory mandates. Unlike Supreme Court decisions that settle private adversarial controversies under \textit{stare decisis}, Executive Orders operate as \textit{ex-ante}, forward-looking statutory instruments possessing the full force of law. Modern Executive Orders establish binding nationwide regulatory mandates, such as Executive Order No.~26 (s.~2017) mandating nationwide public smoking bans, that legally preempt inconsistent local ordinances. Excluding Executive Orders would create an artificial regulatory gap, allowing local ordinances to contradict operative presidential mandates.

        The coverage rates confirm that the national legal archive is nearly complete across all historical governance eras.         During preliminary exploratory research, the proponents conducted initial machine learning experiments on the national statutory archive and identified severe dataset imbalances. In the national collection of 25,432 statutes, routine executive issuances and administrative reorganizations comprise nearly half of all historical enactments, while specialized regulatory domains remain relatively small. Searching for a local regulatory ordinance across this raw, unfiltered collection causes severe search drift because dense retrieval models readily match repetitive administrative phrasing across unrelated legal areas. To resolve these dataset quirks, the proponents formalized a two-stage topic modeling architecture that organizes the statutory archive into cohesive, well-separated subject areas. This structured categorization prevents routine administrative directives from crowding the candidate shortlist during coarse retrieval, establishing a balanced foundation for subsequent conflict checks.

        The topic classification pipeline groups documents automatically while running efficiently on Host~A. Statutory texts are first stripped of HTML tags and filtered against a list of 49 legal stopwords, such as ``hereby'' and ``enacted,'' which appear throughout legal texts without helping distinguish legal topics. Cleaned texts are converted into term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) feature vectors across a 25,000-term vocabulary, rewarding distinctive statutory words over routine administrative phrasing~\cite{manning2008introduction}. To keep processing fast while preserving related concepts, the pipeline applies Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to project the sparse feature vectors into 64 latent semantic dimensions~\cite{deerwester1990indexing}. Following the latent semantic modeling principles of Landauer et al.~\cite{landauer1998introduction}, this 64-dimensional subspace captures core legal associations while filtering out lexical noise, followed by standard $L_2$ vector normalization.

        Dividing statutory texts directly into a small set of broad categories risks mixing unrelated legislative topics. To prevent this, the normalized 64-dimensional vectors are first divided into $K = 28$ smaller clusters using MiniBatch $K$-Means clustering~\cite{sculley2010webscale}. Following the grouping method recommended by Grootendorst~\cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} and Aggarwal~\cite{aggarwal2012mining}, starting with smaller groups separates repetitive laws, such as franchise charters or school conversions, into clean, distinct clusters. The pipeline then adapts class-based TF-IDF ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$) from Grootendorst~\cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} to identify key vocabulary by treating all text within a micro-cluster as a single combined document. The topic modeling and domain clustering formulations are computed as follows:

        \noindent\textit{Class-Based Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$):}
        \begin{equation}
            c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF_{i, c} = \frac{|t_{i, c}|}{|c|} \times \log\left(1 + \frac{A}{\sum_{k} |t_{i, k}|}\right)
            \label{eq:c_tfidf}
            \eqcaption{Class-Based Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$)}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $|t_{i, c}|$ denotes the frequency of term $i$ in cluster $c$, $|c|$ is the total word count of cluster $c$, and $A$ indicates the average word count per cluster across the archive. This cluster-level weighting elevates terminology that specifically characterizes a legislative group while penalizing ubiquitous statutory phrasing.

        Following nearest-centroid classification principles~\cite{rocchio1971relevance, aggarwal2012mining} and temperature-scaled probabilistic mapping~\cite{guo2017calibration}, the macro legal domain centroid and calibrated assignment probability are defined as follows:

        \noindent\textit{Macro Legal Domain Centroid and Softmax Probability:}
        \begin{equation}
            \mathbf{C}_m = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_m} \mathbf{z}_i}{\left\|\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_m} \mathbf{z}_i\right\|_2}, \quad P(m \mid \mathbf{z}_i) = \frac{\exp(\tau \cdot \cos(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{C}_m))}{\sum_{j} \exp(\tau \cdot \cos(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{C}_j))}
            \label{eq:macro_centroid}
            \eqcaption{Macro Legal Domain Centroid and Softmax Probability}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $\mathbf{z}_i$ represents the normalized 64-dimensional SVD vector of document $i$, $\mathbf{C}_m$ denotes the normalized centroid of macro domain $m$, and $\tau = 6.0$ functions as the inverse temperature scaling parameter.

        The formulations in Equations~\ref{eq:c_tfidf} and~\ref{eq:macro_centroid} identify distinctive legal vocabulary and assign consistent topics across the national statutory corpus. Equation~\ref{eq:c_tfidf} calculates class-based TF-IDF ($c\text{-}TF\text{-}IDF$), which treats each group of laws as a combined document to find keywords that stand out in that group. In Equation~\ref{eq:macro_centroid}, document vectors are averaged into cluster centers $\mathbf{C}_m$. A temperature-scaled softmax function then converts cosine similarity into clear probabilities, where the scaling factor ($\tau = 6.0$) follows the calibration method of Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017calibration} to keep probability scores well-separated. This allows the system to organize over 25,000 national statutes into eight clear regulatory domains using standard desktop memory, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:national_macro_domains}.

        To handle provisions in the 5.31\% exceedance tail without information loss under standard-context models, the pipeline implements a hierarchical overlapping sub-chunking fallback. Adapting the sliding window strategy formulated by Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} alongside multi-instance max-pooling aggregation principles from Anand et al.~\cite{anand2020explainable} and Seo et al.~\cite{seo2025sliding}, when an extracted section exceeds a safety threshold of 448 tokens, the preprocessing module divides the section into overlapping sub-chunks using a window of $w = 384$ tokens and a stride of $s = 256$ tokens (128-token overlap). Capping the sub-chunk window at 384 tokens reserves a 64-token structural buffer within the 512-token ceiling to accommodate the prepended metadata header along with mandatory special tokens ($[CLS]$, $[SEP]$)~\cite{devlin2019bert}. Each sub-chunk preserves the prepended structural metadata header $\left[ \text{Statute Title}, \text{ Section } S_i\text{: Catchline} \right]$ to maintain institutional context. In Stage~1 retrieval, the parent provision embedding is computed by mean-pooling sub-chunk vectors, capturing semantic concepts across all paragraphs. In Stage~2 inference, following sliding-window max pooling~\cite{anand2020explainable, seo2025sliding}, the Cross-Encoder evaluates each sub-chunk independently against the local ordinance hypothesis $h$, determining provision-level conflict through max-pooling across sub-chunk contradiction probabilities:

        \noindent\textit{Max-Pooled Sub-Chunk Contradiction Probability:}
        \begin{equation}
            \hat{P}(\text{Contradiction} \mid p_i, h) = \max_{j} P\left(\text{Contradiction} \mid p_{i,j}, h\right)
            \label{eq:max_pooled_subchunk}
            \eqcaption{Max-Pooled Sub-Chunk Contradiction Probability}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $p_{i,j}$ denotes the $j$-th sub-chunk of statutory section $p_i$.

        Equation~\ref{eq:max_pooled_subchunk} defines the max-pooling method used to score long statutory sections that are split into smaller sub-chunks. By assigning the section's overall conflict score to the highest contradiction probability found among its sub-chunks, this ensures that an isolated prohibition or condition buried inside a long section still triggers an alert without being diluted by surrounding agreeable text. In practice, this max-pooling strategy avoids missing conflicts in complex codes where penalties are placed inside longer administrative descriptions, keeping the system sensitive to potential violations. Alongside this sub-chunking approach, the proponents also test ModernBERT as a long-context candidate model~\cite{warner2024modernbert, kadowaki2025modernbert}, checking whether its 8,192-token attention window can process lengthy enactments directly.

\section{Preprocessing and Normalization}
\label{sec:preprocessing_filtering}
Because raw statutory documents originate from disparate digital repositories and historical scanning archives, they require systematic, source-specific preprocessing and text normalization pipelines. National statutes, local municipal archives, and live ex-ante drafts each present distinct document media, formatting conventions, and noise characteristics that must be addressed individually. In the national statutory collection, continuous legislative texts must be cleansed of web markup artifacts, segmented into discrete numbered provisions, and enriched with hierarchical metadata. In the local ordinance collection, physical scanning degradation, photocopier borders, and non-legislative administrative matter must be removed via vision-language modeling and state machine extraction. In live ex-ante drafts, digital-born text must be segmented into substantive clauses while filtering out procedural boilerplate. The following subsections detail the individual normalization architectures developed for each statutory source.

        \subsubsection{Context Prepending and Long-Tail Sub-Chunking}
        \label{sec:national_context_chunking}
        Evaluating isolated statutory sections creates a risk of semantic dilution. An isolated sub-clause stripped of its parent enactment number, title, and catchline loses its governing institutional authority, causing retrieval models to confuse local regulatory penalties with unrelated national tax or criminal codes. To maintain semantic precision and statutory hierarchy across both coarse search and fine inference, the pipeline applies structural context prepending. Listing~\ref{lst:context_prepending} details the context prepending procedure implemented to format granular statutory provisions.

        \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Pseudocode of Section Context Prepending for Granular Provision Chunking}, label={lst:context_prepending}]
def prepend_statutory_context(statute_record, section):
    """
    Prepends governing legislative identity to isolated section text
    to prevent semantic vector dilution and preserve hierarchical rank.
    """
    prefix = (
        f"[{statute_record['instrument_type']} No. {statute_record['enactment_number']}: "
        f"{statute_record['title']} | {section['section_number']}: {section['section_title']}] "
    )
    model_ready_text = prefix + section['raw_text']
    return model_ready_text
        \end{lstlisting}

        Listing~\ref{lst:context_prepending} outlines the context-prepending routine that attaches legislative title and section details to isolated statutory sections before creating vector embeddings. This step is implemented to keep context intact, ensuring that individual provisions separated from their parent law do not lose their legal identity or jurisdiction. In the routine shown, the function formats a metadata header combining instrument type, enactment number, statute title, section number, and title before attaching the section body text. Adding this header allows the transformer attention layers to take the governing legal context into account. As a result, the model reliably distinguishes local regulations from superior national laws during both candidate retrieval and natural language inference.

        Adapting the structural context-prepending methodology of Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} for legal contract and statutory document understanding, every extracted statutory section begins with a structural metadata header containing the statute title, section number, and section catchline:

        \noindent\textit{Context-Prepended Statutory Chunk Representation:}
        \begin{equation}
            \mathbf{x}_{\text{chunk}} = \left[ \text{Statute Title}, \text{ Section } S_i\text{: Catchline} \right] \mathbin{\Vert} \mathbf{t}_{\text{verbatim}}
            \label{eq:hierarchical_chunk}
            \eqcaption{Context-Prepended Statutory Chunk Representation}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $\mathbf{t}_{\text{verbatim}}$ denotes the exact text extracted from the statutory record, and $\mathbin{\Vert}$ represents string concatenation.

        Equation~\ref{eq:hierarchical_chunk} defines the representation of a context-prepended statutory chunk $\mathbf{x}_{\text{chunk}}$, built by attaching the parent metadata header to the verbatim statutory text $\mathbf{t}_{\text{verbatim}}$. This chunk design preserves legal context across both Stage~1 retrieval and Stage~2 inference. By attaching the statute title and section heading to the raw clause, the resulting text contains both the specific legal rule and its broader statutory authority. During inference, transformer attention heads compare words across both the header and the operative sentence. This avoids ambiguity and ensures that downstream models recognize whether a rule comes from a national statute or a local city enactment.

        Transforming physical city paper archives into machine-readable text requires evaluating candidate document ingestion architectures under consumer hardware constraints. While contemporary measures from the digital LISSP database provide clean electronic text, historical enactments from earlier legislative terms exist predominantly as scanned physical PDF documents. To establish an optimal transcription strategy for the 1,664 local records, the proponents formulated a planned candidate extraction experiment comparing three distinct technological paradigms: traditional open-source OCR via Tesseract v5.x~\cite{smith2007overview}, modern layout-aware deep learning OCR via Surya OCR~\cite{paruchuri2024surya}, and a quantized local vision-language model via Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. As an independent reference standard for transcription accuracy, the evaluation protocol establishes a gold-standard digitization baseline using Google Gemini 1.5 Flash multimodal extraction coupled with comprehensive line-by-line human legal verification. The candidate pipelines are evaluated across Character Error Rate (CER), Word Error Rate (WER), Table Fidelity (TEDS), and Legal Marker Precision on a representative 60-document stratified test suite, with empirical benchmarking results formally reported in Chapter~4 (Section~\ref{sec:results_ocr_evaluation}).

        To support offline execution on consumer hardware, the vision-language architecture deploys Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, a multimodal transformer that pairs a vision transformer backbone with a 3-billion parameter causal language model decoder. Executed locally on Host~B (equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 7435HS processor, 16~GB DDR5 system memory, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5050 Laptop GPU with 8~GB GDDR6 VRAM running Windows 11), the architecture is configured to transcribe scanned enactments while preserving exact statutory phrasing. Proprietary cloud platforms such as Google Cloud Document AI~\cite{google2024documentai} were excluded from routine production due to recurring per-page API fees and legal data sovereignty restrictions under the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No.~10173)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. Deploying the quantized open-weight model locally resolves these operational constraints, providing high transcription fidelity while executing completely offline within local government IT boundaries.

        The document ingestion pipeline begins by recursively discovering target PDF files across archival city directories. Individual enactments are loaded using PyMuPDF, which inspects document metadata and iterates through pages sequentially. Adapting archival document image perimeter-trimming heuristics for optical scanning artifact reduction~\cite{lopresti2008optical}, the ingestor applies an inset cropbox that trims exactly 3\% of the page perimeter along all four borders:

        \noindent\textit{Archival Inset Perimeter CropBox Trimming:}
        \begin{equation}
            \text{CropBox} = \left[ 0.03 \cdot W, \; 0.03 \cdot H, \; 0.97 \cdot W, \; 0.97 \cdot H \right]
            \label{eq:cropbox_trim}
            \eqcaption{Archival Inset Perimeter CropBox Trimming}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $W$ and $H$ represent the physical page width and height in points.

        Equation~\ref{eq:cropbox_trim} defines the boundary for trimming 3\% ($0.03$) from each edge of a scanned page with width $W$ and height $H$. This cropping step removes common scanner artifacts---such as dark borders, staple marks, and binder punch holes---found in older paper records. Removing these border marks before sending the image to the vision-language model prevents visual noise from being mistaken for punctuation or causing the model to generate repetitive nonsense tokens. The trimmed bounding box keeps the focus directly on the legislative text, ensuring cleaner and more consistent transcription.

        Following perimeter trimming, the rasterization module renders each page at an image resolution of 105 dots per inch (DPI). Selecting 105 DPI provides an optimal empirical balance between character legibility and sequence length for high-density legal documents. The rendered image is converted to an RGB array and passed to the visual processor. The processor rescales page patches dynamically, clamping total image pixels between a minimum of $200,704$ pixels ($256 \times 28 \times 28$) and a maximum of $351,232$ pixels ($448 \times 28 \times 28$). Because Qwen2.5-VL allocates visual tokens in $28 \times 28$ patch increments using two-dimensional rotary position embeddings (2D-RoPE), bounding the pixel budget stabilizes sequence length and prevents out-of-memory GPU exceptions during long batch runs.

        To accommodate local workstation hardware constraints without sacrificing recognition fidelity, the model weights are quantized using 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) precision via the BitsAndBytes library~\cite{dettmers2024qlora}. The quantization configuration uses double quantization and computes attention projections in 16-bit floating point (\texttt{torch.float16}) using PyTorch scaled dot-product attention (\texttt{sdpa}). Under 4-bit NF4 compression, the model footprint occupies less than 3.5~GB of operational VRAM, allowing the pipeline to execute comfortably on consumer-grade laptop graphics hardware. Text generation is guided by an explicit verbatim system prompt instructing the model to transcribe all section titles, ordinals, dates, names, and monetary values without summarization or commentary. Generation settings enforce deterministic greedy decoding ($\text{do\_sample} = \text{False}$, $\text{use\_cache} = \text{True}$) bounded by a ceiling of 1,000 new tokens per page.

        The pipeline adopts a page-level processing architecture rather than ingesting entire multi-page documents in a single model forward pass. Ingesting full multi-page ordinances simultaneously would require concatenating thousands of visual patch tokens, which dilutes spatial self-attention and exceeds consumer GPU memory ceilings. In contrast, processing documents page by page enforces focused visual attention on discrete text blocks while scaling linearly ($O(N)$) across documents ranging from single-page resolutions to 50-page tax codes. The pipeline inserts explicit page delineators ($\texttt{--- [Page } X\texttt{] ---}$) into the extracted text, ensuring continuous traceability back to physical council records. Furthermore, page-level execution supports fault-tolerant checkpointing: the system inspects output files on disk and automatically resumes execution across interrupted batch queues by skipping previously completed files. Page-level garbage collection (\texttt{gc.collect()}) and explicit CUDA cache clearing (\texttt{torch.cuda.empty\_cache()}) are executed after each document, preventing memory fragmentation during multi-hour ingestion runs.

        \subsubsection{Substantive Legislative Body Extraction}
        \label{sec:local_substantive_extraction}
        Scanned local enactments generally contain three distinct parts: opening attendance rosters, the substantive legislative provisions, and concluding signature blocks. Separating the actual legal text from administrative details is essential for accurate conflict detection. Leaving councillor names in the text introduces personal names that distort Stage~1 dense retrieval embeddings, while including signature blocks adds unnecessary clutter to candidate hypotheses in Stage~2. Splitting documents using fixed page numbers does not work reliably across local archives, because short resolutions place legal provisions on the first page, whereas long regulatory codes spread attendance lists and committee rosters across several front pages.

        To handle these structural variations, the proponents developed an adaptive boundary state machine that extracts operative legal provisions based on standard statutory markers. The extraction module scans transcripts for recognized opening markers, including enactment designations (\texttt{ORDINANCE NO.}), formal titles (\texttt{AN ORDINANCE}), and preamble clauses (\texttt{WHEREAS}). Upon detecting an opening boundary, the state machine captures the enactment identifier, title, preamble, and all operative sections (\texttt{SECTION 1}, \texttt{ARTICLE I}, \texttt{CHAPTER I}). Text capture stops immediately upon encountering formal closing markers, such as Sanggunian enactment notices (\texttt{ENACTED}), secretarial certifications (\texttt{CERTIFIED CORRECT}), vice-mayoral attestations (\texttt{ATTESTED}), or executive approval notations (\texttt{APPROVED}).

        The extraction module incorporates a fallback hierarchy to prevent data loss when archival scans have faded or missing boundary markers. If an enactment header is obscured, the extractor falls back to the long ordinance title; if the title is absent, it anchors on the initial operative section. Similarly, if formal certification headers are missing, the system terminates extraction following the standard effectivity section (\texttt{SECTION X. EFFECTIVITY}). To provide automated quality control over the extraction process, the state machine calculates an empirical boundary verification confidence score:

        \noindent\textit{Substantive Body Boundary Extraction Confidence Score:}
        \begin{equation}
            \text{Confidence}_{\text{extract}} = 0.35 \cdot C_{\text{start}} + 0.35 \cdot C_{\text{end}} + 0.30 \cdot C_{\text{sec}}
            \label{eq:extraction_confidence}
            \eqcaption{Substantive Body Boundary Extraction Confidence Score}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Where $C_{\text{start}}$ and $C_{\text{end}}$ denote boundary match confidences, and $C_{\text{sec}}$ measures section sequential coherence.

        Equation~\ref{eq:extraction_confidence} defines the confidence score $\text{Confidence}_{\text{extract}}$ used to check how cleanly the legislative text was extracted. It combines scores for the start boundary ($C_{\text{start}}$), ending signatures ($C_{\text{end}}$), and section numbering order ($C_{\text{sec}}$) using weights of $0.35$, $0.35$, and $0.30$. This score helps spot extraction errors automatically without stopping batch processing across hundreds of ordinances. Documents scoring at or above $0.70$ move on to indexing, while lower-scoring files are flagged for manual inspection. This approach lets clean scans pass through automatically while sending difficult or unusual documents to human reviewers.

        When confidence falls below 0.70, the document is flagged for manual review while preserving the full unpruned transcript on disk. Listing~\ref{lst:substantive_extraction} outlines the extraction logic.

        \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Adaptive State Machine for Substantive Legislative Body Extraction}, label={lst:substantive_extraction}]
def extract_substantive_body(raw_transcript: str) -> dict:
    """
    Extracts operative statutory provisions from local transcripts,
    stripping administrative attendance rosters and terminal signatures.
    """
    start_match = RE_ORD_NUM.search(raw_transcript) or RE_TITLE.search(raw_transcript)
    start_idx = start_match.start() if start_match else 0
    start_conf = 1.0 if start_match else 0.40

    search_region = raw_transcript[start_idx:]
    term_match = RE_TERMINAL.search(search_region)
    end_idx = start_idx + term_match.start() if term_match else len(raw_transcript)
    end_conf = 1.0 if term_match else 0.50

    substantive_text = raw_transcript[start_idx:end_idx].strip()
    sections = [int(n) for n in RE_ALL_SECTIONS.findall(substantive_text)]
    sec_conf = 1.0 if len(sections) >= 3 and sections[:3] == [1, 2, 3] else 0.40

    confidence = round(0.35 * start_conf + 0.35 * end_conf + 0.30 * sec_conf, 3)
    return {
        "status": "SUCCESS" if confidence >= 0.70 else "FLAGGED_LOW_CONFIDENCE",
        "confidence": confidence,
        "substantive_text": substantive_text
    }
        \end{lstlisting}

        Listing~\ref{lst:substantive_extraction} presents the Python implementation of the adaptive state machine for substantive legislative body extraction. This algorithm is designed to parse raw local transcripts and extract substantive enactments while stripping prefatory attendance rosters, administrative preambles, and terminal signature blocks. In the function, regular expressions scan the text for opening enactment indicators and terminal attestation keywords, slicing the transcript between these indices and validating ordinal section continuity. The function computes the composite confidence score according to Equation~\ref{eq:extraction_confidence} and returns a status dictionary indicating whether the extraction succeeded or requires manual review. Isolating the substantive body in this manner ensures that extraneous councillor names and mayoral titles do not distort dense retrieval vector embeddings or trigger spurious preemption matches in Stage~2.

        \subsubsection{Automated Quality Audit and Conservative Normalization}
        \label{sec:local_quality_audit}
        Following raw transcription, the system executes an automated quality audit to detect and categorize transcription anomalies across seven mutually exclusive categories: definite OCR corruption, possible corruption, formatting noise, valid legal boilerplate, valid table structures, page metadata anomalies, and items requiring human review. The audit scanner identifies severe failure states, such as Unicode replacement characters (\texttt{\textbackslash ufffd}), foreign script hallucinations (CJK and Cyrillic character ranges), raw decoding markers (\texttt{undefined}), and autoregressive repetition loops. Documents with severe corruptions or total lengths below 150 characters are quarantined into a dedicated review queue for manual verification.

        To minimize false positives, the audit pattern matching explicitly accommodates authentic legislative structures. A broad repeated-character regular expression frequently flags Markdown table borders (\texttt{|---|---|}) as OCR corruption. To prevent this error, table boundary delimiters, pipes, and header markers are explicitly excluded from symbol repetition rules. Valid legislative boilerplate notations such as \texttt{"xxx xxx xxx"}, which denote unamended text in amending ordinances, are protected using negative lookahead expressions. Geographic survey coordinates containing degree symbols ($^\circ$), minute marks ($'$), and second marks ($''$), as well as accented Filipino surnames (\textit{Pe\~na}, \textit{Dañguilan}), are validated against an extended Unicode whitelist. Furthermore, page sequences beginning at Page~2 due to excluded cover sheets are recognized as valid contiguous sequences rather than misordered files.

        Deterministic text normalization is performed via a conservative cleaning pipeline that strictly preserves legal meaning. The cleanup module applies Unicode Form KC normalization, strips invisible zero-width characters, removes stray VLM prompt echoes (such as \texttt{**[body text]:**} / \textit{zh\`engw\'en} markers), and collapses runaway character loops exceeding seven consecutive repetitions to a standardized three-character representation. All modifications are logged in an audit report with line-level traceability. Under strict architectural safeguards, automated scripts are prohibited from altering personal names, calendar dates, numerical monetary amounts, ordinance numbers, statutory citations, penalty clauses, or section numbering. Discrepancies involving these sensitive entities are routed exclusively to human legal researchers.

        Downstream from automated cleanup, the architecture incorporates an advisory language quality review module combining a curated Philippine legal and toponymic gazetteer, contextual language model perplexity scoring, and named-entity recognition. The gazetteer whitelists all 182 official barangays of Davao City, administrative districts, local executive departments, and statutory Latin maxims (\textit{in pari materia}, \textit{mutatis mutandis}, \textit{ultra vires}), preventing specialized terminology from triggering false spelling alarms. Contextual perplexity scoring evaluates token predictability within a sliding context window; tokens exhibiting high pseudo-perplexity and characteristic alphanumeric substitution patterns (such as misreading numeral \texttt{0} as uppercase letter \texttt{O} in monetary figures) are assigned high diagnostic confidence. The system presents these candidate errors on an interactive review dashboard, coupling the transcribed text with coordinate bounding boxes linking back to the original physical PDF scan, allowing human annotators to review highlighted anomalies prior to final vector indexing.

        \subsubsection{Procedural Boilerplate Filtering and Provision Formatting}
        \label{sec:local_boilerplate_filtering}
        Filtering formulaic boilerplate prevents search drift and computational waste during candidate retrieval across the local collection. In Davao City legislation, procedural clauses---specifically Title, Separability, Repealing, and Effectivity clauses---contain standardized legislative phrases that carry zero substantive regulatory restrictions. When these sections are vectorized, their embeddings align with unrelated administrative acts that happen to share identical legal boilerplate. By systematically pruning these non-operative sections during ingestion, the preprocessor directs candidate retrieval exclusively toward substantive regulatory and penal provisions. As established in the corpus census, this filtering prunes 3,641 procedural sections (30.85\%) from the local ordinance collection, isolating 8,160 operative provisions for indexing.

        Following boilerplate filtering, each operative provision is formatted with a standardized municipal metadata header following the context prepending principles of Equation~\ref{eq:hierarchical_chunk}. Each local retrieval unit is prepended with its governing local council authority: $\left[ \text{Davao City Ordinance No. } O_i\text{: Title} \mid \text{Section } S_j\text{: Catchline} \right] \mathbin{\Vert} \mathbf{t}_{\text{verbatim}}$. Attaching this municipal metadata ensures that dense bi-encoders and cross-encoders condition on the local legislative origin, preventing candidate retrieval models from confusing municipal penalties with national criminal statutes.

    \subsection{Ex-Ante Draft Ingestion and Statutory Exception Harmonization}
    \label{sec:draft_ingestion_segmentation}
    \label{sec:exception_handling}
    While historical statutory and municipal archives undergo batch offline normalization, newly drafted measures submitted for ex-ante review pass through a specialized live ingestion pipeline.

        \subsubsection{Digital-Born Draft Ingestion and Boundary Parsing}
        \label{sec:draft_ingestion_pipeline}
        A critical operational distinction in this research separates the historical training archive from live ex-ante draft submissions. While the historical Davao City collection consists of scanned physical PDFs requiring optical character recognition and text normalization, newly drafted ordinances submitted for ex-ante review are digital-born documents. Legislative staff create these proposed measures using standard word processing software, generating clean electronic text or searchable PDF files. Because digital-born drafts contain authentic character encodings without scanning artifacts, the live pipeline bypasses OCR error correction entirely. This structural decoupling allows the preprocessor to focus immediately on automated section boundary parsing and clause segmentation.

        To evaluate legislative commands without semantic distortion, an automated regular expression parser splits the submitted draft at formal section boundaries into discrete operative blocks. In Philippine local legislative practice, proposed measures follow standardized structural templates established by the Department of the Interior and Local Government~\cite{dilg2022drafting}. Real-world legislative drafts contain substantial procedural boilerplate that does not express substantive legal commands. If routine clauses such as title declarations, repealing clauses, and effectivity dates are fed into Stage~1 retrieval, they match thousands of administrative national statutes containing similar formulaic phrases. Listing~\ref{lst:provision_extractor} details the regular expression parser that segments live draft measures into discrete operative sections while filtering out procedural boilerplate.

        \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Code Snippet of the Operative Provision Extractor and Boilerplate Filter}, label={lst:provision_extractor}]
import re

def extract_operative_provisions(draft_text):
    """
    Parses digital-born draft ordinance into discrete sections,
    filtering out non-operative procedural boilerplate clauses.
    """
    # Regex pattern to segment text at formal section boundaries
    pattern = r'(SECTION\s+\d+\.?[^\n]*\n.*?)(?=(?:SECTION\s+\d+|CERTIFIED|ENACTED|\Z))'
    matches = re.findall(pattern, draft_text, re.DOTALL | re.IGNORECASE)
    
    # Procedural clauses that do not carry substantive regulatory commands
    boilerplate_keywords = ["TITLE", "SEPARABILITY", "REPEALING", "EFFECTIVITY"]
    operative_sections = []
    
    for match in matches:
        clean_chunk = ' '.join(match.strip().split())
        header = clean_chunk.split('.')[0] if '.' in clean_chunk else clean_chunk[:30]
        
        # Exclude non-operative procedural boilerplate
        if any(kw in header.upper() for kw in boilerplate_keywords):
            continue
            
        operative_sections.append({
            "section_header": header,
            "operative_text": clean_chunk
        })
        
    return operative_sections
        \end{lstlisting}

        Listing~\ref{lst:provision_extractor} presents the Python implementation of the operative provision extractor, featuring regular expression lookahead patterns and a procedural boilerplate keyword filter. This algorithm is incorporated to automate the structural segmentation of raw draft ordinances into clean, self-contained section blocks while systematically discarding administrative clauses. As observed in the listing, the regular expression isolates text between formal section boundaries, while the keyword filter checks section headers against procedural markers including Title, Separability, Repealing, and Effectivity clauses. Discarding these formulaic clauses prevents downstream retrieval models from matching generic legislative boilerplate against thousands of unrelated administrative acts. Consequently, only substantive regulatory and penal provisions advance to the candidate indexing and inference pipeline.

        \subsubsection{Harmonic Interpretation and Exception Handling}
        \label{sec:exception_handling_sub}
        A key challenge in conflict detection is distinguishing genuine normative contradictions from authorized statutory exceptions. In regulatory legislation, general prohibitions and permitted exceptions frequently appear within the same section using conditional syntax. Because statutory sections average between 90 and 122 tokens, an entire section fits comfortably within the 512-token input limit of candidate Cross-Encoders. The model's attention mechanism reads the general prohibition and its qualifying exceptions simultaneously, preventing false positive contradiction alerts. This joint attention ensures that local measures implementing authorized statutory exceptions are recognized as valid exercises of delegated authority.

        When a general prohibition and an authorizing exception reside in separate statutory sections, the pipeline relies on two-stage candidate retrieval. Under Philippine statutory construction, related provisions governing the same subject matter must be interpreted together as a harmonious whole under the doctrine of \textit{in pari materia}~\cite{agpalo2009statutory}. If an ordinance draft relies on an authorized statutory exemption, evaluating a specific clause without retrieving the parent exception can trigger an incorrect conflict warning. The system addresses this challenge by utilizing hybrid retrieval in Stage~1 to pull both general bans and specific exception clauses into the candidate shortlist. The Stage~2 Cross-Encoder then evaluates the draft against the authorizing exception clause and assigns an Entailment classification, confirming that the local measure operates within valid legislative authority.

    \section{Ground Truth Benchmark}
    \label{sec:ground_truth_construction}
    Evaluating candidate Cross-Encoder models in Stage~2 requires an expert-verified ground truth dataset reflecting the authentic legal realities of Philippine local legislation. Unlike general-domain natural language inference benchmarks that rely on crowdsourced sentence pairs, evaluating local conflict detection requires formal statutory interpretation skills grounded in administrative law. Because no existing NLP benchmark encompasses Philippine national statutes and local government ordinances, the proponents established an authoritative 350-pair ground truth evaluation dataset curated in collaboration with active legal researchers from the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod.

    For the ground truth evaluation benchmark, the proponents settled on exactly $N = 350$ premise-hypothesis sentence pairs based on three practical reasons: the number of participating legal researchers, realistic workload limits to avoid review fatigue, and statistical sample size requirements. Through formal coordination with the Sangguniang Panlungsod of Davao City, a cohort of exactly $P = 15$ active legal researchers agreed to evaluate the benchmark pairs. To ensure reliable labels and compute inter-rater agreement, each candidate pair requires independent evaluation by $k = 3$ distinct legal researchers. In specialized legal annotation, reading multi-sentence statutory provisions requires focused concentration. Prior studies indicate that rater attention and analytical accuracy drop when an individual's workload exceeds roughly 70 pairs~\cite{ablazo2019designing, chalkidis2022lexglue}. By grouping the 15 researchers into five balanced panels of three members ($15 / 3 = 5$ panels) and assigning 70 pairs to each annotator, the evaluation covers exactly $5 \times 70 = 350$ unique statutory pairs, producing $350 \times 3 = 1,050$ total independent legal judgments across the cohort.

    \subsection{Sample Size and Statistical Power}
    \label{sec:sample_size_power}
    The determination of the benchmark sample size ($N = 350$ pairs) is grounded in statistical power analysis for multi-class contingency evaluations~\cite{cohen1988statistical, faul2007gpower}. Under Cohen's power framework, detecting semantic conflicts across a three-class discrete decision space ($\mathcal{C} = \{\text{Entailment}, \text{Neutral}, \text{Contradiction}\}$, with $\text{df} = (3-1) = 2$ for goodness-of-fit and $\text{df} = (3-1)(3-1) = 4$ for $3 \times 3$ contingency matrices) with a medium effect size ($w = 0.30$) at a significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$ requires a minimum of $N = 108$ items to achieve standard statistical power of 80\% ($(1 - \beta) = 0.80$).

    Establishing an evaluation suite of $N = 350$ pairs provides an achieved statistical power exceeding $0.985$ ($(1 - \beta) > 98\%$), effectively eliminating Type~II error risks ($\beta < 0.015$) and ensuring robust statistical representation across all eight Macro Legal Domains defined in Section~\ref{sec:domain_categorization}. These eight domains reflect the core public services and regulatory police powers defined in Section~17 and Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160.

    This curated scale directly reflects established standards in specialized legal NLP benchmarking. While general-domain benchmarks like SNLI~\cite{bowman2015snli} rely on open crowdsourcing across tens of thousands of colloquial sentences, domain-specific legal NLI requires accredited legal professionals whose time and cognitive availability are severely constrained. High-quality, expert-adjudicated legal datasets consistently operate within this targeted scale: the SARA statutory tax benchmark comprises 376 cases~\cite{holzenberger2020a}, the annual COLIEE Task~4 legal entailment test suites provide approximately 100 pairs per competition cycle~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, alshehri2026cross}, and ContractNLI~\cite{koreeda2021contract} relies on curated subsets. At 350 premise-hypothesis pairs with a three-rater overlap yielding 1,050 independent legal judgments, the benchmark represents an authoritative, statistically sound gold standard for local legislative review.

    \subsection{Dataset Partitioning}
    \label{sec:dataset_partitioning}
    To maintain experimental rigor, prevent data leakage, and ensure reproducible model evaluation, the 350 Ground Truth pairs are partitioned using a static 70/15/15 train, validation, and test split. This split provides 245 training pairs ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$), 52 validation pairs ($N_{\text{val}} = 52$), and 53 test pairs ($N_{\text{test}} = 53$). Operating within this sample size places the evaluation in a few-shot learning regime, which is well-suited for pretrained transformer language models~\cite{gao2021making}. In machine learning, few-shot task adaptation utilizes the foundational linguistic and semantic representations already encoded within deep pretrained encoders, fine-tuning classification heads and attention parameters on domain-specific instances without requiring thousands of newly labeled examples~\cite{gao2021making}.

    The proponents specifically selected a 70/15/15 partition over a standard 80/10/10 split due to sample size constraints. In a 350-pair dataset, an 80/10/10 allocation yields only 35 validation and 35 test instances. A validation set of only 35 pairs is statistically fragile for fine-grained decision threshold calibration ($\tau^*$), where a single classification error alters observed accuracy by nearly 3\%. Furthermore, a test set of 35 items falls below the $N \ge 50$ sample size threshold established by Card et al.~\cite{card2020with} for reliable Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) in natural language processing and risks violating the asymptotic cell expectations of McNemar's test ($b + c \ge 20$). In contrast, allocating 52 validation pairs and 53 test pairs satisfies both the MDE statistical power criteria and the non-parametric hypothesis testing assumptions while retaining 70\% (245 pairs) for robust few-shot Cross-Encoder fine-tuning~\cite{vabalas2019machine}.

    \subsection{Asymmetric Pairing and Hypothesis Formulation}
    \label{sec:span_pairing}
    To construct a representative ground truth benchmark, candidate statutory premises must reflect the full breadth of Philippine national law. During the exploratory phase of this research, the proponents conducted preliminary machine learning experiments on the national statutes, uncovering severe class imbalances and repetitive drafting formulas across historical eras. These exploratory findings directly informed the consolidated topic modeling architecture detailed in Section~\ref{sec:domain_categorization}, which organizes the corpus into eight validated Macro Legal Domains such as local governance, public health, and taxation. The proponents then sampled national statutory sections across these eight domains to establish the fixed premises for the evaluation benchmark. To evaluate multi-sentence legal commands without breaking internal qualifications, the pipeline uses Asymmetric Span-Level Pairing, coupling each statutory premise with a multi-sentence ordinance hypothesis block~\cite{yin2021docnli}. This asymmetric structure ($1\text{ premise vs } N\text{ hypothesis sentences}$) ensures that local exceptions, cross-references, and penalty conditions are evaluated together against the national law~\cite{koreeda2021contract}.

    To make the evaluation as realistic as possible, the drafted test sentences closely mirror the writing style of Davao City ordinances. The proponents analyzed local laws enacted between 2021 and 2025 to identify standard drafting phrases, administrative office names, and structural penalty clauses following the official drafting conventions of the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG)~\cite{dilg2022drafting}. Each drafted clause uses standard enacting syntax, such as: ``\textit{SECTION X. [TITLE]. --- It shall be unlawful for any person, natural or juridical, within the territorial jurisdiction of Davao City, to...}'' The draft sentences also name real local enforcement bodies, such as the City Transport and Traffic Management Office (CTTMO), the City Health Office (CHO), the City Environment and Natural Resources Office (CENRO), and the Business Bureau of Davao City. Operational units such as the Anti-Vices Task Force (AVTF) and the Davao City Police Office (DCPO) are also included to reflect genuine local enforcement.

    The synthetic ordinance clauses are also linked to real administrative districts in Davao City. Draft rules specify geographic areas from urban centers like Poblacion and Talomo to suburban districts like Buhangin and Toril. Local offices such as the City Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Office (CDRRMO) and the City Social Welfare and Development Office (CSWDO) are included to match actual city administration. This realistic drafting prevents evaluators from reviewing artificial example sentences that differ from real legal texts. As a result, the 15 Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers evaluate draft provisions that look like real measures introduced on First Reading.

    The proponents deliberately utilized domain-grounded, authentic local template-derived hypothesis construction---grounded in existing Davao City ordinances (2021--2025) and DILG drafting manuals, and formally reviewed with thesis adviser Mr.~Adrian ``Ogs'' Ablazo---rather than unconstrained synthetic generation via large language models. Recent evaluations by Gururangan et al.~\cite{gururangan2018annotation} and Poliak et al.~\cite{poliak2018hypothesis} demonstrated that automated or crowd-generated NLI hypotheses frequently introduce severe ``annotation artifacts''---systematic lexical cues such as negation markers (``not'', ``never'') that allow neural models to cheat and predict contradictions without genuinely reading the statutory premise. In specialized legal domains, authoritative benchmarks such as SARA (tax law)~\cite{holzenberger2020a}, DocNLI~\cite{yin2021docnli}, and Clash-of-Leges~\cite{italiani2026clash} establish that rigorous evaluation requires domain-grounded hypotheses reflecting authentic legislative syntax, ensuring models are tested on genuine statutory reasoning rather than superficial generation heuristics.

    While the system uses multi-sentence spans to understand local context, it does not attempt cross-document multi-hop reasoning (detecting conflicts that appear only by combining two or more separate national laws). Resolving dependencies across multiple separate laws is an active research challenge in natural language inference. Current systems that attempt this require either complex knowledge graphs~\cite{lu2023multi} or very large generative language models~\cite{shen2025hopweaver}. Running those multi-premise models would exceed the consumer-grade hardware limits set for local government deployment. For this reason, the ground truth benchmark and the inference system focus strictly on single-premise evaluations.

    \subsection{Class Balancing and Adversarial Injection}
    \label{sec:class_balancing}
    In standard machine learning on tabular data, researchers often handle uneven classes using methods like the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)~\cite{chawla2002smote}. However, the proponents avoid continuous vector oversampling for this legal text pipeline. SMOTE creates artificial examples by interpolating between numerical feature vectors ($x_{\text{new}} = x_i + \lambda(x_{zi} - x_i)$). In high-dimensional language models representing discrete words, averaging or blending token vectors creates artificial representations that confuse attention layers and carry no real legal meaning. Simple word replacement also fails with legal texts because statutory words have exact legal definitions; for example, swapping mandatory words like ``shall'' with permissive words like ``may'' fundamentally changes a legal duty.

    To build a balanced, reliable Ground Truth dataset, the researchers combined historical legal records with expert-guided text adjustments. First, the researchers gathered documented legal clashes from local government records, including mayoral veto messages, repealed ordinances, and explicit statutory amendments~\cite{italiani2026clash}. Second, following the evaluation methods of Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract} and Nie et al.~\cite{nie2020anli}, legal annotators introduced conflicting rules into real statutory provisions. Annotators reversed permissions, changed numerical zoning limits, and added conflicting penalties into ordinance clauses. This expert-guided process produced an exact balanced distribution across the Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction classes in the 350 Ground Truth pairs (~117 pairs per class), preventing class imbalance without creating unnatural legal phrasing.

Furthermore, the 30\% Tier~1, 40\% Tier~2, and 30\% Tier~3 quota follows the adversarial challenge partitioning established in benchmark engineering literature, such as SQuAD~2.0~\cite{rajpurkar2018know}, FEVER~\cite{thorne2018fever}, and ANLI~\cite{nie2020anli}. These foundational benchmarks establish that maintaining an adversarial challenge quota of 30\% to 40\% is optimal to prevent models from exploiting superficial lexical artifacts while preserving sufficient representation of standard rule-based compliance. Under established Supreme Court rulings, a local council cannot enact indirect prohibitions that take away rights protected by national statutes~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce, scp2005manilavlaguio}. To enforce this distinction during testing, the proponents verified that all 104 Tier~3 draft sentences contain zero numbers or monetary amounts. Without explicit numerical clues, the Cross-Encoder must evaluate the underlying legal meaning of the text, such as recognizing when an anti-vagrancy draft detains minors in violation of Republic Act No.~9344. This design ensures that the benchmark provides an authentic diagnostic test of semantic preemption for the subsequent analysis in Chapter~4.

    \subsection{Expert Legal Annotation Panel}
    \label{sec:annotation_panel}
    Classifying the sampled text pairs requires domain-specific legal training to ensure authentic and reliable labels. The Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod currently comprises 24 elected councilors across the city's three legislative districts, supported by a professional staff of approximately 48 active legal researchers~\cite{cortez2025leadership}. In the initial administrative correspondence transmitted to the Sangguniang Panlungsod Secretariat alongside the Legal Annotation Guidebook, the proponents formally requested a cohort of 15 volunteer legal researchers. Unlike general NLP tasks that rely on open crowdsourcing, legal natural language inference requires formal statutory interpretation skills to prevent superficial keyword matching~\cite{snow2008cheap, zheng2021when}. The recruited legal researchers review local ordinances as their regular professional duty, providing the necessary institutional competence for building a dependable gold standard~\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue}.

    To establish an objective and transparent panel structure, the proponents gathered baseline qualifications during the initial intake communication. The intake request systematically recorded two key criteria from each volunteer: highest educational attainment and total years of professional experience in legislative drafting. Using these reported credentials, the 15 researchers were organized into five balanced sub-panels of three members each ($k = 3$). Within each three-member sub-panel, the evaluator possessing the highest educational attainment and the longest drafting tenure was designated as that panel's Senior Annotator. The proponents then communicated these panel assignments and Senior Annotator designations in a follow-up confirmation email to establish operational readiness before annotation began.

    Following the consensus guidelines adapted from Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing}, a label is accepted into the final Ground Truth if at least two of the three reviewers in a sub-panel agree. In cases where all three reviewers select different categories, resulting in a three-way split with no majority, the pair is referred to that panel's designated Senior Annotator for binding qualitative adjudication~\cite{gao2022hierarchical, artstein2008inter}. The Senior Annotator reviews the pair against the standardized codebook to resolve whether the split arose from statutory ambiguity or an overlooked proviso. This hierarchical adjudication step ensures that every item in the 350-pair benchmark reaches an authoritative label while preserving the initial independent ratings for calculating Fleiss' Kappa ($\kappa$). Maintaining this structured division between initial independent scoring and expert tie-breaking safeguards both statistical validity and qualitative consistency across the dataset.

    To recognize the time and professional expertise dedicated by the participating Sangguniang Panlungsod legal researchers, evaluators who complete their assigned annotation batches are awarded an official Certificate of Contribution. This certificate serves as a formal token of appreciation and institutional documentation acknowledging their voluntary advisory assistance in validating the statutory benchmark. Each certificate is endorsed by the academic department leadership, thesis adviser, and student proponents to confer formal institutional validity. Providing this acknowledgment establishes a collaborative partnership between the university research team and the local legislative staff, ensuring appropriate recognition for the legal professionals who support the research.

    \subsection{Annotation Workload Allocation}
    \label{sec:batched_annotation}
    To prevent reviewer fatigue while keeping the results statistically sound, this study adapts the batched review framework developed by Ablazo~\cite{ablazo2019designing} alongside balanced incomplete block design principles for multi-rater NLP annotation~\cite{bowman2015snli, mchugh2012interrater}. In that earlier study, each item required three independent evaluations to determine a majority label. Managing a collection of 12,054 short texts required over 36,000 individual reviews. By dividing this work among 250 students, the workload was reduced to about 145 items per person. This demonstrated how batched distribution can maintain a three-vote overlap without placing an unrealistic burden on reviewers.
    
    This study applies the same mathematical allocation to the legal domain. Because reviewing multi-sentence legal provisions requires much more concentration than reading short messages, the dataset is smaller, but the distribution logic is identical. The workload for the legal reviewers is calculated as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Annotator Workload Allocation Formulation:}
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    N &= 350 \text{ premise-hypothesis pairs} \\
    k &= 3 \text{ independent votes per pair} \\
    P &= 15 \text{ legal annotators} \\[6pt]
    W &= N \times k = 350 \times 3 = 1,050 \text{ total annotations} \\[6pt]
    \text{Work per person} &= \frac{W}{P} = \frac{1,050}{15} = \mathbf{70 \text{ pairs per annotator}}
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:workload_allocation}
    \eqcaption{Annotator Workload Allocation Formulation}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $N$ represents the benchmark size, $k$ is the multi-rater replication factor, $P$ is the annotator pool size, and $W$ denotes total evaluations.

    Equation~\ref{eq:workload_allocation} details the mathematical derivation of the annotator workload distribution, dividing the total required annotations $W$ among the pool of $P = 15$ legal researchers. With a benchmark size of $N = 350$ premise-hypothesis pairs evaluated under a three-rater replication factor ($k = 3$), the protocol requires a total of $W = 1,050$ independent legal judgments. The formulation allocates this workload into five disjoint 70-pair sets, assigning exactly one set of 70 pairs to each three-member sub-panel. Methodologically, capping individual reviewer workload at 70 pairs prevents cognitive fatigue and attention degradation during complex legal evaluation. This batched structure maintains full multi-rater overlap for Fleiss' Kappa reliability calculations while ensuring that no single annotator is overburdened.
    
    To organize this workflow, the dataset is divided into five equal sets of 70 pairs each. The 15 researchers are grouped into five sub-panels of three members, with each sub-panel reviewing one 70-pair set. This arrangement ensures that every pair receives three independent reviews while keeping each reviewer's workload manageable.

    To reduce fatigue during the review process, the proponents provided structured reference materials. Reviewing statutory pairs requires significant effort, as reviewers must compare detailed legal clauses against local drafts. Evaluators received spreadsheets with complete statutory text to ensure they did not have to read isolated sub-clauses out of context. Each reviewer also received a one-page summary sheet explaining the \textit{Magtajas} decision steps and statutory penalty limits. These reference guides helped reviewers work consistently across their 70-pair evaluation sets.

    \subsection{Inter-Rater Reliability}
    \label{sec:inter_rater_reliability}
    To measure the consistency of the Ground Truth labels, the proponents calculate Fleiss' Kappa ($\kappa$). While raw percentage agreement shows how often reviewers picked the same label, it does not account for agreement that occurs by chance. Fleiss' Kappa isolates genuine agreement by mathematically subtracting the probability of random guessing from the final score.
    
    This metric is used instead of Cohen's Kappa because Cohen's formula is limited to exactly two raters, whereas this study assigns three reviewers to each pair. The resulting score is interpreted using the guidelines from Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977measurement}, where a $\kappa$ score between 0.61 and 0.80 indicates ``Substantial Agreement.''

    Using this target in natural language inference is supported by Bowman et al.~\cite{bowman2015snli} in their work on the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) benchmark. Evaluating text relationships involves human judgment; in their study, Bowman et al. reached an overall Fleiss' Kappa of 0.70 (scoring 0.77 for Contradiction, 0.72 for Entailment, and 0.60 for Neutral). They showed that achieving very high agreement ($> 0.80$) in language inference is rare and usually happens only with overly simple test sentences. Because Philippine local ordinances are more complex than general English sentences, expecting agreement higher than 0.70 is unrealistic. Therefore, this study targets a minimum $\kappa$ threshold of 0.61, which confirms substantial agreement among the legal reviewers.

    \section{Pipeline Architecture}
    \label{sec:pipeline_architecture}
    The live conflict detection workflow uses a two-stage retrieve-then-entail architecture designed to combine rapid statutory retrieval with precise logical preemption analysis. The system divides the conflict detection process into two sequential operational phases: coarse candidate retrieval across the dual statutory corpus, and fine natural language inference over paired provisions.

    \subsection{Stage 1 Hybrid Retrieval Pipeline}
    \label{sec:stage1_hybrid_pipeline}
    The first operational phase begins when a user submits a draft local ordinance for review. The system first runs the BM25 algorithm to quickly search the statutory database for exact lexical matches. This sparse search captures specific statutory citations, agency acronyms, and numerical penalty limits. At the same time, a dense Bi-Encoder maps the draft provision and the statutes into a continuous semantic space, measuring cosine distance to capture legal meaning that keyword matching overlooks~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Rather than relying on fragile score normalization, the pipeline combines the two retrievers using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) to generate an ordered shortlist of the top-$k$ candidate provisions~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal}:

    \noindent\textit{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) Scoring:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{1}{k_{\text{rrf}} + \text{rank}_m(d)}
        \label{eq:reciprocal_rank_fusion}
        \eqcaption{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) Scoring}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{BM25}, \text{Dense}\}$ represents the candidate retrieval models, $\text{rank}_m(d)$ denotes the ordinal position of document $d$ produced by model $m$, and $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$ is the smoothing constant.

    Equation~\ref{eq:reciprocal_rank_fusion} defines the Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) formula, combining the ranked search results from BM25 and the dense Bi-Encoder into a single list. RRF avoids having to normalize raw scores, since BM25 scores vary widely based on document length while cosine similarities stay between $-1$ and $1$. By focusing on rank positions with a smoothing parameter ($k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$), the formula gives priority to provisions that score well in both keyword and semantic searches. This ensures that the candidate shortlist includes both exact citation matches and conceptually related laws, providing a balanced pool of candidates for Stage~2 inference.

    Standard linear score interpolation ($\alpha S_{\text{BM25}} + (1-\alpha) S_{\text{Dense}}$) requires min-max normalization across unbounded BM25 distributions, which fluctuates wildly across diverse queries. In contrast, Reciprocal Rank Fusion operates entirely on ordinal rankings, making the combination robust against differences in score scales. The resulting fused score ranks provisions that demonstrate both lexical precision and semantic relevance at the top of the candidate pool. Listing~\ref{lst:stage1_hybrid_retrieval} presents the procedural logic of the dual-stream hybrid retrieval and rank fusion pipeline.

    \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Pseudocode of the Stage 1 Reciprocal Rank Fusion Hybrid Provision Retrieval Pipeline}, label={lst:stage1_hybrid_retrieval}]
def retrieve_top_k_candidates(draft_section, statutory_corpus, k=50, k_rrf=60, beta=1.20):
    # 1. Retrieve top-100 candidates from sparse BM25 and dense Bi-Encoder
    bm25_results = search_bm25(corpus=statutory_corpus, query=draft_section, top_n=100)
    dense_results = search_dense(corpus=statutory_corpus, query=draft_section, top_n=100)

    # 2. Compute Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) scores across candidate pool
    candidate_pool = set(bm25_results.keys()) | set(dense_results.keys())
    fused_scores = {}
    for prov_id in candidate_pool:
        rank_bm25 = bm25_results.get_rank(prov_id, default=101)
        rank_dense = dense_results.get_rank(prov_id, default=101)
        rrf_score = (1.0 / (k_rrf + rank_bm25)) + (1.0 / (k_rrf + rank_dense))
        
        # Apply hierarchy safeguard: boost primary national Republic Acts by 20%
        if prov_id.startswith("RA_") or is_primary_act(prov_id):
            rrf_score *= beta
        fused_scores[prov_id] = rrf_score

    # 3. Sort candidates by fused score and return top-k shortlist
    return sorted(fused_scores.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:k]
    \end{lstlisting}

    Listing~\ref{lst:stage1_hybrid_retrieval} outlines the Python implementation of the Stage~1 hybrid retrieval pipeline, combining BM25 keyword search, dense Bi-Encoder retrieval, and Reciprocal Rank Fusion with hierarchy boosting. This code demonstrates how the system narrows down more than 27,000 statutory provisions to a focused shortlist of top-$k$ candidates ($k = 50$). In this routine, the draft section is searched independently across sparse and dense indices to retrieve the top 100 candidates from each method. The algorithm then iterates across all retrieved provisions, calculates the RRF score using Equation~\ref{eq:reciprocal_rank_fusion}, and applies a 20\% score boost ($\beta = 1.20$) to primary national statutes (Republic Acts) to ensure important laws are not buried under administrative issuances. Sorting the combined scores yields a compact, high-precision candidate shortlist that delivers relevant legal context to Stage~2 inference in under 50 milliseconds.

    Separating Stage~1 retrieval from Stage~2 inference is essential when running on standard office hardware. Feeding full-length statutes directly into a Cross-Encoder requires quadratic computation ($O((L_{\text{ord}} + L_{\text{stat}})^2)$), which quickly exhausts local memory on long legislative texts. Full-text inputs also dilute model attention, burying specific penalty clauses under thousands of routine procedural words. The hybrid retriever in Stage~1 performs a rapid coarse-to-fine filter ($25,432 \rightarrow 50 \rightarrow 1$), removing over 99.8\% of unrelated laws in milliseconds. This separation allows the Cross-Encoder to focus its attention entirely on high-probability candidate provisions.

    \subsection{Stage 2 Conflict Detection Pipeline}
    \label{sec:stage2_nli_pipeline}
    After receiving the top-$k$ candidate shortlist from Stage~1, the pipeline moves to logical evaluation using the Natural Language Inference (NLI) module. The system formats an input sequence for each candidate provision, pairing the retrieved national law (the premise) with the draft ordinance (the hypothesis). To guide the model's attention, the pipeline places a $[CLS]$ token at the start of the sequence and a $[SEP]$ token between the two texts. This structure helps the model distinguish the existing law from the proposed draft. The combined text is then processed by the selected Cross-Encoder model. The model's attention mechanism compares every word in the premise against every word in the hypothesis to evaluate their relationship. A classification layer outputs calibrated softmax probabilities labeling the relationship as Entailment, Contradiction, or Neutral. If the contradiction probability crosses the designated threshold, the Explainable AI (XAI) module highlights the conflicting text for user review.

    \subsection{Baseline Selection}
    \label{sec:baseline_selection}
    To evaluate whether the proposed two-stage retrieve-then-entail architecture achieves meaningful empirical advances over standard approaches, the proponents established formal baselines for both operational stages grounded in recent legal informatics literature.

    For coarse statutory retrieval, the pipeline is benchmarked against pure Okapi BM25 lexical matching adapted from Team AIIR~\cite{wu2025aiir} in the COLIEE 2025 competition. BM25 serves as the canonical baseline for statutory information retrieval (COLIEE Task~3) because legislative drafting frequently relies on explicit section citations, formal agency acronyms, and verbatim penalty amounts. Choosing BM25 as the Stage~1 baseline provides an essential reference point to determine whether semantic dense vector representations and Reciprocal Rank Fusion genuinely improve candidate recall, especially across paraphrased or indirect preemption queries where exact keyword matching is known to fail.

    For fine natural language inference, the pipeline establishes a dual-baseline framework combining an unadapted task baseline with a canonical supervised foundation. Following the legal textual entailment baseline established by Rabelo et al.~\cite{rabelo2022coliee} for COLIEE Task~4, an unadapted zero-shot Cross-Encoder tests whether off-the-shelf general-domain or multi-task language models can detect statutory preemption without local domain adaptation. This baseline establishes the empirical lower bound and directly quantifies the severity of affirmative bias. Complementing this, the proponents establish the standard \texttt{bert-base-uncased} model (110M parameters) developed by Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019bert} as the canonical supervised baseline. Fine-tuned under identical training conditions on the curated statutory pairs, standard BERT isolates the exact performance premium contributed by specialized attention mechanisms, legal domain pretraining, and warm NLI initialization over traditional masked language modeling.

        \item \textbf{Covering Diverse Model Architectures:} The top five candidates represent different model designs, including pre-aligned NLI reasoners (\texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI} and \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-base-NLI}), modern long-context models (\texttt{ModernBERT-large}), unaligned general encoders (cold \texttt{deberta-v3-base}), and legal domain pretraining (\texttt{PoL-BERT-Large}).

    \subsection{Overfitting Controls and Calibration}
    \label{sec:overfitting_controls}
    Because fine-tuning deep transformer models in a few-shot setting ($N_{\text{train}} = 245$) can lead to memorizing training phrasing, the researchers use several regularization techniques. First, model optimization uses the AdamW algorithm with weight decay ($\lambda = 0.01$), as developed by Loshchilov and Hutter~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}. This weight decay keeps network parameters small and prevents individual weights from growing disproportionately large. Second, dropout layers with a rate of $p = 0.1$ are applied across attention layers and the classification head~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}. By turning off a random subset of neurons during training passes, dropout encourages the model to learn broader semantic features rather than depending on specific word patterns.

    Third, training uses early stopping based on validation results~\cite{prechelt1998early}. The system measures validation loss and the validation Macro F1-Score after every training epoch on the 52-pair validation set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$). If the validation Macro F1-Score does not improve over four consecutive checks ($\text{patience} = 4$), training stops automatically. The system then restores the model checkpoint that achieved the lowest validation loss, preventing the model from fitting too closely to the training data. Finally, the learning rate warms up linearly over the first 10\% of training steps before decreasing steadily, which helps keep gradient updates stable during the initial phase of fine-tuning.

    Recent empirical findings from the COLIEE 2026 legal textual entailment benchmarks (Task~4) demonstrated that neural language models frequently exhibit affirmative bias, defaulting toward entailment or neutral classifications when confronted with complex statutory syntax~\cite{alshehri2026cross, tran2026enhancing}. In local legislative review, failing to detect an authentic conflict permits an invalid ordinance to proceed to enactment. To counter this affirmative bias, the proponents calibrate the decision threshold for the contradiction class using validation holdout curves. Rather than assuming an uncalibrated argmax decision rule, the threshold is adjusted to maximize the Contradiction F1-Score while maintaining high specificity. This calibration ensures that genuine preemption risks are surfaced to legislative staff rather than being suppressed by ambiguous phrasing.

    \section{Evaluation Metrics}
    \label{sec:evaluation_metrics}
    Evaluating the two-stage pipeline requires separate metrics for each operational phase. The coarse retrieval phase in Stage~1 prioritizes candidate recall over final classification, while the inference phase in Stage~2 requires high precision to avoid false conflict alerts.

    \subsection{Stage 1 Retrieval Metrics}
    \label{sec:stage1_metrics}
    Measuring search performance in Stage~1 requires ranking metrics rather than standard classification accuracy~\cite{manning2008introduction}. Because the purpose of the coarse retriever is to find candidate laws rather than make the final decision, standard accuracy does not reflect search quality. If the relevant national statute is left out of the top-$k$ shortlist, the downstream Cross-Encoder cannot detect the conflict. For this reason, the retrieval stage is evaluated primarily using Recall at rank $k$ ($\text{Recall@}k$) at thresholds of $k \in \{10, 30, 50\}$. This metric measures the percentage of test queries where the correct national law appears within the candidate shortlist:

    \noindent\textit{Recall at Rank $k$ ($\text{Recall@}k$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Recall@}k = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \mathbf{1}\left(\text{rank}(d_q^*) \le k\right)
        \label{eq:recall_at_k}
        \eqcaption{Recall at Rank $k$ ($\text{Recall@}k$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $|Q|$ is the total number of test queries, $d_q^*$ is the correct national statute paired with query $q$, and $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is an indicator function that equals one when the target law ranks within the top $k$ candidates.

    Complementing candidate recall, Mean Reciprocal Rank at rank $k$ ($\text{MRR@}k$) evaluates how early the target statute appears within the candidate ranking~\cite{manning2008introduction}:

    \noindent\textit{Mean Reciprocal Rank at Rank $k$ ($\text{MRR@}k$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MRR@}k = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{\text{rank}(d_q^*)} \cdot \mathbf{1}\left(\text{rank}(d_q^*) \le k\right)
        \label{eq:mrr_at_k}
        \eqcaption{Mean Reciprocal Rank at Rank $k$ ($\text{MRR@}k$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $d_q^*$ is the correct national statute retrieved for query $q$, with $1/\text{rank}(d_q^*)$ representing the reciprocal rank score.

    Equations~\ref{eq:recall_at_k} and~\ref{eq:mrr_at_k} define the ranking metrics used to test Stage~1 retrieval across the 25,432 national statutes. Because the goal of coarse retrieval is to create a top-$k$ shortlist ($k \in \{10, 30, 50\}$) rather than making final decisions, standard accuracy is not suitable. $\text{Recall@}k$ measures the percentage of test queries where the correct statute appears in the top-$k$ shortlist, while $\text{MRR@}k$ measures how close to the top that statute ranks. High Recall@50 ensures that Stage~2 inference receives the governing statute needed to detect a conflict, while higher MRR places the most relevant law near the top of the list for legal researchers to review. These two metrics guide the selection of the best retrieval models in Chapter~4.

    In this formula, a target statute retrieved at rank one receives a reciprocal score of 1.0, while a statute retrieved at rank ten receives 0.10. The 350 curated premise-hypothesis pairs serve as the query benchmark for testing Stage~1. By using the 350 draft clauses as queries against the 25,432-statute collection, the proponents measure how often candidate retrieval models locate the paired statutory provision. Calculating $\text{Recall@}10$, $\text{Recall@}30$, and $\text{Recall@}50$ confirms whether the coarse retriever supplies a dependable candidate pool for subsequent natural language inference.

    \subsection{Stage 2 Inference Metrics}
    \label{sec:stage2_metrics}
    The Stage~2 Cross-Encoder evaluates candidate statutory pairs across three discrete logical categories: $\mathcal{C} = \{\text{Entailment}, \text{Neutral}, \text{Contradiction}\}$. Following standard evaluation conventions in information extraction and natural language processing~\cite{chinchor1992muc4, vanrijsbergen1979information}, the baseline inference performance metrics are computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Class-Specific Precision, Recall, and $F_1$-Score:}
    \begin{equation}
        P_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c}, \quad R_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c}, \quad F_{1,c} = \frac{2 \cdot P_c \cdot R_c}{P_c + R_c}
        \label{eq:class_metrics}
        \eqcaption{Class-Specific Precision, Recall, and $F_1$-Score}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $TP_c$, $FP_c$, and $FN_c$ represent the true positive, false positive, and false negative classifications for category $c$, respectively. Precision reflects how reliably the model avoids false alarms, while Recall measures the proportion of genuine legal relationships successfully detected.

    \noindent\textit{Macro-Averaged $F_1$-Score Across Logical Classes:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Macro } F_1 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|} \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} F_{1,c}
        \label{eq:macro_f1}
        \eqcaption{Macro-Averaged $F_1$-Score Across Logical Classes}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $|\mathcal{C}| = 3$ ensures that performance across all three categories is evaluated equally without allowing dominant neutral pairs to skew the outcome.

    Equations~\ref{eq:class_metrics} and~\ref{eq:macro_f1} define the performance metrics for Stage~2 inference across the three NLI categories: Entailment, Neutral, and Contradiction ($|\mathcal{C}| = 3$). Precision ($P_c$) measures how well the model avoids false alarms, Recall ($R_c$) measures how many actual conflicts or relationships are caught, and the $F_1$-score balances both. Macro $F_1$ averages these scores equally across all three classes ($1/3$ each), ensuring that a model cannot achieve an artificially high score simply by guessing the most common class. This provides a balanced measure for comparing candidate models during screening.

    In statutory conflict detection, classification errors carry fundamentally unequal operational costs~\cite{elkan2001foundations, fawcett2006roc}. A false negative, where the system fails to flag an unconstitutional local clause, permits an invalid ordinance to be enacted. This failure triggers costly litigation and statutory invalidation under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine~\cite{scp1994magtajasvpryce}. In contrast, a false positive, where the system flags a compliant draft clause, merely directs a human legislative researcher to examine the flagged text on the review dashboard, requiring only seconds of reviewer time. Standard classifiers default to a symmetric decision threshold of $P(\text{Contradiction}) \ge 0.50$, which assumes that false alarms and missed conflicts cause identical harm. In foundational cost-sensitive learning theory, Elkan~\cite{elkan2001foundations} proved that when false negative costs exceed false positive costs ($C(FN) > C(FP)$), setting the decision boundary at 0.50 is mathematically suboptimal and produces an unsafe number of missed conflicts~\cite{fawcett2006roc, koreeda2021contract}.

    To address this cost asymmetry, the proponents adapt the generalized $F_\beta$ formulation of Van Rijsbergen~\cite{vanrijsbergen1979information} and Chinchor~\cite{chinchor1992muc4} with $\beta = 2$, implementing a validation-tuned decision boundary $\tau_{\text{conflict}}$ for contradiction alerts. Rather than using a fixed 0.50 cutoff, the pipeline sweeps candidate thresholds across $\tau \in [0.25, 0.50]$ in increments of $\Delta \tau = 0.01$ exclusively on the validation set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$, $N = 52$) following empirical decision calibration principles~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. The cost-sensitive contradiction metrics are computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Cost-Sensitive Contradiction $F_2$-Score:}
    \begin{equation}
        F_{2, \text{conflict}} = \frac{5 \cdot P_{\text{conflict}} \cdot R_{\text{conflict}}}{4 \cdot P_{\text{conflict}} + R_{\text{conflict}}}
        \label{eq:f2_conflict}
        \eqcaption{Cost-Sensitive Contradiction $F_2$-Score}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $P_{\text{conflict}}$ and $R_{\text{conflict}}$ denote Precision and Recall on the Contradiction class, weighting recall twice as heavily as precision ($\beta = 2$) to aggressively capture potential legal conflicts.

    \noindent\textit{Optimal Contradiction Decision Threshold Calibration ($\tau^*$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \tau^* = \arg\max_{\tau \in [0.25, 0.50]} F_{2, \text{conflict}}(\tau; \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}})
        \label{eq:optimal_threshold}
        \eqcaption{Optimal Contradiction Decision Threshold Calibration ($\tau^*$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$ denotes the 52 validation pairs used to sweep candidate decision thresholds $\tau \in [0.25, 0.50]$ while enforcing an empirical precision floor of $P_{\text{conflict}} \ge 0.70$.

    Equations~\ref{eq:f2_conflict} and~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold} define the cost-sensitive evaluation metric and decision threshold calibration for conflict alerts. This design reflects a practical reality: missing a real conflict (a false negative) allows an invalid ordinance to pass, which can lead to legal challenges, whereas a false alarm (a false positive) is quickly dismissed by a legal researcher. In Equation~\ref{eq:f2_conflict}, setting $\beta = 2$ weights recall twice as heavily as precision, prioritizing catching potential conflicts. Equation~\ref{eq:optimal_threshold} searches for the best decision threshold $\tau^*$ between $0.25$ and $0.50$ on the 52 validation pairs ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$), while keeping precision at or above $0.70$. This tuning helps the system flag genuine conflicts reliably without overwhelming researchers with too many false alarms.

    During live inference, a candidate provision triggers a formal conflict alert whenever its softmax contradiction probability satisfies $P(\text{Contradiction} \mid p_i, h) \ge \tau^*$.

    When evaluating complete multi-section ordinances during live system execution, Stage~1 retains its full safety buffer of $k = 50$ candidates for each substantive section ($S_i$). To prevent runtime delays when scoring up to 500 total candidate pairs per document, the Stage~2 inference engine employs vectorized mini-batch collation ($B = 16$). Batching evaluates candidate provisions in parallel tensor forward passes rather than sequential single-item loops, keeping inference runtime under five seconds on GPU hosts and under thirty seconds on standard LGU processors. To consolidate the resulting candidate scores into a single decision for each section, adapting multi-instance max-pooling principles~\cite{anand2020explainable}, the aggregation metric is computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Section-Level Max-Pooled Conflict Probability:}
    \begin{equation}
        P(\text{Conflict} \mid S_i) = \max_{j \in [1, k]} P(\text{Contradiction} \mid P_j, S_i)
        \label{eq:section_conflict_prob}
        \eqcaption{Section-Level Max-Pooled Conflict Probability}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $P_j$ ranges over all $k = 50$ candidate statutory provisions retrieved for draft section $S_i$.

    Equation~\ref{eq:section_conflict_prob} calculates the overall conflict score for a draft section ($S_i$) by taking the highest contradiction probability among all $k = 50$ retrieved candidate laws. This approach, known as max-pooling, is used during full-document review where each draft clause is compared against multiple candidates. Taking the highest score ensures that if a draft section clearly conflicts with even one national law, an alert is triggered, even if the other 49 retrieved candidates are neutral or unrelated. This directs the reviewer's attention to the specific statute causing the conflict, preventing an actual violation from being hidden by non-conflicting candidates.

    A section triggers an operative conflict warning if and only if $P(\text{Conflict} \mid S_i) \ge \tau^*$, with the system highlighting the specific governing candidate provision $P_j^* = \arg\max_{j} P(\text{Contradiction} \mid P_j, S_i)$ that produced the highest conflict probability. This max-pooling aggregation ensures that an authentic statutory clash ranked lower in the candidate shortlist is not obscured by neutral or harmonious provisions appearing at higher retrieval ranks. Listing~\ref{lst:stage2_inference_aggregation} outlines the operational inference and conflict aggregation procedure.

    \begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Pseudocode of the Stage 2 Cost-Sensitive Cross-Encoder Inference and Section-Level Conflict Aggregation}, label={lst:stage2_inference_aggregation}]
def detect_section_conflicts(draft_section, candidate_provisions, model, threshold=0.35, batch_size=16):
    """
    Stage 2 Cost-Sensitive Cross-Encoder Inference:
    Scores candidate law pairs and flags conflicts using max-pooling.
    """
    # 1. Pair each candidate statutory provision (premise) with the draft section (hypothesis)
    pairs = [(provision.text, draft_section.text) for provision in candidate_provisions]

    # 2. Run mini-batch inference through the fine-tuned Cross-Encoder
    contradiction_scores = []
    for i in range(0, len(pairs), batch_size):
        batch = pairs[i : i + batch_size]
        # Get softmax probabilities across [Entailment, Neutral, Contradiction]
        probabilities = model.predict_probabilities(batch)
        # Extract contradiction class probability (index 2)
        batch_contradictions = [p["contradiction"] for p in probabilities]
        contradiction_scores.extend(batch_contradictions)

    # 3. Find candidate provision with highest contradiction score (Max-Pooling)
    peak_score = max(contradiction_scores)
    peak_index = contradiction_scores.index(peak_score)
    governing_provision = candidate_provisions[peak_index]

    # 4. Apply cost-sensitive decision threshold
    has_conflict = peak_score >= threshold

    return {
        "alert": has_conflict,
        "conflict_probability": peak_score,
        "conflicting_law": governing_provision.citation,
        "conflicting_text": governing_provision.text
    }
    \end{lstlisting}

    Listing~\ref{lst:stage2_inference_aggregation} outlines the Python implementation of the Stage~2 Cross-Encoder inference and section-level conflict aggregation routine. This algorithm is provided to illustrate the mini-batch tensor processing and cost-sensitive thresholding that transforms candidate provision pairs into actionable preemption alerts. In the procedure, the candidate statutory provisions and draft section are paired into premise-hypothesis sequences and evaluated in vectorized mini-batches ($B = 16$) through the fine-tuned model. The function extracts softmax contradiction probabilities, identifies the peak score via max-pooling according to Equation~\ref{eq:section_conflict_prob}, and checks whether this peak crosses the calibrated decision threshold $\tau^*$. If an alert is triggered, the routine returns the conflicting statutory citation, text, and model confidence, delivering immediate diagnostic transparency to the First Reading review dashboard.

    \subsection{Statistical Hypothesis Testing}
    \label{sec:statistical_comparisons}
    To evaluate whether performance disparities between candidate Cross-Encoder models represent statistically significant differences rather than random sampling fluctuations, the proponents employ formal non-parametric hypothesis tests recommended by Dem{\v{s}}ar~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Traditional paired $t$-tests assume normally distributed classification errors, an assumption routinely violated by discrete NLP accuracy and F1 scores on small test sets~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Therefore, this study adopts McNemar's Test for pairwise model comparisons and the Friedman Test for multi-model ranking across legal domains.

    When comparing the classification accuracy of two competing Stage~2 models (such as fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3 versus fine-tuned ModernBERT) evaluated on the identical 53-item test set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$), the proponents employ McNemar's test for pairwise significance~\cite{mcnemar1947note}. The test constructs a $2 \times 2$ contingency table analyzing discordant predictions, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_contingency}. In this contingency framework, cell $b$ represents items correctly classified by Model~A but misclassified by Model~B, while cell $c$ represents items correctly classified by Model~B but misclassified by Model~A. Under the null hypothesis $H_0: p_b = p_c$, the two models have equivalent error rates.

    Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_contingency} presents the $2 \times 2$ contingency matrix of discordant and concordant predictions utilized by McNemar's test for pairwise model significance testing. This matrix structure is incorporated to isolate the exact instances where two competing Cross-Encoder architectures disagree on the 53-item test split ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$). In the layout, cells $a$ and $d$ capture concordant outcomes where both models are simultaneously correct or incorrect, while off-diagonal cells $b$ and $c$ record discordant predictions where only Model~A or Model~B classifies the pair correctly. Under the null hypothesis, the two models share equivalent error rates ($p_b = p_c$). By evaluating only discordant pairs rather than raw overall accuracy, the test provides high statistical sensitivity to marginal algorithmic improvements while remaining robust against non-normal classification error distributions.

    Because the Stage~2 evaluation compares three candidate models ($k = 3$: MiniLM, DeBERTa-v3, ModernBERT), pairwise evaluation yields $m = \binom{3}{2} = 3$ distinct model comparisons. To prevent Type~I error inflation (false positive significance) across multiple tests, the proponents apply the standard Bonferroni correction~\cite{demsar2006statistical}, evaluating each pair against the adjusted threshold $\alpha' = \alpha / m = 0.05 / 3 \approx 0.0167$. 

    Furthermore, when expanding candidate model explorations to larger suites (e.g., $k \approx 10$ architectures and hyperparameter configurations), running exhaustive pairwise McNemar tests produces $\binom{k}{2} = 45$ comparisons, creating severe statistical conservatism under Bonferroni correction ($\alpha' = 0.05 / 45 \approx 0.0011$). To handle larger candidate pools without losing statistical power, the proponents follow Dem{\v{s}}ar's two-step testing procedure~\cite{demsar2006statistical}: first executing the omnibus Friedman test across all $k$ models simultaneously, and only upon rejecting the global null hypothesis ($p < 0.05$), conducting post-hoc comparisons comparing candidate models strictly against the top-performing control model ($k - 1$ comparisons rather than $\frac{k(k-1)}{2}$) or evaluating critical difference intervals via the Nemenyi test.

    For pairwise statistical significance, following the recommendations of Dietterich~\cite{dietterich1998approximate} for comparing supervised machine learning algorithms, the proponents employ McNemar's chi-squared test~\cite{mcnemar1947note} with Edwards' continuity correction~\cite{edwards1948note}. The test evaluates whether discordant classifications between Model~A and Model~B occur in equal proportions:

    \noindent\textit{McNemar's Chi-Squared Test with Edwards' Continuity Correction:}
    \begin{equation}
        \chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c} \sim \chi^2_1
        \label{eq:mcnemar_test}
        \eqcaption{McNemar's Chi-Squared Test with Edwards' Continuity Correction}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $b$ represents items correctly classified by Model~A but misclassified by Model~B, $c$ represents items correctly classified by Model~B but misclassified by Model~A, and the numerator incorporates Edwards' continuity correction ($-1$) for discrete cell frequencies.

    Equation~\ref{eq:mcnemar_test} defines McNemar's chi-squared test with Edwards' continuity correction for comparing paired predictions. The formula measures whether the number of times Model~A succeeds where Model~B fails ($b$) differs significantly from the reverse case ($c$). The minus-one correction accounts for discrete count data, and dividing by total disagreements ($b+c$) produces a test statistic that follows a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom. In practice, this test checks whether one model genuinely outperforms another on difficult borderline cases rather than by chance. If the resulting $p$-value falls below the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold ($\alpha' = 0.0167$), the null hypothesis is rejected, confirming that the accuracy difference between the two models is statistically significant.

    Beyond pairwise significance, assessing the consistency of candidate architectures across the eight Macro Legal Domains without making restrictive distributional assumptions requires non-parametric multi-model ranking. For this purpose, the proponents utilize the Friedman non-parametric two-way analysis of variance by ranks~\cite{friedman1937use, demsar2006statistical}. For $k$ candidate models evaluated across $N = 8$ distinct legal domains, each model is assigned a rank $r_i^j$ within domain $i$, where the best-performing model receives rank 1. The average rank of model $j$ across all domains is given by $R_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^j$. The omnibus rank statistic is computed as follows:

    \noindent\textit{Friedman Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks ($\chi_F^2$):}
    \begin{equation}
        \chi_F^2 = \frac{12N}{k(k+1)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^k R_j^2 - \frac{k(k+1)^2}{4}\right]
        \label{eq:friedman_test}
        \eqcaption{Friedman Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks ($\chi_F^2$)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $N = 8$ is the number of macro legal domains, $k$ is the number of candidate models, and $R_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^j$ is the average rank of model $j$ across all domains ($r_i^j$ being the rank of model $j$ in domain $i$).

    Equation~\ref{eq:friedman_test} computes the Friedman rank test statistic ($\chi_F^2$) to compare candidate models across multiple legal domains without assuming normal distributions. The formula uses the average ranks ($R_j$) of each model across the eight macro legal domains, comparing observed rank differences against what would be expected if all models performed equally. Under the null hypothesis that all models have similar performance across domains, $\chi_F^2$ follows a chi-squared distribution with $k - 1$ degrees of freedom. If the test rejects this null hypothesis ($p < 0.05$), it indicates meaningful performance differences across legal domains, allowing post-hoc tests such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Nemenyi comparisons to identify which models generalize best across different areas of law~\cite{demsar2006statistical}.

    \section{System-Level Evaluation}
    \label{sec:system_evaluation_framework}
    While isolated benchmarks measure retrieval recall and natural language inference accuracy separately, evaluating the live utility of the system requires testing both stages as an integrated pipeline. In real legislative drafting, an inference engine cannot detect a contradiction if the retrieval engine fails to supply the governing statute, and a retrieval engine is useless if the inference engine misclassifies the pair. Following the multi-stage system evaluation standards established in legal information extraction competitions~\cite{rabelo2022coliee, alshehri2026cross} and fact verification benchmarks~\cite{thorne2018fever}, the proponents implement a structured Three-Tier Evaluation Framework. This multi-tier framework connects controlled statistical benchmarking with full-document stress testing. Together, these three evaluation tiers systematically assess whether the complete pipeline can operate reliably within the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod.

    The first evaluation tier measures the combined algorithmic performance of Stage~1 retrieval and Stage~2 inference when executed sequentially without human intervention. Adapting the joint scoring formulation established in the FEVER fact verification framework by Thorne et al.~\cite{thorne2018fever}, the cascaded benchmark feeds only the local draft hypothesis clause $h_q$ from the 53 held-out test pairs into Stage~1 coarse retrieval. The retrieval engine searches across all 27,000+ national statutes and local ordinances to extract a candidate shortlist of the top-$k$ provisions ($\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$), which is then passed directly to Stage~2 for inference. A test item is scored as a Cascaded True Positive if and only if Stage~1 retrieves the correct governing statute $p_q^*$ within the top-$k$ shortlist and Stage~2 correctly classifies the pair $(p_q^*, h_q)$ as a Contradiction:

    \noindent\textit{End-to-End Pipeline Hit Indicator (FEVER Metric):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Hit}_{\text{casc}}(q) = \mathbf{1}\left(p_q^* \in \mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}\left(\hat{y}(p_q^*, h_q) = y_q^*\right)
        \label{eq:cascaded_hit}
        \eqcaption{End-to-End Pipeline Hit Indicator (FEVER Metric)}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $p_q^*$ is the target governing statute, $\mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$ is the candidate shortlist of top-$k$ provisions retrieved in Stage~1, and $\hat{y}(p_q^*, h_q) = y_q^*$ indicates whether Stage~2 correctly classifies the candidate pair as a contradiction.

    Equation~\ref{eq:cascaded_hit} defines the joint pipeline hit metric ($\text{Hit}_{\text{casc}}$) based on the end-to-end evaluation approach of Thorne et al.~\cite{thorne2018fever}. This metric counts a test item as a success only when both stages work correctly in sequence. Specifically, the formula multiplies two check conditions: whether Stage~1 retrieval includes the true governing statute ($p_q^*$) in its top-$k$ shortlist, and whether Stage~2 inference predicts the correct gold label ($y_q^*$). Combining retrieval recall and inference accuracy this way prevents a high score in one stage from hiding failures in the other. In real legislative workflows, successfully catching a conflict requires both finding the relevant statute and correctly identifying the contradiction.

    Adapting this joint metric from the FEVER fact verification framework~\cite{thorne2018fever} isolates whether an operational error stems from retrieval omissions ($p_q^* \notin \mathcal{K}_q^{(k)}$) or classification errors ($\hat{y} \neq y_q^*$), preventing either stage from masking pipeline failures.

    The second evaluation tier assesses how the complete coarse-to-fine pipeline processes full, multi-section legislative drafts rather than isolated sentence pairs. In local legislative practice, proposed ordinances contain extensive procedural text, including administrative definitions and standard effectivity clauses, which should not trigger conflict warnings. Adapting the behavioral perturbation and fault-injection principles formulated by Ribeiro et al.~\cite{ribeiro2020beyond} and Koreeda and Manning~\cite{koreeda2021contract}, the proponents construct synthetic and semi-synthetic ordinance drafts containing controlled legal faults. In each test document, the majority of sections remain compliant with superior law, while targeted operative provisions contain deliberate statutory violations, such as unauthorized regulatory mandates or excessive penalty amounts. The automated preprocessor chunks the submitted document into operative sections, auditing every section independently against the statutory archive.

    Evaluating full documents measures both the sensitivity of the pipeline to authentic legal defects and its ability to avoid false alarms on compliant text. Following standard diagnostic classification metrics~\cite{altman1994diagnostic, fawcett2006roc}, the proponents calculate Section-Level Sensitivity to measure the percentage of injected conflicts successfully flagged, paired with Section-Level Specificity to assess false-alarm resistance:

    \noindent\textit{Section-Level Specificity for Full-Document Stress Testing:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Specificity}_{\text{sec}} = \frac{TN_{\text{sec}}}{TN_{\text{sec}} + FP_{\text{sec}}}
        \label{eq:section_specificity}
        \eqcaption{Section-Level Specificity for Full-Document Stress Testing}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $TN_{\text{sec}}$ denotes compliant draft sections correctly passing without triggering a false alarm, and $FP_{\text{sec}}$ denotes compliant draft sections erroneously flagged as contradictory.

    Equation~\ref{eq:section_specificity} defines Section-Level Specificity ($\text{Specificity}_{\text{sec}}$) for the Tier~2 full-document test. This metric measures how well the pipeline avoids false alarms when checking long drafts that include standard administrative sections and boilerplate clauses. The formula calculates the percentage of compliant sections ($TN_{\text{sec}}$) that correctly pass without triggering an alert out of all non-conflicting sections. In local legislative work, frequent false warnings on routine clauses lead to alert fatigue, causing researchers to doubt the tool. Maintaining high specificity ensures that conflict alerts remain credible and that researchers can focus their attention on genuine legal risks.

    Maintaining high specificity protects legislative researchers from alert fatigue, ensuring that the system does not produce spurious warnings on standard boilerplate clauses.     \section{Prototype and User Interface}
    \label{sec:prototype_development}
    The completed pipeline is deployed as an accessible, single-page web application designed for standard office desktop computers in local government units. The interface uses a clean, intuitive layout that mirrors familiar document conversion tools. Users begin by uploading digital draft ordinances in common text or PDF formats through a drag-and-drop file ingestion area. After submission, an active progress indicator keeps the user informed while the backend pipeline parses operative sections, runs Stage~1 hybrid retrieval across the 27,000+ statutory knowledge base, and executes Stage~2 Cross-Encoder inference. This streamlined layout avoids complex parameter settings or technical menus, allowing legal staff to conduct pre-enactment conflict checks quickly during active committee sessions.

    When the analysis finishes, the interface presents a structured diagnostic dashboard organized to support rapid human verification. The top of the screen displays an executive summary showing the total number of audited sections, the count of flagged potential conflicts, and the overall statutory compliance status of the draft. Below the summary, an interactive table lists each audited draft section alongside its corresponding Stage~1 candidate provisions. Clicking any flagged item expands a dedicated review card that presents the local ordinance section and the retrieved governing statute in a direct side-by-side comparative reading view. Placing the two texts side by side allows legal researchers to inspect the alleged contradiction immediately without switching between multiple browser tabs or searching through printed statute digests, substantially lowering cognitive load during legislative review~\cite{sweller1988cognitive}.

    To make the system's reasoning transparent and easy to interpret, the interface incorporates an Explainable AI (XAI) module focused directly on reading clarity and practical decision support~\cite{doshi2017towards, rudin2019stop}. Rather than displaying abstract neural activation weights or complex probability matrices, the interface provides intrinsic visual cues by highlighting the exact words that cause legal friction. The Cross-Encoder's internal cross-attention weights between the premise statute and the draft hypothesis are normalized into soft visual highlights directly over the text. The display visually contrasts opposing deontic modal operators, distinguishing strict prohibitions (such as \textit{prohibited}, \textit{unlawful}, or \textit{shall not}) from statutory permissions (such as \textit{authorized}, \textit{allowed}, or \textit{may}). In addition, matching regulated subject terms and conflicting numbers (such as local fines exceeding statutory penalty ceilings or differing distance setbacks) are highlighted in complementary colors, enabling researchers to identify the specific source of legal tension in seconds.

    To support actionable decision-making, the dashboard organizes detected inconsistencies using a two-tiered alert hierarchy. Critical Vertical Preemption Alerts are displayed with red warning banners, signaling that a proposed local provision directly contradicts a superior national statute under the \textit{Magtajas} doctrine and risks being declared \textit{ultra vires}. Informational Horizontal Notices are shown with blue informational banners, notifying the researcher that the draft overlaps with an existing Davao City ordinance and may cause unintended duplication or implied repeal. Accompanying each alert is a concise, plain-language \textit{ratio decidendi} summary explaining the legal basis of the warning (for example, stating that a local penalty of ten thousand pesos exceeds the five-thousand-peso statutory maximum authorized under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160). Legal researchers can review these highlights and explanations to decide whether the draft should be cleared, amended, or referred for committee debate, ensuring human oversight throughout the local legislative process.

    \section{End-User Usability Evaluation}
    \label{sec:user_evaluation_study}
    To evaluate how the automated conflict detection system operates within actual local legislative workflows, the study incorporates an end-user evaluation grounded in descriptive survey research. While computational benchmarks quantify retrieval recall and pairwise natural language inference accuracy, verifying whether the system serves as a viable decision-support assistant requires assessing its practical utility, task-technology fit, and software usability when operated by municipal legislative staff. The research design is structured as a descriptive, cross-sectional evaluation where participants test the prototype on realistic local ordinance drafts and immediately record their assessments through a structured quantitative instrument.

    The evaluation cohort comprises the 15 active legal researchers serving the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod. These professional evaluators represent the city's three legislative council districts and routinely perform preliminary statutory compliance audits and draft ordinance preparation under Republic Act No.~7160~\cite{philgov1991ra7160}. In local legislative administration, legal researchers examine proposed drafts before committee presentation, checking that draft clauses do not exceed devolved municipal powers or conflict with national legislation. Because these participants perform legislative auditing as their regular professional responsibility, their quantitative ratings provide an authentic assessment of whether the software aligns with the cognitive and administrative demands of municipal governance.

    The evaluation follows a structured, hands-on simulation replicating the First Reading committee preparation phase. Each testing session proceeds through three standardized steps. First, evaluators attend a brief ten-minute orientation introducing the prototype's assistive advisory role, input format requirements, and the distinction between vertical preemption alerts and horizontal consistency notices. Second, researchers interact directly with the single-page prototype dashboard on standard local government desktop computers matching the Host~A specification. Evaluators upload multi-section draft ordinances containing both fully compliant regulatory clauses and injected preemption edge cases under Section~458 of Republic Act No.~7160. Researchers inspect the retrieved Stage~1 statutory provisions, examine the side-by-side comparative views, evaluate the highlighted deontic command words, and review the plain-language ratio decidendi summaries. Third, upon completing the hands-on review, researchers immediately complete a standardized quantitative survey hosted on Google Forms to record their evaluations.

    To ensure methodological rigor while keeping the survey concise and free of cognitive fatigue during demanding council schedules, the quantitative instrument integrates two validated psychometric frameworks. First, overall software usability and learnability are measured using Brooke's 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS)~\cite{brooke1996sus}. The SUS instrument is widely recognized across human-computer interaction and legal informatics research for its high reliability and quick completion time. The 10 items use a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 for strongly disagree to 5 for strongly agree) and alternate between positive and negative statements to control for acquiescence bias. Second, task-specific utility and workflow alignment are measured using a targeted 4-item questionnaire adapted from Davis' Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)~\cite{davis1989perceived} and Goodhue and Thompson's Task-Technology Fit (TTF) framework~\cite{goodhue1995task}. These four items evaluate essential dimensions of legislative auditing: search efficiency, preemption risk detection, diagnostic clarity of highlighted phrases, and routine committee adoption viability. An optional open-ended question is included at the end of the survey to capture qualitative feedback on specific statutory edge cases and recommendations for institutional deployment. The remaining four items operationalize Davis' (1989) Technology Acceptance Model and Goodhue and Thompson's (1995) Task-Technology Fit theory, measuring domain-specific utility in accelerating statutory searches, detecting preemption risks, interpreting attention highlights, and supporting institutional adoption. Providing this explicit questionnaire mapping ensures that every survey metric is anchored in established research literature, enabling structured descriptive analysis of the prototype's real-world viability.

    Following descriptive survey research principles, the statistical analysis focuses on clear, interpretable measures of central tendency, variability, and standardized composite usability scoring. For each survey item, participant responses on the 5-point Likert scale are summarized using the Mean ($\mu$), Standard Deviation ($\sigma$), and Median. Reporting both parametric and non-parametric descriptive statistics provides a transparent distribution summary across all evaluation dimensions without imposing unrealistic normality assumptions on ordinal survey data.

    To compute overall software usability, the raw responses from the ten SUS items are converted into a standardized composite score ranging from 0 to 100:

    \noindent\textit{System Usability Scale Composite Score:}
    \begin{equation}
        S_{\text{SUS}} = 2.5 \times \left[ \sum_{i \in \text{odd}} (x_i - 1) + \sum_{i \in \text{even}} (5 - x_i) \right]
        \label{eq:sus_score}
        \eqcaption{System Usability Scale Composite Score}
    \end{equation}
    \noindent Where $x_i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ denotes the raw 5-point Likert response for survey item $i$, with odd items ($i \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9\}$) representing positively phrased statements and even items ($i \in \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$) representing negatively phrased statements.

    Equation~\ref{eq:sus_score} defines the canonical formula for calculating the composite System Usability Scale score ($S_{\text{SUS}}$) established by Brooke~\cite{brooke1996sus}. The formulation normalizes the raw Likert responses by subtracting 1 from the scores of positively phrased odd items and subtracting the raw scores of negatively phrased even items from 5, adjusting all items to a uniform scale from 0 to 4. Summing these adjusted contributions and multiplying by a scaling factor of 2.5 yields a standardized composite usability index spanning from 0 to 100. Methodologically, this formulation eliminates acquiescence bias by penalizing inconsistent responding across alternating polarities. The resulting composite score is benchmarked against the empirical acceptability ranges established by Bangor et al.~\cite{bangor2008empirical} and Sauro and Lewis~\cite{sauro2012quantifying}, where a composite score of 68 represents the global empirical average for acceptable software usability and scores exceeding 80 indicate superior usability and high user satisfaction.

    To verify the internal consistency reliability of the 4-item Technology Acceptance and Task-Technology Fit scale prior to reporting aggregate findings, the proponents compute Cronbach's alpha ($\alpha$)~\cite{cronbach1951coefficient}. Calculating Cronbach's alpha confirms whether the four domain-specific acceptance items measure a cohesive underlying construct of legislative utility. Furthermore, to evaluate consistency in legal determinations across researchers during the interactive First Reading simulation, inter-rater consensus is measured using Fleiss' Kappa ($\kappa$)~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}. Computing Fleiss' Kappa verifies that user verdicts on test drafts reflect shared legal standards rather than subjective individual impressions.

    The evaluation protocol strictly adheres to the ethical and data privacy mandates of Republic Act No.~10173 (the Data Privacy Act of 2012)~\cite{philgov2012ra10173}. Participation in the evaluation study is entirely voluntary, and all participants complete an electronic informed consent form prior to survey submission. To protect evaluator confidentiality, participant names, specific committee assignments, and councilor affiliations are permanently decoupled from response records and indexed exclusively through anonymized identification tokens (such as Evaluator A-01 through A-15). Survey submissions are stored on secure university research storage and analyzed solely in aggregate descriptive form for academic thesis reporting.

    \section{Methodological Constraints}
    \label{sec:methodological_constraints}
    The first constraint involves the language of the legislative documents. Following Executive Order No.~292 (the Administrative Code of 1987) and national publishing standards, the statutory archive and local ordinance records examined in this study are limited to official English-language enactments. Because of this, the automated pipeline does not process vernacular Cebuano-Visayan phrases, colloquial regional idioms, or mixed Taglish expressions that occasionally arise during informal committee deliberations or floor debates. While operative legal provisions in the Philippines are formally enacted in English, statutory questions that depend on localized dialectal nuances require manual review by legal researchers. This constraint establishes a clear boundary for the tokenizer and avoids confusion when evaluating formal statutory commands against informal draft notes.

    The second constraint involves the age and physical condition of the local ordinance archive. The historical enactment records obtained from the Davao City Sangguniang Panlungsod display uneven chronological density, where ordinances passed between 2021 and 2025 benefit from clean digital records within the Legislative Information Support System Portal (LISSP). In contrast, older local enactments dating from 1945 to 2020 exist primarily as physical carbon-copy scans, marked by faded ink, page skew, and photocopy marks. Although the vision-language ingestion pipeline utilizing Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct substantially reduces transcription errors compared to traditional optical character recognition, residual character errors on pre-1980 archival enactments inevitably affect extraction fidelity. The retrospective evaluation of local ordinances prior to the modern digital era is therefore focused on landmark enactments that have undergone manual verification alongside high-resolution scanning.

    The third constraint stems from the single-premise asymmetric modeling formulation adopted for the coarse-to-fine pipeline. The natural language inference engine models vertical conflict detection as an asymmetric pair, matching an individual candidate statutory premise ($p_i$) against an operative draft ordinance block ($h_q$). This structural formulation effectively captures direct contradictions where a single national statute explicitly invalidates an ordinance clause, such as unauthorized penalty escalation under Republic Act No.~7160. However, the system does not inherently detect multi-statute cumulative conflicts, where an ordinance provision complies with each national statute in isolation but breaches a combined legislative framework formed by multiple distinct codes. Expanding the inference mechanism to support multi-premise reasoning across multiple statutes exceeds local workstation constraints and is left for future work.

    The final constraint defines the institutional and operational role of the automated conflict detection system within local governance. The pipeline is designed exclusively as an ex-ante assistive advisory tool intended for committee legal researchers during First Reading preparation, rather than an autonomous decision maker. The system produces calibrated confidence estimates and highlighted text spans to point out potential legal friction, but it does not make binding statutory determinations or substitute for judicial review under the Supreme Court of the Philippines. Furthermore, political questions, discretionary policy choices, and socio-economic trade-offs remain exclusively with elected city council members. Defining this operational boundary ensures that the system functions strictly within established principles of administrative law, safeguarding human oversight throughout the local legislative process.
