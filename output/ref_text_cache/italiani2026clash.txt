Legal conﬂicts between statutes or constitutional articles present a signiﬁcant challenge in maintaining con-
sistency and coherence within legal systems. Addressing these conﬂicts requires extensive human expertise, 
making the process labor-intensive and time-consuming. In this paper, we introduce Clash-of-Leges, a novel 
multilingual dataset derived from rulings by the Constitutional Court of the Italian Republic, designed to aid 
the automation of conﬂict detection and explanation between legal articles. We identify three key tasks: Con-
ﬂict Classiﬁcation, which determines whether two legal articles are in conﬂict; Conﬂict Explanation Generation, 
which provides detailed explanations for identiﬁed conﬂicts; and Reference Retrieval, which sources relevant 
legal bases and precedents to substantiate interpretations. These tasks are intended to facilitate the development 
of AI models that can automatically identify and explain contradictions between legal provisions.2
1.  Introduction
The integration of artiﬁcial intelligence(AI) into the legal domain is 
reshaping longstanding practices, from legal research and case analysis 
to drafting and decision-making. While traditionally resistant to automa-
tion, the legal sector is increasingly adopting AI tools that bring eﬃ-
ciency, consistency, and accuracy to complex tasks. Among AI’s primary 
contributions to legal practice are automated document review and le-
gal forecasting. By employing natural language processing (NLP), AI 
systems swiftly identify pertinent legal statutes and precedents, allow-
ing lawyers to streamline case preparation (Rissland & Ashley, 1987). 
Additionally, predictive analytics trained on historical case data en-
able lawyers to anticipate case outcomes, helping them evaluate case 
strength and advise clients with greater conﬁdence. For instance, mod-
els trained on past court decisions have shown promising results in pre-
dicting outcomes in areas such as bankruptcy, intellectual property, and 
contract disputes (Aletras et al., 2016). AI-driven applications now assist 
also with document summarization (Moro et al., 2023a; Moro & Ragazzi, 
2022, 2023; Moro et al., 2023c; Ragazzi et al., 2024, 2025) and ques-
tion answering (Italiani et al., 2025) for case law analysis, and contract 
management (Ashley, 2017) by parsing vast legal texts, revealing pat-
terns, and providing rapid, precise insights that enhance productivity 
and reduce costs. These advances underscore AI’s potential to perform

Despite these advancements, the process of drafting new laws re-
mains an underexplored area of AI application, particularly in address-

vent overlaps or contradictions with existing laws. Conﬂicting statutes 
or ambiguous regulations can destabilize legal interpretations, increase 
litigation, and place burdens on judicial systems tasked with resolving 
these issues. Traditionally, legislative drafters consult constitutional ex-
perts and subject the laws to thorough reviews to minimize conﬂicts. 
In some jurisdictions, like Italy, the Constitutional Court plays a crucial 
role in reviewing new laws to ensure they align with constitutional prin-
ciples. Yet, even with such safeguards, legal conﬂicts frequently arise 
within complex legal systems, complicating judicial processes.
Once identiﬁed, these conﬂicts must be resolved by courts to clar-
ify statutory hierarchies and maintain legal coherence. For instance, 
in the Italian legal system, the Constitutional Court often deals with 
conﬂicts between national laws and constitutional articles, issuing rul-
ings to clarify the hierarchy and proper interpretation of competing le-
gal provisions. However, manually detecting and resolving such con-
ﬂicts is time-intensive and demands signiﬁcant legal expertise. Legal
professionals must sift through extensive legal texts, interpret over-

Existing works have evident limitations (see Table 1). As outlined 
by Schumann and Gómex (2024), only two studies proposed datasets 
containing more than 250 cases of conﬂict, and the vast majority of 
conﬂict cases (87 %) were (semi-)synthetically generated. This reliance 
on synthetic conﬂicts raises concerns about the generalizability of these 
datasets, as they may not accurately reﬂect the complexity and nuances 
of real-world conﬂicts. Furthermore, half of all studies demonstrated 
their approaches using only a few individual examples rather than eval-
uating them holistically with annotated test datasets. Such limited eval-
uations make it challenging to assess the robustness and applicability of 
these methods in practical scenarios. Finally, in terms of addressed tasks, 
current datasets primarily focus on classiﬁcation, without supporting

downstream tasks like conﬂict explanation or the retrieval of relevant 
legal provisions–both essential for real-world legal applications.
Our work addresses these gaps by directly engaging with real-world 
legal rulings from the Constitutional Court of the Italian Republic 
(CCIR). In particular, we present Clash-of-Leges, a novel bilingual 
dataset designed to assist AI models in detecting and explaining con-
ﬂicts within statutory law (see Fig. 1). By focusing on rulings where 
constitutional conﬂicts are explicitly identiﬁed, our dataset provides a 
realistic and practical foundation for evaluating detection approaches. 
This emphasis on real-world data ensures that the models developed us-
ing our dataset are better equipped to handle the complexity of conﬂicts 
as they occur in practice, bridging the gap between theoretical frame-
works and practical legal applications. Additionally, we also incorporate 
an automatically translated version of the dataset in English to enhance 
its accessibility and broaden its utility for international research. Based 
on this resource, we introduce three core tasks essential for addressing 
legal conﬂicts in drafting and statutory review:
• Conﬂict Detection: Identifying whether two legal provisions con-
ﬂict.
• Conﬂict Explanation Generation: Providing automated explana-
tions for detected conﬂicts.
• Article Retrieval: Sourcing relevant legal bases and precedents to 
substantiate interpretations.
We perform comprehensive quantitative analyses on Clash-of-
Leges, utilizing a broad array of evaluation metrics to pave the way for 
future research developments. In particular, we systematically evaluate 
the performance of state-of-the-art multilingual large language models 
(LLMs) under practical limitations, such as running on a single 24 GB 
GPU. Our study evaluates these models’ adaptability across languages 
and tasks, revealing that signiﬁcant improvements are needed for prac-
tical usability.
2.  Related work
2.1.  Datasets and jurisdictions
In recent years, a diverse set of legal NLP benchmarks has emerged, 
reﬂecting the structural, linguistic, and jurisdictional heterogeneity of 
legal systems. Within common law systems, widely adopted datasets in-
clude CaseHOLD (Zheng et al., 2021) for legal judgment prediction in 
the U.S. context and ECtHR (Chalkidis et al., 2021a) for classiﬁcation 
and outcome prediction in rulings by the European Court of Human 
Rights. In addition, the COLIEE task, based on Canadian case law, sup-
ports legal question answering and textual entailment across multiple 
subtasks. Conversely, in civil law systems, datasets are more often cen-
tered on statutory interpretation and normative reasoning. Notable ex-
amples include LeCaRD (Ma et al., 2021), a Chinese dataset for legal

2.2.  Conﬂict detection datasets
Only a limited number of corpora provide structured annotations for 
legal conﬂicts. Most benchmarks, such as ContractNLI (Koreeda & Man-
ning, 2021) and NeuralConﬂict (Huang et al., 2024b), focus on contract 
law and rely on synthetic or semi-synthetic conﬂict pairs, with an exclu-
sive emphasis on English and common law contexts. Some eﬀorts target 
normative or policy documents (Zadrozny et al., 2017), but they rarely 
include real-world judicial data, constitutional review, or multilingual 
content. Similarly, works like Aires et al. (2019a) concentrate on clause-
level norm conﬂicts using semantic representations and entailment-
based reasoning, assuming monolingual, decontextualized settings that 
reﬂect common law interpretive patterns. Exceptions include the align-
ment of EU directives with Member State laws (Araszkiewicz et al., 
2021), or compliance between SOPs and medical guidelines (Zadrozny 
et al., 2017), yet these are often domain-speciﬁc, lack statutory focus, 
and are not framed within a structured legal hierarchy. In contrast, con-
ﬂict detection in civil law systems remains largely underexplored. Ex-
isting approaches rarely engage with real-world statutory conﬂicts as 
they emerge in legislative or constitutional review. Our work addresses 
this gap by introducing a bilingual corpus grounded in Italian Consti-
tutional Court rulings, supporting not only binary conﬂict detection as 
in the current benchmarks, but also explanation and reference retrieval, 
thereby enabling the development of interpretable, multilingual legal 
AI for civil law contexts.
2.3.  Explanation generation in legal AI
Explanation generation is an increasingly prominent objective in le-
gal AI, motivated by the need for transparency and trust. In tasks such 
as judicial outcome prediction (He et al., 2025), several eﬀorts have fo-
cused on generating natural language rationales (Chalkidis et al., 2021b; 
He et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2020) and legal entailment (Koreeda & 
Manning, 2021). These approaches typically rely on pre-trained lan-
guage models ﬁne-tuned to output structured or free-form explanations, 
sometimes augmented with attention or rationale extraction layers to 
enhance interpretability. Other works adopt rule-based or logic-driven 
templates, especially in highly structured domains like regulatory com-
pliance (Rabelo et al., 2021).
Despite this growing interest, explanation generation in the con-
text of statutory conﬂict detection remains underexplored. Existing 
datasets often lack explicit annotations of the legal reasoning behind 
conﬂicts, and few benchmarks support multilingual, cross-document, 
or constitutionally-grounded explanation tasks. Our work addresses this 
gap by explicitly framing conﬂict explanation as a supervised task, pair-
ing each identiﬁed conﬂict with concise rationales extracted from real-
world constitutional rulings. This enables the training and evaluation of 
models that not only detect contradictions, but also articulate the legal 
principles and statutory inconsistencies that underlie them.
2.4.  NLP Methods for legal conﬂicts
The task of identifying conﬂicts within legal texts aligns closely with 
broader research in the domain of consistency checking and conﬂict 
detection across textual content. One prominent approach involves the 
use of formal logic systems to model and analyze regulatory texts (Chitti-
malli & Anand, 2016; Chittimalli et al., 2019; Mitra et al., 2018). By rep-
resenting regulations through logical expressions, conﬂicts can be iden-
tiﬁed by detecting logical inconsistencies. Deontic logic, which deals 
with obligation, permission, and prohibition, has been particularly use-
ful in this context. This method allows for the formalization of norms 
and the automated detection of normative conﬂicts within and between 
regulations.
NLP techniques have also garnered signiﬁcant attention, and one of 
the most prevalent approaches involves training classiﬁers in a super-
vised fashion. Convolutional Neural Networks have been widely used 
for this task, as seen in studies such as Aires et al. (2019c), Aires and 
Meneguzzi (2021, 2017). Other architectures, such as Long Short-Term 
Memory (LSTM) networks, have also been applied to norm conﬂict 
detection. For example, studies like Alshamsan and Chaudhry (2022), 
Huang et al. (2024b) utilized LSTMs for this purpose. Similarly, Sup-
port Vector Machines have been leveraged in various works, includ-
ing Aires et al. (2019b,d), highlighting their eﬀectiveness in classi-
ﬁcation tasks related to norm conﬂicts. Moreover, transformer-based 
models have gained attention in recent works, including Ichida and 
Meneguzzi (2021), van Leijenhorst et al. (2023).
In order to train such supervised solutions, several methodologies 
have been explored to construct pairs of conﬂicting texts. One preva-
lent approach focuses on sentence pairing based on similarity metrics. 
Tools employing sentence embeddings or n-gram-based cosine similarity 
have been utilized in Hematialam (2021), van Leijenhorst et al. (2023), 
Sai et al. (2023). These methods involve linking sentences into candidate 
pairs once a similarity threshold is met. For example, in Sai et al. (2023), 
a two-step pairing process was applied to align sentences from hospi-
tal’s standard operating procedures (SOPs) with recommendations from 
medical guidelines. Similarly, term-based or entity-based pairing ap-
proaches were employed in Aires et al. (2018, 2017), Araszkiewicz et al. 
(2023), where only provisions concerning the same parties were com-
pared. Additionally, techniques like TextDiﬀ functions, as used in Abual-
haija et al. (2024), enabled pair creation by identifying diﬀerences or ab-
sence between provisions in separate lists. Semi-synthetic datasets have 
also been a signiﬁcant focus in conﬂict detection research, with studies 
such as Aires et al. (2019b,d), Aires and Meneguzzi (2021), Huang et al. 
(2024b) that generated semi-synthetic evaluation datasets by inserting 
contradictions into conﬂict-free texts. This was achieved by manually or 
semi-automatically altering random normative sentences to create anno-
tated sentence pairs for model training. Other studies, such as Alsham-
san and Chaudhry (2022), Koreeda and Manning (2021), constructed 
a Cartesian product of all relevant text components and subsequently 
performed their analysis on the resulting pairs.
While the aforementioned studies primarily compare information 
within similar document types, some have extended this to cross-type 
document comparisons. Examples include the alignment of SOPs with 
medical guidelines (van Leijenhorst et al., 2023), EU Directives with 
Member State laws (Araszkiewicz et al., 2021), and Data Protection 
Guidelines with the GDPR (Alshamsan & Chaudhry, 2022). These inter-
document comparisons illustrate the potential for identifying conﬂicts 
across heterogeneous legal sources.

3.1.  Dataset construction
Conﬂict Detection. The ﬁrst step in constructing the dataset was identi-
fying rulings where the CCIR assessed the constitutional legitimacy of 
one or more legal articles. In these cases, the Court determines whether 
the contested legal provisions align with the Italian Constitution, and if 
not, the Court declares the provisions unconstitutional. We manually re-
viewed these rulings to extract instances where such constitutional con-
ﬂicts were explicitly identiﬁed, forming the foundation of our dataset. 
All rulings were reviewed by a single legal expert and not by multiple 
annotators, since identifying conﬂicting provisions is largely objective. 
In fact, pairs of conﬂicting articles are immediately recognizable within 
each ruling due to the standardized language used to signal an antinomy. 
In addition, we also annotated cases where conﬂicts arose between two 
articles belonging to diﬀerent laws. These conﬂicts often stem from sit-
uations where one legal provision challenges the validity or application 
of another, potentially creating indirect constitutional issues (see Fig. 2

when referenced (see Fig. 1 step .(a) for the graphical breakthrough 
of our annotation approach). Then, to construct negative examples for 
the classiﬁcation task—instances where two articles are determined not 
to be in conﬂict—we adopted a systematic approach instead of relying 
on simple random pairing. Speciﬁcally, we utilized CCIR rulings where 
constitutional illegitimacy claims were deemed unfounded. This method 
increases the task’s complexity, presenting the model with pairs of arti-
cles that exhibit a plausible, albeit non-conﬂicting, relationship, rather 
than unrelated or at best loosely associated articles.
Conﬂict Explanation. The annotations focused also on the reasoning pro-
vided by the Court in its verdict. When available, this was extracted 
using regex rules; in cases where these rules proved insuﬃcient, Chat-
GPT was employed to assist in the extraction operation (see Fig. 1 step 
.(b)). This process ensures that the dataset includes not only the con-
ﬂicting articles but also provides valuable context about the nature of 
the conﬂict, which is critical for facilitating more accurate analysis of 
legal precedents and patterns across similar cases.
Reference Retrieval. The text of the articles may contain references to 
others, and in some cases, this contextual information is essential for 
accurate interpretation. The meaning of an article can easily be misin-
terpreted, as its implications often depend on its connections to related 
texts (Tiersma, 2000). Therefore, we automatically identify these ref-
erences by leveraging the Akoma Ntoso standard,3 which provides a 
structured, domain-speciﬁc XML vocabulary for representing executive, 
legislative, and judicial documents in a standardized legal format. We 
deﬁne a retrieval task where textual references within the article text 
are removed, requiring the retriever model to accurately identify and 
return the correct referenced document from a database of 20,000 Ital-
ian legal articles sourced from Normattiva,4 a comprehensive repository 
of Italian legal texts (see Fig. 1 step ).

3.2.  Language translation
We perform automatic translation of articles, references, and expla-
nations from Italian to English by evaluating various machine transla-
tion models and services: GPT-4o, Google Translate, and LLaMA-
3-8B. To assess translation quality, we employ two quality control
methods and measure the eﬀectiveness of the translations using a sam-
ple of 102 instances, evenly distributed across the training, validation, 
and test sets. In line with recent studies that circumvent human analysis 
due to its high cost (Feng et al., 2022; Scao et al., 2022), particularly 
when translating lengthy legal texts, we adopt a standard approach to 
assess translation eﬀectiveness through Round-Trip Translation (or Back 
Translation). Technically, it involves translating a text into a diﬀerent 
language and re-translating it to the original one. We implement the 
same approach as Feng et al. (2022) and Zhu et al. (2019) and employ 
two evaluation criteria. First, to assess syntactic accuracy, we compute 
ROUGE scores (Lin, 2004) by comparing the original text with its back-
translated counterpart. Second, to evaluate beyond syntactic aspects, 
we measure sentence-level semantic similarity using an Italian cross-
encoder.5

To validate the reliability of the automatically translated dataset, 
we also conducted a human evaluation on a subset of instances. 
Speciﬁcally, we selected 127 translated article pairs from the test 
set of the conﬂict detection task and 26 translated explanations 
from the test set of the conﬂict explanation task. Each translation 
was independently assessed by two bilingual (Italian-English) anno-
tators with legal expertise. Annotators rated each translation on a 
scale from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) according to the three dimen-
sions deﬁned in Table 5. The resulting inter-annotator agreement, 
measured by Cohens kappa, was 0.38. Table 6 reports the aver-
age scores across both annotators for the entire evaluated subset. 
Overall, the automatic translations achieved satisfactory performance 
across all three dimensions, with an average score across categories of 
3.69 and 3.79 for article pairs and explanation, respectively. Impor-
tantly, the semantic-ﬁdelity scores conﬁrm that the original meaning is
preserved.

executed on a workstation with a single Nvidia GeForce RTX3090 GPU 
of 24 GB of dedicated memory, 64 GB of VRAM, and an Intel® Core™i9-
10900X1080 CPU @ 3.70GHz. The operating system is Ubuntu 20.04.3 
LTS. To enhance consistency and portability, our development environ-

3 epochs using the Unsloth library (Daniel Han & team, 2023) to reduce 
VRAM consumption and enable faster ﬁne-tuning, with the AdamW op-
timizer (learning rate 5e-6), a linear scheduler, and a ﬁxed random seed 
of 42 for reproducibility; no preprocessing was performed.

Baselines. For the conﬂict classiﬁcation and explanation tasks we con-
sider LLaMA-3-8B,8 which represents a signiﬁcant improvement over 
its predecessor, LLaMA-2, setting a new standard for LLMs of this 
scale through enhanced architecture and training techniques. Gemma-2-
9B (Team et al., 2024) delivers one of the best-in-class performances for 
its size, competing eﬀectively with models 2-3× larger, while remain-
ing open and accessible to the community. Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 
2023) comprises grouped-query attention (Ainslie et al., 2023) and slid-
ing window attention (Beltagy et al., 2020) to achieve a harmonious 
equilibrium between the pursuit of high performance and eﬃciency.
SaulLM-7B (Colombo et al., 2024) builds upon the Mistral-7B archi-
tecture and is trained on a specialized English legal corpus comprising 
over 30 billion tokens. This tailored design achieves state-of-the-art per-
formance in legal text comprehension and generation tasks. Addition-
ally, we consider recent closed-source LLMs. Speciﬁcally, we employ
GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024), which oﬀers substantial improvements on 
text in non-English languages, while also delivering signiﬁcantly faster 
inference and a 50 % reduction in API cost. DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 
2025) is a Mixture-of-Experts language model optimized for reasoning 
tasks, combining supervised ﬁne-tuning with large-scale reinforcement 
learning to achieve GPT-4-level performance with improved eﬃciency.

speciﬁc adaptation, we compare the performance of open-source mod-
els in zero-shot and few-shot settings. The zero-shot experiments were 
conducted by prompting the models with the same instructions used 
for the closed-source LLMs, as shown in Table 8. In the few-shot set-

prompt. Fig. 7 illustrates that ﬁne-tuning consistently outperforms both

diﬀerences are minor and overall performance remains poor. This ﬁnd-
ing further highlights the originality of our dataset. The knowledge ac-

performance, clearly indicating that our data lies outside the domain 
of the pre-training corpora. Moreover, the zero-shot setting consistently 
outperforms the few-shot setting, likely because two examples are in-
suﬃcient to capture the overall data distribution and instead introduce 
additional noise into the prompt.

LLM-as-a-Judge Factuality Evaluation. Given the high number of combi-
nations of models and languages, we opt for an LLM-based evaluation 
protocol to evaluate the factual consistency of the generated explanation 
with respect to the target one. In particular, we drew on the method-
ological frameworks of G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023), Prometheus (Kim et al., 
2024), and recent analyses of LLM-as-a-Judge reliability (Huang et al., 
2024a), which collectively show that LLM evaluators, when properly 
constrained, correlate strongly with expert assessments on factuality and 
faithfulness tasks. All factuality scores for the conﬂict explanation task 
were produced using GPT-5-mini (OpenAI, 2025) under human supervi-
sion. Two authors manually inspected random subsets of the generated 
scores to verify alignment with human expectations and to correct cases 
where the evaluator’s reasoning was inconsistent with the articles’ legal 
meaning. The evaluator prompt was designed following the structured 
rubric paradigm of G-Eval, assigning an integer factuality score from 0 
to 5 based solely on factual and legal correctness. Table 12 showcases 
the prompt used to evaluate all models.
Each evaluation was repeated across three independent passes, 
with scores averaged to reduce stochastic variation as recommended 
by Huang et al. (2024a). This setup allowed us to obtain reproducible, 
ﬁne-grained factuality judgments at scale while maintaining the inter-
pretability and reliability standards advocated in recent LLM-as-a-Judge 
research. The results in Fig. 9 illustrate how this factuality-oriented 
analysis reshapes the interpretation of explanation quality. Notably, 
closed-source models achieve markedly higher factuality scores, sug-
gesting that their lower syntactic and semantic overlap with reference 
explanations primarily reﬂects stylistic diﬀerences rather than deﬁcien-
cies in content preservation. Conversely, open-source models, even after 
ﬁne-tuning, exhibit substantially lower factuality scores and frequent in-
stances of hallucinated facts or legal inaccuracies.

5.  Conclusion
This paper introduced Clash-of-Leges, a novel bilingual dataset de-
rived from real-world constitutional court rulings in Italy, speciﬁcally 
designed to address the challenges of legal conﬂict detection and expla-
nation. We deﬁned three core tasks–Conﬂict Detection, Conﬂict Expla-
nation Generation, and Reference Retrieval–emphasizing their poten-
tial to advance AI applications in legal drafting, statutory analysis, and 
judicial review. The dataset bridges signiﬁcant gaps in prior research 
by focusing on authentic conﬂicts between provisions in diﬀerent legal 
documents rather than synthetically generated ones and incorporating 
bilingual support to broaden accessibility and research utility. Our ex-
perimental results highlight the performance of state-of-the-art multilin-
gual and domain-speciﬁc LLMs across the proposed tasks. While these 
models achieved promising results, the ﬁndings also revealed persistent 
challenges, particularly in handling the nuanced language of legal texts 
and achieving robust performance in less-resourced languages like Ital-
ian. Our results underscore the unique challenges posed by our Clash-
of-Leges benchmark and the new capabilities that can be learned from 
it. Open-source models, having been exposed to the task through tar-
geted ﬁne-tuning, captured the nuanced linguistic and structural cues 
that diﬀerentiate conﬂictual from non-conﬂictual articles. In contrast, 
the closed-source models, evaluated without any in-task examples, con-
sistently underperformed in conﬂict detection and explanation, indicat-
ing that their pre-training alone was insuﬃcient to internalize these
specialized patterns. This disparity persists despite the closed-source 
models’ greater size and larger pre-training corpora, illustrating that 
sheer scale cannot substitute for task-speciﬁc adaptation. However, 
when explanations were assessed through an LLM factuality evaluation 
framework, a more nuanced picture was revealed. Closed-source models 
exhibited higher factual consistency and fewer hallucinated statements. 
This suggests that their lower lexical overlap and less elaborate argu-
mentative structure stem more from stylistic divergence than from deﬁ-
ciencies in factual grounding. Taken together, these results show that 
scale and ﬁne-tuning play complementary roles. Closed-source mod-
els provide stronger factual grounding, whereas ﬁne-tuned open-source 
models excel at legal reasoning and interpretive explanation. The de-
velopment and evaluation of Clash-of-Leges mark a signiﬁcant step 
toward automating the identiﬁcation and resolution of legal conﬂicts. 
However, future work must focus on enhancing the reasoning capabili-
ties and interpretability of AI models (Moro et al., 2024)–also with the 
aid of knowledge graph learning techniques (Frisoni et al., 2020)–and 
expanding datasets to include diverse jurisdictions and legal systems 
to test cross-domain performance (Domeniconi et al., 2015; Lodi et al., 
2010; Moro & Monti, 2012).
