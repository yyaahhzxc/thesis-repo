ABSTRACT 
 
With the numerous storms and typhoons that have plagued the 
Philippines, social media websites such as Twitter have proven to 
be of aid to people in the communication and dissemination of 
information between the local government units and citizenry. The 
national government implements and uses a unified hashtag 
convention to easily track and monitor any news or information 
regarding these disasters. However, not all users utilized these 
hashtags to spread newsworthy tweets. Some of them sent tweet 
messages that were not meaningful for the intended purpose. Thus, 
this study aims to determine the bases for the validity of news 
posted in social networking sites, such as Twitter, to use data 
mining techniques on Twitter's streaming API to gather past tweet 
data and to classify these tweet data in an automated 
implementation of classifying tweets with storm-related news. 
 
Keywords: Twitter, storms, hashtag, streaming API, data mining

CHAPTER 1  
 
INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background of the Problem 
Twitter is a free micro-blogging service with over a million users worldwide. It 
allows users to post and exchange tweet messages with up to 280 characters. 
Twitter is used through a wide variety of users. As of 2010, about 46% of active 
users correspond to mobile users (Williams, 2010). However, not all tweets are 
informative. Most Twitter users post about themselves (Naaman, Boase, & Lai, 
2010). Although most tweets are considered unimportant, others have 
interesting and relevant information, and most of these relevant tweets are 
associated with hashtags. A hashtag is a word or phrase preceded by the # 
symbol that classifies or categorizes the accompanying text (Hashtag definition 
by Merriam-Webster, 2015). 
 
 
Hashtags are also indicators of a tweet’s meaning, topic, or, its intended 
audience (Efron, 2010). Due to its categorical aspect, it is used by news agencies 
as a flag for them to gather data related to certain situations from the masses, 
such as the arrival of a typhoon or storm. However, not even all tweets with 
corresponding hashtags about the storm’s arrival are deemed informatively 
valid. Some tweets spread rumors, false news, and even irrelevant personal 
information. Thus, there is a need to know what tweet is valid or relevant 
during storm arrivals. 
 
To do this, there is a need to analyze a tweet's corpus for relevance. There have 
been studies which analyze tweet corpus in times of emergencies, storms, and 
earthquakes. These studies do tweet data mining, using Twitter's streaming 
API, followed by classification and labeling of tweets. Though relevant tweets 
were classified appropriately for their content, these were done manually, using 
crowdsourcing services. Crowdsourcing involves a lot of respondents to get the 
job done, usually with the help of online crowdsourcing services. There had been 
studies which use crowdsourcing to manually label datasets to implement an 
automated method of classifying data. Thus, there is also a need to do an 
automated method of classifying tweet data. 
 
There had been no attempt of implementing an automated method of 
classifying tweet data according to its content in the context of disaster 
communications in the Philippines through a web visualization tool. Thus, the

CHAPTER 2  
 
REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE 
2.1 Twitter as a communication tool in emergencies 
Twitter is an online microblogging service which is used as a communication 
tool by users all around the world. This service is used by many users for 
personal communication, but one of the most relevant uses of the said service 
is to spread information about emergencies or even events, which entice mass 
convergence. It had been used as a tool to spread information about 
emergencies about floods, storms, and earthquakes. Due to this fast and free 
method of online communication, Twitter had been used in previous studies 
about human behavior during natural calamities (De Leon, Estuar, Lee, & 
Ybañez, 2013). But it was also discovered that the tweets that users send in the 
dire times of storm arrivals or earthquakes are not entirely relevant and could 
contain rumors and false news (Castillo, Mendoza, & Poblete, 2010). To this 
end, there had been studies that provide a basis to identify and classify tweets 
with situational awareness in times of disasters (Vieweg, 2012). 
2.2 The classification of Tweet corpus in emergencies 
Tweets written in the wake of a storm disaster are varied and free-flowing. 
Therefore, there is a need to classify each tweet’s corpus – to determine its 
relevance. A relevant tweet in the context of mass emergencies is a tweet, which 
provides situational awareness. Tweets considered to have situational 
awareness must provide tactical, actionable information that can aid people in 
making decisions, advise others on how to obtain specific information from 
various sources, or offer immediate post-impact help to those affected by the 
mass emergency. A study presented a total of 34 specific types of information 
that contributes to situational awareness, these were: Advice – Information 
Space, 
Animal 
Management, 
Caution, 
Evacuation, 
Fatality, 
Feeding/Hydration, General Population Information, Immediate Recovery, 
Injury, Medical Attention, Missing, Offer of Help, Preparation, Report of Crime, 
Request for Help, Request for Information, Rescue, Response – Community, 
Response – Formal, Response – Miscellaneous, Response – Personal, 
Sheltering, Status – Community/Population, Status – Personal, Damage, 
Status – Infrastructure, Status – Personal Property, Status – Public Property, 
General Area Information, General Hazard Information, Historical, Prediction, 
Status – Hazard and lastly, Weather (Vieweg, 2012). With these criteria for 
classification, there have been studies that capture and process tweets in the 
wake of natural disasters in general.

One such study adapted the same categories for tweet messages stated earlier. 
This study’s categories for tweet-relevance comprises the following: (1) 
Caution and advice, (2) Casualties and damage, (3) Donations of money, 
goods or services, (4) People missing, found or seen, and (5) Information 
sources. Along with these adapted categories, the study presented the 
extraction of information nuggets from microblogging messages during the 
Joplin tornado of 2011. The study used a crowdsourcing service, CrowdFlower, 
to manually classify tweet messages into a set of classes and to extract short, 
self-contained structured information that can be used for complex data 
analysis and integration beyond plain text. The study also employed the use of 
Naive-Bayesian classifier to do an automated classification of the tweet data 
(Imran, Elbassuoni, Castillo, Diaz, & Meier, 2013).  
 
Another study used the same criteria stated above to create a mobile 
application that would determine relevant and irrelevant tweets (Cobo, Parra, 
& Navon, 2013). However, their adaptation of the criteria did not include the 
Donations of money, goods or services criteria. In the said study, a classifier 
was implemented to filter tweets relevant and irrelevant to an earthquake. The 
data gathered for this study were tweets from the Chilean earthquake of 2010. 
The initial classification of the tweets according to the situational awareness 
ontology were done manually by six people. 
2.3 Corpus sentiment analysis 
There had been studies, which implement the analysis of text data. Most of 
such studies analyze text-data for any emotions and do automated methods of 
classification as to what emotion is present in the given corpus.  
 
A study was presented to implement a method, which recognizes emotion using 
the Knowledge-Based Artificial Neural Networks (K-BANN), and Emotional 
Keyword Dictionary (EKD) (Arcilla, Amoguis, & Tan, 2013). In this study, 
corpus, which contained randomly, picked sentences and words were used to 
train and test the system. Corpus has gone through the evaluation of KBANN 
and EKD as patterns to determine whether a statement contains any trace of 
depression. 
 
Another study presented an approach about emotion recognition in Tagalog 
song lyrics. A hybrid method of an affective Lexicon with Hierarchical 
Clustering (HCL) and Support Vector Machines (SVM) classifier was used to 
recognize and classify emotions present in a song lyric (Carreos & Liboon, 
2014).

There was a study, which analyzed and classified the behavior of Filipinos on 
Twitter during a disaster (De Leon, Estuar, Lee, & Ybañez, 2013). The study 
used Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) and Principal Component Analysis 
(PCA) to extract and classify the different topics and sentiments present during 
a disaster. Their findings include what topic and sentiment are more likely 
retweeted, what language participants use in disasters and primarily, what 
emotions are present in tweets in times of disasters in the Philippines. 
However, this study primarily focused on the emotions present in tweet data, 
and not the relevance of tweets in times of disasters. 
2.3.1 Naïve Bayes 
Document classification is an important machine-learning concept. In 
document classification, each instance represents a list of documents, a string 
of words, or text corpora, along with the instance's class is the document's topic 
or label. One way of applying machine learning to document classification is to 
treat the presence or absence of each word as a Boolean attribute. Naive Bayes 
is a technique best suited for this application due to its very fast and accurate 
nature (Witten & Frank, 2005). 
 
The Naïve Bayes classifier has been proven to be an effective machine-learning 
algorithm for sentiment analysis. A comparative study on Multinomial and the 
multi-variate Bernoulli Naïve Bayes was conducted to prove which of the two 
variants was effective in effectively class labeling in the context of anti-spam e-
mail filtering. The findings of this study stated that Multinomial NB proved to 
be less biased towards one class or label and achieved slightly higher accuracy 
than the Bernoulli Naïve Bayes (Schneider, 2003).  These two NB variants 
make assumptions about the distribution of the feature vectors of each 
document involved. Multinomial NB is often used to classify data based on the 
frequency of words in a string or document, whereas, Bernoulli NB or 
multivariate Bernoulli NB is used to classify data based on the presence of 
words or features.  
2.3.2 N-Grams 
An N-Gram is a sequence of N-words (Kumar, 2017). N-gram models can be 
described to be a method to predict succeeding words given the previous N-1 
words. The Maximum Likelihood Estimation is a probability assigned to N-
grams, which is a method that determines values for the parameters of a model. 
The parameter values are found such that they maximize the likelihood that 
the process described by the model produced the data that were observed 
(Brooks-Barlett, 2018).

2.4 Twitter API 
Twitter’s policy for data sharing is very open, providing a free Streaming API, 
which returns tweets matching a query provided by the Streaming API user. 
One disadvantage of the Streaming API is that it only returns at most 1% of 
tweets on Twitter at a given moment. Once the volume of the query surpasses 
1% of all the tweets on Twitter, the response is sampled. 
2.4.1 Web Applications which uses Twitter's streaming API 
There have been several current web applications that display data analytics 
based on Twitter's streaming API. Tweetchup.com is a website which displays 
data analytics and infographics on how many mentions or retweets a user gets. 
It also pinpoints what location did most of a user's mentions came from. This 
web application also allows users to analyze another user's Twitter data 
analytics and know how many times certain keywords were mentioned in a 
tweet within a specified period. However, all these functionalities are made 
possible with tweets that are publicly available, and not with ones that are set 
to private. 
 
Another similar web application which uses Twitter's streaming API is 
Twitonomy.com. It has similar functionalities as Tweetchup.com but gives 
users an option to download and print the tweet data analytics gathered by the 
application, if users opt to upgrade to a premium account.  
 
Although these web applications provide forms of analytics to users, these do 
not include data or tweet news filtering. 
2.4.2 Tweepy 
Tweepy is an easy to use open-source Python library. Its source code is freely 
available on GitHub and available in the PIP python package management 
system (Novalic, 2013). It enables users to communicate with the Twitter API 
using Python. Tweepy utilizes the OAuth authentication, to provide the 
following functionalities in the utilization of the Twitter API: 
• 
Tweets can be customized to have a string which identifies the app 
which was used. 
• 
It doesn’t reveal twitter credentials. 
• 
Twitter permissions (e.g., read, write, delete tweets or direct messages) 
are easier to manage, given that consumer and access tokens and keys 
are made specifically for a specific use case only (e.g., if the generated 
tokens and keys are for Reading tweets only, one cannot post tweets).

2.5 Data Visualization 
Data Visualization is how humans can understand the results of machine 
learning and sentiment analysis (Radich, 2017). Effective data visualization 
should be informative, efficient appealing and interactive (Pittenturf, n.d.).  
Data visualization gives readers a better understanding and appreciation of 
how to interpret big data, machine learning, and sentiment analysis.  
 
Since this study involves multi-label tweet data, there is a need to visualize 
this. A study presented a novel visualization approach for tree data structures. 
In this paper, large hierarchical data, or trees, were presented using circle 
packing. Each child node is represented by corresponding circles, within bigger 
circles that constitute its parent node (Wang, Wang, Dai, & Wang, 2006). The 
study’s circle packing implementation was developed using VC++6.0 and

3.1 Gather the storm-related tweets 
The initial step was to gather storm-related tweets using Twitter’s streaming 
API. Two datasets were gathered, tweets during the tornado that struck Joplin, 
Missouri on May 22, 2011, and tweets during the Hurricane Sandy, which hit 
Northeastern US on October 29, 2012. The two datasets were gathered using 
Twitter’s streaming API through the hashtags #joplin and #sandy respectively.   
3.2 Manual classification of tweets 
The second step was the classification of the tweets, as most tweet messages 
generated during a disaster are extremely varied and free-flowing. The dataset 
was both classified manually and automatically. The manual classification 
process used was crowdsourcing (using CrowdFlower), and each assessor was 
shown a tweet, along with instruction and an empty text input field and was 
asked to copy-paste a word or short phrase from the tweet conveying the 
specified information. Tweets were manually classified thrice based on the 
criteria and ontologies derived from previous studies. 
 
(1) Initial classification task was to filter informative messages based on three 
main classes: Personal – messages, which were only of, interest to its author 
and his/her immediate circle of family/friends; Informative – messages of 
interest to other people beyond the author’s immediate circle; and Other – 
messages that are not related to the disaster at all.  
 
(2) Only Informative tweet messages were considered, and were classified again 
by type based on the following classes: Caution and Advice – messages that 
convey or reports information about some warning or piece of advice about a 
possible hazard of an incident;  Casualties and Damage – messages that report 
about casualties or infrastructure damage done by an incident; Donations – 
messages about goods or services offered or needed by the victims of an incident;

incident, or reports the reaction or visit of a celebrity; Information Sources – 
messages that point to information sources, photos, videos or mentions a 
website, TV or radio station providing extensive coverage; and Other – which 
conveys messages about other types of informative messages.  
 
(3) For each of the said types, a separate crowdsourcing task was created where 
workers were asked to classify a message by sub-type and were asked to extract 
a sub-string from the message containing a given piece of information. 
3.3 Automatic classification of tweets 
The automated classification process involved training a set of multi-label 
classifiers, using Naïve Bayesian classifiers implemented in Weka, where some 
binary, scalar and text features were employed. The binary features 
determined whether a tweet contained the following features or not: the @ 
symbol, a URL, a hashtag, an emoticon, and a number. The scalar feature 
implied only the tweet length. While, text features excluded non-words from 
the binary features, and extracted the following: unigrams, bigrams, Part of 
Speech (POS) tags, POS tag-bigrams, and VerbNet classes. The classifier 
repeated the three tasks stated above. This was made possible by training the 
classifier using the data gathered from the manual classification method.

4.2.3.1 Multi-rater Reliability using Fleiss’ Kappa 
Fleiss’ kappa is a statistical measure used to evaluate the reliability of 
agreement between 3 to many raters when assigning a categorical rating to 
several items. Fleiss’ Kappa is defined as:  
 
𝜅= 𝑃̅ −𝑃̅𝑒
(1 −𝑃̅𝑒) 
 
𝑃̅ is the mean of all of all the raters’ agreed ratings, and 𝑃̅𝑒 is the mean of all 
the rater’s ratings agreeing solely due to chance. Subtracted, 𝑃̅ −𝑃̅𝑒 gives the 
degree of agreement of ratings achieved above chance, and 1 – 𝑃̅𝑒 gives the 
degree of agreement that is attainable above chance. If 𝜅 = 1, then the raters 
are in complete agreement, else there is no agreement between raters if 𝜅≤ 0.  
 
For this study’s manually classified data, the raters are the three batches of 
annotators that labeled the data three times. The achieved 𝜅 value was 
computed at 0.53609. This implies that there was a moderate agreement 
between raters. This interpretation was based on a study, which discussed the 
interpretations between 𝜅 value ranges (Landis & Koch, 1977).

4.2.5 Feature Extraction    
Feature Extraction is the process of transforming both the training and testing 
text data into numerical features which are usable by machine learning 
algorithms such as Naïve Bayes. These text data were vectorized and 
transformed respectively by the CountVectorizer and TfidfTransformer 
 
 
23 
 
modules of sklearn.feature_extraction.text. The output of the CountVectorizer 
module is a matrix of token counts which is fed to the TfidfTransformer, which 
produces Numpy array which contained numerical features derived from the 
non-numerical and arbitrary data of the tweet data set. 
 
Sklearn’s CountVectorizer() converts a collection of text documents or strings 
to a matrix of token counts. For this implementation, the CountVectorizer 
accepts and uses the following parameters: ngram_range and stop_words. The 
ngram_range parameter accepts a tuple python sequence, which contains two 
values: the lower and upper boundary of the range of n-values for different n-
grams to be extracted (i.e., a minimum of 2 and maximum of 7 n-grams).  
 
The data is fed to CountVectorizer by using the method .fit_transform(). This 
method accepts the training text dataset. The .fit_transform() method learns 
the vocabulary dictionary based on the dataset, the ngram_range values, which 
stop words to omit and returns a term-document matrix. The term-document 
matrix is a tally of all instances of terms from the data set for each tweet text 
instance, where zero (0) indicates an absence of a said term otherwise, a 
positive non-zero value would indicate the presence of a said term. In this 
implementation, a term is an n-gram between 1 to 6-grams or 1 to 6 adjacent 
word combinations.

Within the Pipeline’s parameters, is a pythonic list containing the following 
tuple pairs: vect and CountVectorizer(), tfidf and TfidfTransformer(), and, clf 
and MultinomialNB() / BernoulliNB(). Each tuple pair observes a key implies 
value relationship, such that each value would then be referenced later on, by 
calling its respective key.

when classifying tweets using Multinomial Naïve Bayes. An AUC-ROC 
diagram is a performance measurement score intended for classification 
models. The higher an AUC score implies that a classification model is better 
at distinguishing between classes. The best classification model has an AUC 
score of 1.0 whereas an inefficient classifier score at 0.5 or even less. Receiver 
Operating Characteristics (ROC) is the curve plotted on a cartesian plane, with 
False Positive Rates (FPR) or specificity on the X-axis, and True Positive Rates 
(TPR) or recall on the Y-axis. Each point on the ROC curve represents an FPR-
TPR point that corresponds to a decision threshold. ROC curves that are 
leaning towards the upper left corner of the graph indicates a higher accuracy 
of determining TPRs over FPRs.

5.2.3 Implementing the Visualization of Results in Django 
Django follows a Model-View-Template architecture. For this project, Django 
version 2.1 was used and installed in a virtual environment set up on a 
LUbuntu machine. The Django framework relies on five python files which are 
generated automatically once a Django project and application is initialized; 
these are (1) settings.py, (2) urls.py, (3) models.py, (4) views.py and (5) the 
HTML files found in the templates directory. 
 
The settings.py file contains the configurations of the Django installation such 
as, what applications are to be included in the web app, what is the time zone 
to be followed, what are the default directory paths for static and media files, 
etc. The urls.py file contains the URL configuration, which maps out URL path 
expressions to Python functions, which are found in the views.py file of an 
application.  
 
The models.py file contains the source of information of the data being used in 
the Django web application, such as the data types of these data, which data 
are essential, and what are the behaviors of these data. For this project, the 
models.py file wasn’t used, as the data being visualized on the web page are all 
from the static output.json file generated inside the views.py.  
 
The views.py file contains all Python user-defined functions which act as the 
controllers of data. This file is where computations and other data are passed 
to certain template or HTML files. This is also where the data is molded from 
the ready_for_visualization.csv into the output.json file. The HTML files found 
in the templates directory is where users view dynamic and static data.  
 
Visualization of the n-gram features was implemented using the D3js library’s 
circle packing tool. D3.js is a Javascript library which allows beautiful and 
interactive visualizations of statistical data, and classes which are stored in 
JSON file. A premade Circle Packing implementation was customized and used 
to visualize the data in the JSON file (Bostock, n.d.). Each node in the JSON 
file was visualized through these circle shapes. On page load, corresponding 
circle-packing clusters would be generated based on the size of each class’ n-
gram population.

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
6.1 Conclusion 
The typhoon Nona dataset was gathered successfully using Twitter’s streaming 
API – through the pythonic library Tweepy. Tweepy allowed Twitter 
interactions particularly searching for tweets with the hashtag #nonaPH. 
However, the API could not do data mining. Data mining is the process of 
analyzing the patterns of large data sets by using data cleaning, natural 
language processing, and machine learning methods. The API is limited to 
giving access to twitter interactions, such as tweeting (creating and posting 
tweets), deleting personal tweets, and searching for tweets based on a text 
query or category (hashtag, user search, or keyword search).  
 
The necessary tools needed to analyze the tweet corpora consists of developed 
scripts, and, methods belonging to the Sklearn and NLTK Python libraries. 
Before tweet classification, the tweet corpora had undergone various pre-
processing procedures: removal of symbols, hyperlinks, user mentions, hashtag 
symbols, blank cells, duplicate tweets, retweeted tweets, and stop words. Each 
of the tweets was also subject to CountVectorization and TfIDF transformation 
– which made the data ‘understandable’ to the chosen Bayesian classifiers.  
 
The validity of a tweet in the context of Typhoon disasters focused on its 
informative classes. These classes are (1) Caution and advice, (2) Casualties 
and damage, (3) Donations of money, goods or services, (4) People missing, 
found or seen, and (5) Information sources. These five classes served as both 
criteria and factors for tweet context validity and were the basis behind the 
Informative label in the Bayesian classifiers.  
 
The tweet classification process was made possible by utilizing Sklearn’s 
Multinomial and Bernoulli Naïve Bayes methods and was simplified further 
with the use of Sklearn’s Pipeline method. Between the two Bayesian 
classifiers, Multinomial Naïve Bayes performed with a better accuracy and 
classification scores as compared to Bernoulli Naïve Bayes because MNB 
classifies a tweet text based on the counts it finds of multiple n-grams or term 
frequency. MNB investigates the importance of each n-gram or term for each 
tweet text. BNB, on the other hand, bases its classification on the existence of 
certain n-grams features, in other words, BNB does a checklist of n-grams that 
 
 
53 
 
are observed to be present and absent in tweet texts that are labeled with a 
certain class.  
 
Post-processing is defined to be the process where the results of a classification 
problem can further be evaluated and visualized to further the understanding 
of a given data set (Bruha & Famili, 2000). The post-processing of tweets was 
done in two parts of the study: (1) During evaluation of the two Bayesian 
methods and (2) during the visualization of the common n-grams per tweet 
category. 
 
The results proved that tweet relevance, or its informativeness, in the context 
of a Typhoon’s landfall, can be identified by utilizing the Bayesian variant, 
Multinomial Naïve Bayes. Among the two variants used, Multinomial Naïve 
Bayes performed exceptionally better than Bernoulli Naïve Bayes. However, 
not all tweet features that were gathered was used in the process, because of 
the nature of the Bayesian classifiers. These classifiers only considered text 
data and its respective class-labels. 
 
6.2 Recommendations 
The following recommendations are suggested based on the drawn 
conclusions: 
1. Consider other tweet features, not only tweet text data in tweet 
classification problems. 
2. Consider the presence of emojis, as these would also prove to be a basis for 
text sentiments on online platforms. 
3. One of the struggles of dealing with classification problems is dealing with 
imbalanced data. The class count of each informative subclass was very 
imbalanced. Future researchers may consider data over sampling, under 
sampling, or gathering more data to address this concern.
